Day 36 showed how to project a vector onto a subspace, but its formula only works when the basis of that subspace is orthogonal. The bases you are handed, such as the columns of a matrix, almost never are, so today asks how to repair one. Starting from any basis, the **Gram–Schmidt process** builds an orthogonal basis for the same subspace by projecting each new vector onto the ones already built and keeping only the part that sticks out.

## Subtracting the shadow

The goal is a new basis with the same span in which every pair of vectors meets at a right angle, built one vector at a time. Keep $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = \mathbf x_1$. The **shadow** of $\blue{\mathbf x_2}$ on the line through $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$ is its projection $\frac{\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1}\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$. That shadow is the part of $\blue{\mathbf x_2}$ that leans along $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$, so removing it leaves a vector at a right angle to $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$:

$$
\blue{\mathbf v_2} = \mathbf x_2 - \frac{\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1}\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1}.
$$
</Pair>

## The Gram–Schmidt process

For a third vector you subtract its shadow on every vector built so far. Because $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$ and $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ are already orthogonal, the shadow on their plane is just the sum of the two separate shadows, which is Day 36's projection formula.

<Definition term="Theorem (the Gram–Schmidt process)">
Let $\{\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_p\}$ be a basis for a nonzero subspace $W$ of $\mathbb R^n$. Set $\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf x_1$ and, for $k = 2, \dots, p$,

$$
\mathbf v_k = \mathbf x_k - \frac{\mathbf x_k\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1}\,\mathbf v_1 - \cdots - \frac{\mathbf x_k\cdot\mathbf v_{k-1}}{\mathbf v_{k-1}\cdot\mathbf v_{k-1}}\,\mathbf v_{k-1}.
$$

Then $\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$, and $\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_k\} = \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_k\}$ for every $k$.
</Definition>

Each $\mathbf v_k$ is $\mathbf x_k$ minus its projection onto $W_{k-1} = \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_{k-1}\}$, so the orthogonal decomposition theorem makes it perpendicular to every earlier $\mathbf v$. It still lies in $W$ because it is $\mathbf x_k$ minus a combination of earlier vectors. It is never zero, because $\mathbf x_k$ is not in $W_{k-1}$ when the $\mathbf x$'s are independent. Always project onto the new $\mathbf v$'s, never onto the old $\mathbf x$'s, because the shadows only add up correctly when the vectors you project onto are orthogonal.

Only the part of $\blue{\mathbf x_2}$ that sticks out from the line survives. Find a starting vector that straightens to the ring without already being there.

Here is the video's example with $\mathbf x_1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $\mathbf x_2 = (1, 0, 1)$ and $\mathbf x_3 = (0, 1, 1)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\yellow{\mathbf v_1} &= \mathbf x_1 = (1, 1, 0) \\
\blue{\mathbf v_2} &= \mathbf x_2 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (1, 0, 1) - (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0) = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1) \\
\pink{\mathbf v_3} &= \mathbf x_3 - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1} - \frac{1/2}{3/2}\,\blue{\mathbf v_2} \\
&= (0, 1, 1) - (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0) - (\tfrac16, -\tfrac16, \tfrac13) = (-\tfrac23, \tfrac23, \tfrac23)
\end{aligned}
$$

Every pair checks out: $\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + 0 = 0$, $\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_3 = -\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 = 0$ and $\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf v_3 = -\tfrac13 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac23 = 0$.

You may rescale any $\mathbf v_k$ to clear fractions before the next step, for example $\mathbf v_2' = 2\mathbf v_2 = (1, -1, 2)$. The projection does not change, because the factor 2 appears twice on top (in $\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf v_2'$ and in $\mathbf v_2'$) and twice on the bottom (in $\mathbf v_2'\cdot\mathbf v_2'$), so it cancels: $\frac{\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf v_2'}{\mathbf v_2'\cdot\mathbf v_2'}\,\mathbf v_2' = \tfrac16(1, -1, 2)$, which equals $\tfrac13\mathbf v_2$ as before.

## Normalizing

A set is **orthonormal** when its vectors are orthogonal and each has length 1. Unit vectors make every denominator $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u$ in Day 36's formula equal to 1, so each projection weight becomes a single dot product. Dividing each $\mathbf v_k$ by its length keeps every right angle and turns an orthogonal basis into an orthonormal one:

$$
\mathbf u_k = \frac{\mathbf v_k}{\lVert\mathbf v_k\rVert}
$$

For the video's example this gives

$$
\yellow{\mathbf u_1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0), \quad \blue{\mathbf u_2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, -1, 2), \quad \pink{\mathbf u_3} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(-1, 1, 1).
$$

Normalize last. If you normalize along the way, every later projection carries square roots.

## QR factorization

The goal of this section is to record all of Gram–Schmidt's work in two matrices you can reuse. $Q$ holds the orthonormal basis, and $R$ holds the weights that rebuild the original vectors from it. Put the $\mathbf x$'s into the columns of $A$ and the $\mathbf u$'s into the columns of $Q$. The span property says $\mathbf x_k$ is a combination of $\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_k$ only, so the weights that rebuild $\mathbf x_k$ have zeros below position $k$. Those weight columns form an upper triangular $R$ with $A = QR$.

<Pair>
<Figure src="qr-weights" alt="Three equations: x1 = r11 u1, x2 = r12 u1 + r22 u2, x3 = r13 u1 + r23 u2 + r33 u3. Beside them, R is a 3 by 3 matrix with the r entries on and above the diagonal and orange zeros below it." />

<Definition term="Theorem (QR factorization)">
If the $m \times n$ matrix $A$ has linearly independent columns, then $A = QR$, where the columns of the $m \times n$ matrix $Q$ are an orthonormal basis for $\operatorname{Col} A$ and $R$ is $n \times n$, upper triangular and invertible, with positive diagonal entries.
</Definition>
</Pair>

You never need to track the weights by hand. Orthonormal columns give $Q^TQ = I$, so $Q^TA = Q^TQR = R$. For the video's example,

$$
R = Q^TA = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt2 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \\ \glow{0} & \tfrac{3}{\sqrt6} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6} \\ \glow{0} & \glow{0} & \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The diagonal holds the lengths $\lVert\mathbf v_1\rVert = \sqrt2$, $\lVert\mathbf v_2\rVert = \sqrt{3/2}$ and $\lVert\mathbf v_3\rVert = \sqrt{4/3}$. In the widget below, $\yellow{\mathbf u_1}$ and $\blue{\mathbf u_2}$ are already orthonormal, and you choose the weights that rebuild each column of $A$.

On a computer, rounding errors pile up in the plain process, so software uses a reordered version called modified Gram–Schmidt, or builds $Q$ from reflections instead.
