Day 36 showed how to project a vector onto a subspace, but its formula only works when the basis of that subspace is orthogonal. The bases you are handed, such as the columns of a matrix, almost never are, so today asks how to repair one. Starting from any basis, the **Gram–Schmidt process** builds an orthogonal basis for the same subspace by projecting each new vector onto the ones already built and keeping only the part that sticks out.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The box after each walkthrough names the idea you just used.

## Subtracting the shadow

<Problem>
The vectors $\yellow{\mathbf x_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\blue{\mathbf x_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ are a basis of $\mathbb R^2$, but they are not perpendicular. Keep $\mathbf x_1$ as it is. Find the number $c$ that makes $\mathbf x_2 - c\,\mathbf x_1$ perpendicular to $\mathbf x_1$, and write down that new vector.

<Hint>
Perpendicular means the dot product is zero. Write $(\mathbf x_2 - c\,\mathbf x_1)\cdot\mathbf x_1$ in terms of $c$ and set it to zero.
</Hint>

<Solution>
We want to change $\mathbf x_2$ as little as possible so that it meets $\mathbf x_1$ at a right angle. Sliding it back along $\mathbf x_1$ is the natural move, because that keeps it inside the same span, and the dot product tells us how far to slide.

<Steps>
<Step>
Write the dot product in terms of $c$:

$$
(\mathbf x_2 - c\,\mathbf x_1)\cdot\mathbf x_1 = \mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_1 - c\,(\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_1) = (3 + 3) - c\,(9 + 1) = 6 - 10c.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Set it to zero: $6 - 10c = 0$ gives $c = \tfrac{6}{10} = \tfrac35$.
</Step>
<Step>
Subtract: $\mathbf x_2 - \tfrac35\,\mathbf x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - \tfrac95 \\ 3 - \tfrac35 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -\tfrac45 \\ \tfrac{12}{5} \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Check: $3\cdot(-\tfrac45) + 1\cdot\tfrac{12}{5} = -\tfrac{12}{5} + \tfrac{12}{5} = 0$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The number $c = \frac{\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf x_1}{\mathbf x_1\cdot\mathbf x_1}$ is Day 36's projection weight, so $c\,\mathbf x_1$ is the **shadow** of $\mathbf x_2$ on the line through $\mathbf x_1$. Subtracting the shadow removes the part of $\mathbf x_2$ that leans along $\mathbf x_1$, and what is left stands at a right angle to it.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<ShadowSubtractSlider v1={[3, 1]} x2={[1, 3]} />

<Concept title="One step of Gram–Schmidt">
Keep the first vector, $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = \mathbf x_1$. Then replace $\mathbf x_2$ by what is left after subtracting its shadow on $\mathbf v_1$:

$$
\blue{\mathbf v_2} = \mathbf x_2 - \frac{\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1}\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1}.
$$

By the orthogonal decomposition theorem from Day 36, $\mathbf v_2$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf v_1$.
</Concept>

The new pair must still describe the same subspace, or we would have repaired the wrong thing. The next problem checks that in $\mathbb R^3$.

<Problem>
Let $\mathbf x_1 = (1, 1, 0)$ and $\mathbf x_2 = (1, 0, 1)$, which span a plane $W$ in $\mathbb R^3$. Compute $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$. Then show that $\mathbf x_2$ can be rebuilt from $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$, so the new pair spans the same plane.

<Solution>
Follow the one-step recipe, then undo it.

<Steps>
<Step>
Keep $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = \mathbf x_1 = (1, 1, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
The shadow weight is $\frac{\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1} = \frac{1 + 0 + 0}{1 + 1 + 0} = \frac12$, so

$$
\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (1, 0, 1) - \tfrac12(1, 1, 0) = \left(\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12,\ 1\right).
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Check the right angle: $\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + 0 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step>
Rearrange the formula for $\mathbf v_2$: $\mathbf x_2 = \mathbf v_2 + \tfrac12\,\mathbf v_1$. So $\mathbf x_2$ is a combination of $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$, and $\mathbf v_2$ is a combination of $\mathbf x_1$ and $\mathbf x_2$. Each pair can build the other, so they span the same plane.
</Step>
</Steps>

The vector $\mathbf v_2$ is not zero, because $\mathbf x_2$ is not a multiple of $\mathbf x_1$. If it were, all of $\mathbf x_2$ would be shadow and nothing would be left.
</Solution>
</Problem>

Only the part of $\blue{\mathbf x_2}$ that sticks out from the line survives. That means many different starting vectors straighten to the same $\mathbf v_2$. Find one that is not already at the ring.

<Warning>
If a step of Gram–Schmidt produces $\mathbf v_k = \mathbf 0$, then $\mathbf x_k$ was already in the span of the earlier vectors, so the vectors you started with were not a basis. Drop that vector and say so. Do not divide by $\mathbf 0\cdot\mathbf 0$ in the next step.
</Warning>

## The Gram–Schmidt process

With a third vector there are two earlier directions, so there are two shadows to remove.

<Problem>
Continue Problem 2 with $\mathbf x_3 = (0, 1, 1)$. You already have $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (1, 1, 0)$ and $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)$. Find $\pink{\mathbf v_3}$, perpendicular to both.

<Solution>
We want the part of $\mathbf x_3$ that sticks out of the plane spanned by $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$. That is $\mathbf x_3$ minus its projection onto the plane. Because $\mathbf v_1$ and $\mathbf v_2$ are already orthogonal, Day 36's formula lets us compute that projection as the sum of the two separate shadows.

<Steps>
<Step>
Shadow on $\mathbf v_1$: $\frac{\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1} = \frac{0 + 1 + 0}{2} = \frac12$, so the shadow is $\tfrac12(1, 1, 0) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Shadow on $\mathbf v_2$: $\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf v_2 = 0 - \tfrac12 + 1 = \tfrac12$ and $\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf v_2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + 1 = \tfrac32$. The weight is $\frac{1/2}{3/2} = \frac13$, so the shadow is $\tfrac13(\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1) = (\tfrac16, -\tfrac16, \tfrac13)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Subtract both shadows:

$$
\pink{\mathbf v_3} = (0, 1, 1) - \left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0\right) - \left(\tfrac16, -\tfrac16, \tfrac13\right) = \left(-\tfrac23,\ \tfrac23,\ \tfrac23\right).
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Check every pair: $\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_3 = -\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 + 0 = 0$ and $\mathbf v_2\cdot\mathbf v_3 = -\tfrac13 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac23 = 0$. Together with $\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_2 = 0$ from Problem 2, all three pairs are perpendicular.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="third-step" alt="Oblique 3D view. A teal sheet is the plane spanned by yellow v1 and blue v2. The faded pink arrow x3 = (0, 1, 1) drops a dashed line onto the sheet, where two purple-gray bars along v1 and v2 and dashed edges meet. Solid pink v3 rises from the origin, with orange right-angle corners between it and both v1 and v2." wide />

<Definition term="Theorem (the Gram–Schmidt process)">
Let $\{\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_p\}$ be a basis for a nonzero subspace $W$ of $\mathbb R^n$. Set $\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf x_1$ and, for $k = 2, \dots, p$,

$$
\mathbf v_k = \mathbf x_k - \frac{\mathbf x_k\cdot\mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1\cdot\mathbf v_1}\,\mathbf v_1 - \cdots - \frac{\mathbf x_k\cdot\mathbf v_{k-1}}{\mathbf v_{k-1}\cdot\mathbf v_{k-1}}\,\mathbf v_{k-1}.
$$

Then $\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$, and $\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_k\} = \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf x_1, \dots, \mathbf x_k\}$ for every $k$.
</Definition>

Each $\mathbf v_k$ is $\mathbf x_k$ minus its projection onto $W_{k-1} = \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_{k-1}\}$, so the orthogonal decomposition theorem makes it perpendicular to every earlier $\mathbf v$. It still lies in $W$ because it is $\mathbf x_k$ minus a combination of earlier vectors. It is never zero, because $\mathbf x_k$ is not in $W_{k-1}$ when the $\mathbf x$'s are independent.

<Warning>
Always project onto the new $\mathbf v$'s, never onto the old $\mathbf x$'s. The shadows only add up to the projection when the vectors you project onto are orthogonal. In Problem 3, using $\mathbf x_2 = (1, 0, 1)$ in place of $\mathbf v_2$ gives $(0, 1, 1) - (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0) - \tfrac12(1, 0, 1) = (-1, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, and its dot product with $\mathbf v_1$ is $-1 + \tfrac12 = -\tfrac12$, not $0$.
</Warning>

Fractions pile up quickly, and you are allowed to clear them.

<Problem>
Replace $\mathbf v_2 = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)$ by $\mathbf v_2' = 2\mathbf v_2 = (1, -1, 2)$ and redo the shadow of $\mathbf x_3 = (0, 1, 1)$ on it. Does $\mathbf v_3$ change?

<Solution>
The dot products are $\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf v_2' = 0 - 1 + 2 = 1$ and $\mathbf v_2'\cdot\mathbf v_2' = 1 + 1 + 4 = 6$, so the shadow is $\tfrac16(1, -1, 2) = (\tfrac16, -\tfrac16, \tfrac13)$. That is the same shadow as in Problem 3, so $\mathbf v_3$ does not change.

The factor 2 appears twice on top (in $\mathbf x_3\cdot\mathbf v_2'$ and in $\mathbf v_2'$ itself) and twice on the bottom (in $\mathbf v_2'\cdot\mathbf v_2'$), so it cancels. Any nonzero rescaling of an intermediate $\mathbf v_k$ is safe. The same argument lets us replace $\mathbf v_3$ by $\mathbf v_3' = -\tfrac32\mathbf v_3 = (1, -1, -1)$, or by $(-1, 1, 1)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Choice
  question="You run Gram–Schmidt on $\mathbf x_2$ first and then $\mathbf x_1$, the reverse of the usual order. What do you get?"
  options={["The same orthogonal basis", "A different orthogonal basis of the same subspace", "Something that is no longer a basis"]}
  answer={1}
>
The first vector you keep is whichever one comes first, so the output changes with the order. It is still an orthogonal basis of the same subspace, so both answers are correct. When you check someone's work, test that the vectors are perpendicular and lie in the subspace rather than matching them against one particular answer.
</Choice>

## Normalizing

An **orthonormal** set is an orthogonal set in which every vector has length 1. Unit vectors make every denominator $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u$ in Day 36's formula equal to 1, so each projection weight becomes a single dot product.

<Problem>
Turn the orthogonal basis $\mathbf v_1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $\mathbf v_2' = (1, -1, 2)$, $\mathbf v_3' = (-1, 1, 1)$ from Problems 3 and 4 into an orthonormal one.

<Solution>
Dividing a vector by its length keeps its direction and makes its length 1. Directions are what make the vectors perpendicular, so every right angle survives.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf v_1\| &= \sqrt{1 + 1 + 0} = \sqrt2, & \yellow{\mathbf u_1} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0) \\
\|\mathbf v_2'\| &= \sqrt{1 + 1 + 4} = \sqrt6, & \blue{\mathbf u_2} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1, -1, 2) \\
\|\mathbf v_3'\| &= \sqrt{1 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt3, & \pink{\mathbf u_3} &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(-1, 1, 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

Rescaling to whole numbers first made these lengths easy to compute.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Normalize at the end">
To turn an orthogonal basis into an orthonormal one, divide each vector by its length:

$$
\mathbf u_k = \frac{\mathbf v_k}{\lVert\mathbf v_k\rVert}.
$$

Do this after the whole Gram–Schmidt run. If you normalize along the way, every later projection carries square roots.
</Concept>

## QR factorization

The goal of this section is to record all of Gram–Schmidt's work in two matrices you can reuse. $Q$ holds the orthonormal basis, and $R$ holds the weights that rebuild the original vectors from it.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ with columns $\mathbf x_1 = (3, 4)$ and $\mathbf x_2 = (-1, 7)$. Build an orthonormal basis $\mathbf u_1, \mathbf u_2$ from the columns. Then find the weights that rebuild each column from $\mathbf u_1$ and $\mathbf u_2$.

<Solution>
Run Gram–Schmidt and normalize, then read the weights off with dot products. Because the $\mathbf u$'s are orthonormal, the weight of $\mathbf u_j$ in any vector is just that vector dotted with $\mathbf u_j$.

<Steps>
<Step>
$\|\mathbf x_1\| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, so $\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac15(3, 4) = (0.6, 0.8)$.
</Step>
<Step>
The shadow of $\mathbf x_2$ on $\mathbf u_1$ has weight $\mathbf x_2\cdot\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac15(-3 + 28) = 5$. Subtracting it leaves $\mathbf x_2 - 5\,\mathbf u_1 = (-1 - 3,\ 7 - 4) = (-4, 3)$.
</Step>
<Step>
$\|(-4, 3)\| = 5$, so $\mathbf u_2 = (-0.8, 0.6)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Rebuild the columns. $\mathbf x_1 = 5\,\mathbf u_1 + 0\,\mathbf u_2$ and $\mathbf x_2 = 5\,\mathbf u_1 + 5\,\mathbf u_2$. Put these weights in columns:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 5 \\ \glow{0} & 5 \end{bmatrix} = QR.
$$
</Step>
</Steps>

The zero below the diagonal appears because $\mathbf x_1$ lies along $\mathbf u_1$ and needs none of $\mathbf u_2$. Multiply out to check: the first column of $QR$ is $5(0.6, 0.8) = (3, 4)$ and the second is $5(0.6, 0.8) + 5(-0.8, 0.6) = (-1, 7)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="qr-weights" alt="Three equations: x1 = r11 u1, x2 = r12 u1 + r22 u2, x3 = r13 u1 + r23 u2 + r33 u3. Beside them, R is a 3 by 3 matrix with the r entries on and above the diagonal and orange zeros below it." />

<Definition term="Theorem (QR factorization)">
If the $m \times n$ matrix $A$ has linearly independent columns, then $A = QR$, where the columns of the $m \times n$ matrix $Q$ are an orthonormal basis for $\operatorname{Col} A$ and $R$ is $n \times n$, upper triangular and invertible, with positive diagonal entries.
</Definition>
</Pair>

The span property says $\mathbf x_k$ is a combination of $\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_k$ only, so the weights that rebuild $\mathbf x_k$ have zeros below position $k$. That is why $R$ is upper triangular.

You never need to track the weights by hand. Orthonormal columns give $Q^TQ = I$, so $Q^TA = Q^TQR = R$. In Problem 6, $Q^TA$ has entries $\mathbf u_i\cdot\mathbf x_j$, which are exactly the dot products we computed. For the three vectors of Problem 3,

$$
R = Q^TA = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt2 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} \\[6pt] \glow{0} & \tfrac{3}{\sqrt6} & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt6} \\[6pt] \glow{0} & \glow{0} & \tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The diagonal holds the lengths $\lVert\mathbf v_1\rVert = \sqrt2$, $\lVert\mathbf v_2\rVert = \sqrt{3/2}$ and $\lVert\mathbf v_3\rVert = \sqrt{4/3}$ of the unscaled Gram–Schmidt vectors. In the widget below, $\yellow{\mathbf u_1}$ and $\blue{\mathbf u_2}$ are already orthonormal, and you choose the weights that rebuild each column of $A$.

<QrWeightSliders u1={[0.8, 0.6]} u2={[-0.6, 0.8]} x1={[4, 3]} x2={[2.5, 5]} />

<Warning>
The Gram–Schmidt weights $\frac{\mathbf x_k\cdot\mathbf v_j}{\mathbf v_j\cdot\mathbf v_j}$ are not the entries of $R$, because they were computed with unnormalized vectors. Use $R = Q^TA$. If a diagonal entry of $R$ comes out negative, flip the sign of that column of $Q$ and that row of $R$.
</Warning>

On a computer, rounding errors pile up in the plain process, so software uses a reordered version called modified Gram–Schmidt, or builds $Q$ from reflections instead.

<Check>
Suppose $A = QR$, where $Q$ is $m \times n$ and $R$ is $n \times n$, and the columns of $A$ are linearly independent. Show that $R$ must be invertible. (Modeled on Lay §6.4, Exercise 19.)

<Hint>
Suppose $R\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ and multiply both sides by $Q$ on the left.
</Hint>

<Answer>
If $R\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$, then $A\mathbf x = Q(R\mathbf x) = Q\mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0$. The columns of $A$ are independent, so the only solution of $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ is $\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$. So $R\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ has only the trivial solution, and the square matrix $R$ is invertible by the Invertible Matrix Theorem (Day 19).
</Answer>
