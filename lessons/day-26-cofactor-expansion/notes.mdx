On Day 25 the determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix was the factor by which it scales area, and the picture gave the formula $ad - bc$. That formula stops at $2 \times 2$, but the uses ahead need larger matrices. Day 29 finds the eigenvalues of $3 \times 3$ matrices by solving $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, and the test "$\det A \neq 0$ means invertible" only helps if you can compute $\det A$. Today builds every larger determinant out of $2 \times 2$ pieces, then finds a faster route through row reduction.

## Minors and cofactors

A $3 \times 3$ matrix hides nine $2 \times 2$ matrices inside it, one for each entry. We start by pulling one out.

<Problem>
Take the matrix from the video,

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Cross out row 2 and column 3. Four entries are left. Write them as a $2 \times 2$ matrix, keeping their positions, and find its determinant.

<Solution>
Row 2 is $(1, 0, 1)$ and column 3 is $(1, 1, -1)$ reading down. Crossing both out leaves the entries in rows 1 and 3 and columns 1 and 2:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & \cancel{1} \\ \cancel{1} & \cancel{0} & \cancel{1} \\ 0 & -2 & \cancel{-1} \end{bmatrix}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The entries keep their relative places, so row 1 of the small matrix is $(2, 2)$ and row 2 is $(0, -2)$. Day 25's formula gives

$$
\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{vmatrix} = 2 \cdot (-2) - 2 \cdot 0 = -4.
$$

The vertical bars are a common shorthand for "the determinant of this matrix."
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Minors and cofactors">
Deleting row $i$ and column $j$ of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ leaves an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ **submatrix**, written $A_{ij}$. In the problem, $A_{23} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$. Its determinant, $\det A_{23} = -4$, is called a **minor**.

The **cofactor** attaches a sign to the minor. The sign is $+$ or $-$ according to a checkerboard that starts with $+$ in the top-left corner, and position $(2, 3)$ lands on a $-$. So

$$
C_{23} = -\det A_{23} = -(-4) = 4.
$$

In symbols, the checkerboard sign is $(-1)^{i+j}$, and $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}$. For $(2, 3)$, $(-1)^{5} = -1$.
</Concept>

<Figure src="checkerboard" alt="The matrix A with rows (2, 2, 1), (1, 0, 1) and (0, −2, −1). Row 2 and column 3 are dimmed, and a small plus or minus sign sits at the upper left of every entry in a checkerboard pattern, with the minus sign at position (2, 3) in orange. Beside it, A23 is the 2 by 2 matrix with rows (2, 2) and (0, −2), det A23 = 2·(−2) − 2·0 = −4, and C23 = (−1) to the power 2 + 3 times det A23 = 4." wide />

Pick any entry to see its minor light up in blue and its cofactor computed. One position has cofactor $-1$. Find it.

<CofactorHunt matrix={[[2, 2, 1], [1, 0, 1], [0, -2, -1]]} target={-1} />

<Warning>
The sign of a cofactor comes from the **position**, never from the entry. Position $(3, 2)$ always carries $-$, whether the entry sitting there is $5$, $0$ or $-2$. When the entry is itself negative, the two minus signs are separate and both count.
</Warning>

<Choice
  question="In a $4 \times 4$ matrix, which sign does the checkerboard put at position $(4, 2)$?"
  options={["$+$", "$-$"]}
  answer={0}
>
$(-1)^{4+2} = (-1)^6 = +1$. You can also walk the checkerboard: row 4 starts with $-$ because row 1 starts with $+$ and each new row flips, so row 4 reads $-, +, -, +$, and column 2 is $+$.
</Choice>

## Cofactor expansion

Cofactors are useful because a determinant can be rebuilt from them. We test that on a $2 \times 2$ matrix first, where we already know the answer.

<Problem>
Let $M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ from Day 25, whose determinant is $3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5$. Find the cofactors $C_{11}$ and $C_{12}$. Then compute $3\,C_{11} + 1\,C_{12}$, which multiplies each entry of row 1 by its own cofactor.

<Solution>
For $C_{11}$, delete row 1 and column 1. The only entry left is $2$, and a $1 \times 1$ matrix has that entry as its determinant. Position $(1, 1)$ is $+$, so $C_{11} = 2$.

For $C_{12}$, delete row 1 and column 2. The entry left is $1$, and position $(1, 2)$ is $-$, so $C_{12} = -1$.

Now weight each cofactor by its entry:

$$
3\,C_{11} + 1\,C_{12} = 3 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot (-1) = 5.
$$

That is exactly $\det M$. Written with letters, $a\,C_{11} + b\,C_{12} = a\,d + b\,(-c) = ad - bc$, so the $2 \times 2$ formula is already a sum of entries times cofactors.
</Solution>
</Problem>

The $2 \times 2$ case suggests a recipe for bigger matrices: go along a row, and multiply each entry by its cofactor. The goal is to write one $3 \times 3$ determinant as a sum of $2 \times 2$ determinants, which we know how to compute.

<Problem>
Use the recipe on $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ along row 1.

<Solution>
Row 1 holds the entries $2, 2, 1$, and the checkerboard signs above them are $+, -, +$. Take them one at a time.

<Steps>
<Step>
Entry $a_{11} = 2$. Delete row 1 and column 1, which leaves $\begin{vmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 0 \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot (-2) = 2$. The sign is $+$, so this term is $+2 \cdot 2 = 4$.
</Step>
<Step>
Entry $a_{12} = 2$. Delete row 1 and column 2, which leaves $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 1 \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot 0 = -1$. The sign is $-$, so this term is $-2 \cdot (-1) = 2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Entry $a_{13} = 1$. Delete row 1 and column 3, which leaves $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{vmatrix} = 1 \cdot (-2) - 0 \cdot 0 = -2$. The sign is $+$, so this term is $+1 \cdot (-2) = -2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Add the three terms: $\det A = 4 + 2 - 2 = 4$.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Figure src="expansion" alt="The row 1 expansion of A written out. Row 1 of A is yellow and the signs plus, minus, plus above it are orange. Three lines show +2 times the determinant with rows (0, 1) and (−2, −1), which is +2(2) = 4, then −2 times the determinant with rows (1, 1) and (0, −1), which is −2(−1) = +2, then +1 times the determinant with rows (1, 0) and (0, −2), which is +1(−2) = −2. The sum reads det A = 4 + 2 − 2 = 4." />
</Solution>
</Problem>

Nothing in the recipe said we had to use row 1. The next problem tries a column instead.

<Problem>
Compute $\det A$ for the same matrix by going down column 2, whose entries are $2, 0, -2$. Do you get $4$ again?

<Solution>
Column 2 has checkerboard signs $-, +, -$ from top to bottom.

<Steps>
<Step>
Entry $a_{12} = 2$ with sign $-$. Its minor is $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = -1$, so the term is $-2 \cdot (-1) = 2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Entry $a_{22} = 0$ with sign $+$. A zero entry makes the whole term zero, so there is no need to compute its minor.
</Step>
<Step>
Entry $a_{32} = -2$ with sign $-$. Its minor is $\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 2 - 1 = 1$, so the term is $-(-2) \cdot 1 = 2$. The position's minus and the entry's minus cancel.
</Step>
<Step>
Add: $\det A = 2 + 0 + 2 = 4$, the same as before, with one fewer minor to compute.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Cofactor expansion (Lay, Theorem 1)">
The determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ can be computed along any row $i$,

$$
\det A = a_{i1}C_{i1} + a_{i2}C_{i2} + \cdots + a_{in}C_{in},
$$

or down any column $j$,

$$
\det A = a_{1j}C_{1j} + a_{2j}C_{2j} + \cdots + a_{nj}C_{nj}.
$$
</Definition>

Every line gives the same number. If you unwind any expansion completely, you get the same signed sum of products, each taking exactly one entry from every row and every column. The checkerboard signs are what keep those signs consistent from one line to the next.

Because a zero entry kills its whole term, the best line to expand along is the one with the most zeros. Try every line of this matrix and find the cheapest.

<CofactorLinePicker matrix={[[3, 1, -2], [4, 0, 5], [1, 0, 2]]} />

<Warning>
Each term is the entry times its cofactor, $a_{ij}C_{ij}$. A common slip is to add up the cofactors alone and forget the entry in front. Another is to write $-2$ instead of $+2$ for a term like the last one above, by using only one of the two minus signs.
</Warning>

## Zeros and triangular matrices

A $4 \times 4$ expansion has four $3 \times 3$ minors, and each of those has three $2 \times 2$ minors. Zeros are what keep that from getting out of hand.

<Problem>
Compute $\det B$ for

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

<Solution>
Look for the line with the most zeros. Row 2 is $(0, 2, 0, 0)$, so expanding along it leaves one term.

<Steps>
<Step>
The only nonzero entry of row 2 is $2$ at position $(2, 2)$, whose sign is $+$. Deleting row 2 and column 2 leaves a $3 \times 3$ minor:

$$
\det B = +2\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Look for zeros again. Column 2 of the minor is $(0, 3, 0)$, and its only nonzero entry sits at position $(2, 2)$ with sign $+$:

$$
\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 4 & 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = +3\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 3 \cdot (1 \cdot 2 - 2 \cdot 0) = 6.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Put the pieces together: $\det B = 2 \cdot 6 = 12$. Choosing well meant computing one $2 \times 2$ determinant instead of twelve.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

The same trick makes one kind of matrix almost free.

<Problem>
Compute $\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix}$.

<Solution>
Column 1 is $(3, 0, 0)$, so expand down it. Only the $3$ survives, with sign $+$:

$$
\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 2 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix}
= 3\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 0 & -1 \end{vmatrix}
= 3 \cdot \big(2 \cdot (-1) - 5 \cdot 0\big)
= 3 \cdot 2 \cdot (-1) = -6.
$$

The minor was triangular again, so its determinant was just $2 \cdot (-1)$. The answer is the product of the diagonal entries, and every number above the diagonal dropped out.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Triangular matrices">
If every entry below the diagonal is zero, expanding down column 1 keeps only the top-left entry, and its minor is again triangular. Repeating that peels off one diagonal entry at a time. The same happens with every entry above the diagonal zero, using row 1. So the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries (Lay, Theorem 2):

$$
\begin{vmatrix} \yellow{3} & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & \yellow{2} & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & \yellow{-1} \end{vmatrix} = \yellow{3} \cdot \yellow{2} \cdot (\yellow{-1}) = -6.
$$
</Concept>

## Row operations

A full cofactor expansion of an $n \times n$ matrix takes roughly $n!$ multiplications, which is already millions for $n = 10$. Row reduction is much cheaper, and it ends at a triangular matrix, whose determinant we can read off. The question is what each row operation does to the determinant along the way.

<Problem>
Start with $M = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, where $\det M = 4 - 1 = 3$. Apply these three row operations one after another and compute the determinant after each: first $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$, then swap the two rows, then $R_1 \leftarrow 2R_1$.

<Solution>
Do each operation on the current matrix and use $ad - bc$ each time.

<Steps>
<Step>
$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1$ turns row 2 into $(1 - 2, 2 - 1) = (-1, 1)$. The matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and its determinant is $2 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot (-1) = 3$. The replacement changed nothing.
</Step>
<Step>
Swapping the rows gives $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, with determinant $(-1) \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot 2 = -3$. The swap flipped the sign.
</Step>
<Step>
$R_1 \leftarrow 2R_1$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, with determinant $(-2) \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 2 = -6$. Doubling one row doubled the determinant.
</Step>
</Steps>

The picture from Day 25 explains all three. The columns of $M$ build a parallelogram. A replacement shears it, which keeps the area. A swap reflects it, which reverses its orientation. Scaling a row stretches the plane in one direction, which scales the area by the same factor.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Row operations and the determinant (Lay, Theorem 3)">
Let $A$ be a square matrix, and let $B$ come from $A$ by one row operation.

- If a multiple of one row is added to another row, then $\det B = \det A$.
- If two rows are interchanged, then $\det B = -\det A$.
- If one row is multiplied by $k$, then $\det B = k \det A$.
</Definition>

<Figure src="row-operations" alt="Four small planes in a row. The first shows the parallelogram of the columns of M = (2, 1; 1, 2) in yellow with det = 3. After R2 ← R2 − R1 the parallelogram is sheared below the axis, still yellow, with det = 3. After R1 ↔ R2 it is flipped over and pink, with det = −3. After R1 ← 2R1 it is stretched sideways and pink, with det = −6." wide />

Drag the green tip to add a multiple of row 1 to row 2. The parallelogram shears, and its area stays the same the whole time.

<ReplacementShear matrix={[[1, -1], [1, 2]]} />

So the plan for a big matrix is to row reduce to a triangular matrix using replacements and swaps, multiply the diagonal, and flip the sign once for every swap.

<Problem>
Compute $\det C$ by row reduction, where $C = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
We want zeros below the diagonal. The top-left entry is $0$, so it cannot clear anything beneath it, and the first move has to bring a nonzero entry up.

<Steps>
<Step>
Swap rows 1 and 2. A swap flips the sign, so a minus sign goes in front:

$$
\det C = -\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Clear the $2$ in row 3 with $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 2R_1$. Row 3 becomes $(2 - 2, 3 - 4, 4 - 2) = (0, -1, 2)$. A replacement changes nothing:

$$
\det C = -\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Clear the $-1$ in row 3 with $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + R_2$. Row 3 becomes $(0, 0, 4)$, and again nothing changes:

$$
\det C = -\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
The matrix is triangular, so multiply the diagonal: $\det C = -(1 \cdot 1 \cdot 4) = -4$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

You do not have to reduce all the way. A few replacements can clear one column down to a single entry, and then one cofactor expansion finishes the job. Try it here.

<ColumnClearExpand matrix={[[1, 2, -1], [3, 5, 2], [-2, 1, 4]]} />

<Warning>
Only $R_i \leftarrow R_i + c\,R_j$ is free. The operation $R_i \leftarrow c\,R_i + R_j$ first scales row $i$ by $c$, so it multiplies the determinant by $c$. In the same way, if you divide a row by $2$ to make a pivot equal $1$, the new determinant is half the old one, and you have to multiply back by $2$ at the end.
</Warning>

Row reduction also settles invertibility. A triangular matrix has nonzero determinant exactly when none of its diagonal entries is zero, which is when every column has a pivot. Row operations never turn a nonzero determinant into zero, because they only multiply it by $1$, $-1$ or a nonzero $k$. So a square matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$ (Lay, Theorem 4). Day 29 applies this test to $A - \lambda I$ to find eigenvalues.

<Choice
  question="Is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 6 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ invertible?"
  options={["Yes", "No"]}
  answer={1}
>
Column 2 is twice column 1, so the columns are linearly dependent and $\det = 0$. You can see it by row reduction too: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 3R_1$ leave rows $(0, 0, -5)$ and $(0, 0, -5)$, and two equal rows reduce to a zero row.
</Choice>

## Transposes and products

Two more rules let you skip the arithmetic entirely. The rows of $A^T$ are the columns of $A$, so expanding $A^T$ along row 1 is the same computation as expanding $A$ down column 1. That gives $\det A^T = \det A$, and it means every rule above for rows holds for columns too. Products have a rule of their own, which the next problem uncovers.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $\det A$, $\det B$, $\det(AB)$ and $\det(A + B)$. Which of the last two can you predict from the first two?

<Solution>
First the two separate determinants: $\det A = 2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5$ and $\det B = 1 \cdot 1 - (-1) \cdot 2 = 3$.

Next the product. Row 1 of $A$ times the columns of $B$ gives $(2 + 2, -2 + 1) = (4, -1)$, and row 2 gives $(1 + 6, -1 + 3) = (7, 2)$. So

$$
AB = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ 7 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det(AB) = 4 \cdot 2 - (-1) \cdot 7 = 15 = 5 \cdot 3.
$$

Finally the sum, $A + B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, whose determinant is $12$. That is not $5 + 3 = 8$.

The product matches $(\det A)(\det B)$ because $B$ scales every area by $3$ and then $A$ scales the result by $5$. Adding matrices has no such picture, and no rule predicts $\det(A + B)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Transposes and products">
For square matrices of the same size,

$$
\begin{gathered}
\det A^T = \det A \\
\det(AB) = (\det A)(\det B)
\end{gathered}
$$

Day 27 proves the product rule. There is no rule for sums, and $\det(A + B)$ is usually not $\det A + \det B$.
</Concept>

Scaling the whole matrix is a special case worth doing slowly. The matrix $kA$ has every row of $A$ multiplied by $k$. For an $n \times n$ matrix that is $n$ separate row scalings, and each one multiplies the determinant by $k$, so

$$
\det(kA) = k^n \det A.
$$

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, $\det(3A) = 9\det A$, because both sides of the parallelogram get 3 times longer. Find the $k$ that makes the area 4 times larger.

<MultipleAreaScale matrix={[[1, -1], [1, 1]]} factor={4} />

<Warning>
$\det(kA)$ is not $k \det A$. For a $3 \times 3$ matrix with $\det A = 5$, $\det(2A) = 2^3 \cdot 5 = 40$, not $10$.
</Warning>

Let $A$ and $B$ be $3 \times 3$ matrices with $\det A = 2$ and $\det B = -3$. Find $\det(3A)$, $\det(AB^T)$ and $\det(A^TB^2)$. (Modeled on Lay §3.2 #39.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[54, -6, 18]} labels={["det(3A)", "det(ABᵀ)", "det(AᵀB²)"]} />

<Hint>
$3A$ scales three rows. For the others, use the product rule and $\det M^T = \det M$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Scaling a $3 \times 3$ matrix by $3$ scales three rows, so $\det(3A) = 3^3 \cdot 2 = 54$. The transpose keeps the determinant, so $\det(AB^T) = (\det A)(\det B) = 2(-3) = -6$. Finally, $\det(A^TB^2) = (\det A)(\det B)(\det B) = 2 \cdot 9 = 18$.
</Answer>
</Check>

### Challenge

<Check>
For which two values of $t$ is $\begin{bmatrix} t & 3 & 1 \\ 2 & t + 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ not invertible? (Modeled on Lay §3.2 #21–26.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-3, 2]} labels={["smaller t", "larger t"]} />

<Hint>
Expand along row 3. The matrix fails to be invertible exactly when the determinant is $0$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Row 3 has a single nonzero entry, $2$, at position $(3, 3)$ with sign $+$:

$$
\det = 2\begin{vmatrix} t & 3 \\ 2 & t + 1 \end{vmatrix} = 2\big(t(t + 1) - 6\big) = 2(t^2 + t - 6) = 2(t + 3)(t - 2).
$$

The determinant is $0$ when $t = -3$ or $t = 2$, so those are the two values where the matrix is not invertible. For every other $t$ its columns are linearly independent.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Compute $\det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 6 & 1 & 3 \\ -1 & -2 & 4 & 0 \\ 3 & 9 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ by clearing column 1 with replacements and then expanding. (Modeled on Lay §3.2 #11.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-15]} labels={["det"]} prefix="\det =" />

<Hint>
Use $R_2 - 2R_1$, $R_3 + R_1$ and $R_4 - 3R_1$. Then look at column 1 of the $3 \times 3$ minor.
</Hint>

<Answer>
The three replacements change nothing and leave rows $(0, 0, -3, 5)$, $(0, 1, 6, -1)$ and $(0, 0, -6, 5)$ under row 1. Expanding down column 1 leaves one term:

$$
\det = 1\begin{vmatrix} 0 & -3 & 5 \\ 1 & 6 & -1 \\ 0 & -6 & 5 \end{vmatrix}.
$$

Column 1 of this minor has only the $1$ at position $(2, 1)$, whose sign is $-$:

$$
\det = -1\begin{vmatrix} -3 & 5 \\ -6 & 5 \end{vmatrix} = -\big((-3)(5) - (5)(-6)\big) = -(-15 + 30) = -15.
$$
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
A square matrix is **skew-symmetric** when $A^T = -A$, for example $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -1 \\ -2 & 0 & 3 \\ 1 & -3 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Show that every $3 \times 3$ skew-symmetric matrix has determinant $0$.

<Hint>
Compute $\det A^T$ in two ways: once with the transpose rule, and once by writing $A^T = (-1)A$ and using the rule for $\det(kA)$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
The transpose rule gives $\det A^T = \det A$. Since $A^T = (-1)A$ and $A$ is $3 \times 3$, the scaling rule gives $\det A^T = (-1)^3 \det A = -\det A$. Both are the same number, so $\det A = -\det A$, and therefore $\det A = 0$.

The argument works for every odd size $n$, because $(-1)^n = -1$. For even $n$ it fails, and $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has determinant $1$.
</Answer>
</Check>
