Everything in the course so far has been built from adding and scaling vectors, and neither operation can tell you how long a vector is or whether two vectors are perpendicular. The rest of the course needs both. On Day 38 an equation $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ will have no solution, and you will look for the $\mathbf x$ that brings $A\mathbf x$ as close to $\mathbf b$ as possible, which is a question about distance. On Day 40 every matrix will turn out to send one set of perpendicular directions to another. Both rest on a single number, the dot product, which Day 34 borrowed early to show that a symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvectors. Today Day 1's vectors $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$ and $\blue{\mathbf w} = (-1, 2)$ come back to show what that number measures.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The box after each walkthrough names the idea you just used.

## The dot product

<Problem>
For $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$ and $\blue{\mathbf w} = (-1, 2)$, multiply matching entries and add: compute $\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \blue{\mathbf w}$ and $\blue{\mathbf w} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf v}$. Then compute $(2\yellow{\mathbf v}) \cdot \blue{\mathbf w}$.

<Solution>
Matching entries are the two first entries and the two second entries. So $\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \blue{\mathbf w} = (3)(-1) + (2)(2) = -3 + 4 = 1$.

Swapping the order multiplies the same pairs, since $(-1)(3) = (3)(-1)$ and $(2)(2) = (2)(2)$. So $\blue{\mathbf w} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf v} = 1$ too.

Doubling $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ gives $(6, 4)$, and $(6)(-1) + (4)(2) = -6 + 8 = 2$. Every product doubled, so the total doubled.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Two vectors in, one number out">
The **dot product** (or **inner product**) of two vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ multiplies matching entries and adds the results:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w &= \mathbf v^T\mathbf w \\
&= v_1w_1 + v_2w_2 + \cdots + v_nw_n.
\end{aligned}
$$

As the problem showed, order does not matter and scalars pull out: $\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf u$ and $(c\mathbf u) \cdot \mathbf v = c(\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v)$. It also splits over sums, $(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) \cdot \mathbf w = \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf w + \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w$.
</Concept>

## Length and distance

<Problem>
Compute $\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf v}$ for $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$. How does it compare with the length of the arrow from the Pythagorean theorem? Then find the length of $(1, -2, 2, 0)$ in $\mathbb R^4$.

<Solution>
$\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf v} = 3^2 + 2^2 = 13$. The arrow is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $3$ and $2$, so Pythagoras gives its length as $\sqrt{3^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{13}$. The dot product of a vector with itself is its squared length.

In $\mathbb R^4$ there is no picture, so we use the same formula as the definition: $1 + 4 + 4 + 0 = 9$, and the length is $\sqrt9 = 3$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Length comes from the dot product">
The **length** (or **norm**) of $\mathbf v$ is $\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}$. A **unit vector** has length 1, and dividing a nonzero $\mathbf v$ by $\|\mathbf v\|$ gives the unit vector pointing the same way. This step is called **normalizing** $\mathbf v$. For example, $(1, -2, 2, 0)$ normalizes to $\tfrac13(1, -2, 2, 0)$, and the check $\tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac49 + 0 = 1$ needs no square root.
</Concept>

Normalize a vector by hand. Find the scalar that puts the tip on the unit circle.

<UnitCircleScale vector={[3, 4]} start={0.5} />

<Warning>
Scaling multiplies length by the absolute value of the scalar: $\|c\mathbf v\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf v\|$. The vector $-2\mathbf v$ points the other way, but it is twice as long, not $-2$ times as long.
</Warning>

<Problem>
How far apart are the tips of $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$ and $\blue{\mathbf w} = (-1, 2)$?

<Solution>
The arrow from the tip of $\blue{\mathbf w}$ to the tip of $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ is $\yellow{\mathbf v} - \blue{\mathbf w}$ (Day 1). Here that is $(3 - (-1),\ 2 - 2) = (4, 0)$, and its length is $\sqrt{16 + 0} = 4$.

The picture agrees, since both tips sit at height $2$ and are $4$ apart sideways.
</Solution>
</Problem>

The **distance** between $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ is the length of their difference, $\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf u, \mathbf v) = \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\|$.

## The angle between two vectors

<Problem>
Let $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 0)$. Compute $\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \blue{\mathbf w}$ for $\blue{\mathbf w} = (1, 2)$, $(0, 2)$ and $(-1, 1)$. What does the sign tell you about where $\blue{\mathbf w}$ points?

<Solution>
Only the first entries matter, because $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ has second entry $0$: the dot products are $3 \cdot 1 = 3$, then $3 \cdot 0 = 0$, then $3 \cdot (-1) = -3$.

Drop a perpendicular from the tip of $\blue{\mathbf w}$ onto the $x$-axis, the line of $\yellow{\mathbf v}$. The foot lands at $1$, then $0$, then $-1$. That signed **shadow** is exactly what the dot product measured, times the length $3$ of $\yellow{\mathbf v}$. A positive shadow means $\blue{\mathbf w}$ leans the same way as $\yellow{\mathbf v}$, zero means it stands straight up, and a negative shadow means it leans backward.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="shadow" alt="Three copies of the same yellow vector v = (3, 2) with its faint line, and a blue vector w of length root 5 at 40, 90 and 140 degrees from v. A dashed drop runs from the tip of w to the line of v. At 40 degrees the shadow along v is a teal bar and v·w = 6.18. At 90 degrees there is no shadow, an orange right-angle mark sits at the origin, and v·w = 0.00. At 140 degrees the shadow is a pink bar pointing backward from the origin and v·w = −6.18." wide />

<Concept title="The dot product measures the angle">
The signed shadow of $\mathbf w$ on the line of $\mathbf v$ has length $\|\mathbf w\|\cos\theta$, and the dot product is $\|\mathbf v\|$ times that shadow:

$$
\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w = \|\mathbf v\|\,\|\mathbf w\|\cos\theta .
$$

A positive dot product means $\theta$ is acute, a negative one means $\theta$ is obtuse, and zero means a right angle. For Day 1's vectors, $\cos\theta = 1/(\sqrt{13}\sqrt5) \approx 0.124$, so $\theta \approx 82.9^\circ$, which is just short of perpendicular.
</Concept>

Make the shadow vanish. The shadow bar turns teal when it points along $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ and pink when it points backward.

## Orthogonal vectors

A zero dot product gives a right angle in the plane. The next problem shows why the same test makes sense in $\mathbb R^n$, where there is no protractor.

<Problem>
Let $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$ and $\blue{\mathbf w} = (-2, 3)$. Compute $\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \blue{\mathbf w}$, then the squared distances $\|\yellow{\mathbf v} - \blue{\mathbf w}\|^2$ and $\|\yellow{\mathbf v} + \blue{\mathbf w}\|^2$.

<Solution>
$\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \blue{\mathbf w} = -6 + 6 = 0$.

$\yellow{\mathbf v} - \blue{\mathbf w} = (5, -1)$, so $\|\yellow{\mathbf v} - \blue{\mathbf w}\|^2 = 25 + 1 = 26$. And $\yellow{\mathbf v} + \blue{\mathbf w} = (1, 5)$, so $\|\yellow{\mathbf v} + \blue{\mathbf w}\|^2 = 1 + 25 = 26$.

The tip of $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ is equally far from $\blue{\mathbf w}$ and from $-\blue{\mathbf w}$. In a picture, that is exactly what it means for the line through $\blue{\mathbf w}$ to be perpendicular to $\yellow{\mathbf v}$. Also, $26 = 13 + 13 = \|\yellow{\mathbf v}\|^2 + \|\blue{\mathbf w}\|^2$, which is Pythagoras for the right triangle with legs $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ and $\blue{\mathbf w}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="equal-distances" alt="Yellow v = (3, 2) with blue w at a right angle to it and gray −w pointing the opposite way. A blue dotted segment labelled ‖v − w‖ joins the tip of v to the tip of w, and a gray dotted segment labelled ‖v + w‖ joins the tip of v to the tip of −w. An orange right-angle mark sits between v and w." />

<Concept title="Equal distances mean a right angle">
Expanding both squared distances with the dot product rules gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf v - \mathbf w\|^2 &= \|\mathbf v\|^2 + \|\mathbf w\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w, \\
\|\mathbf v + \mathbf w\|^2 &= \|\mathbf v\|^2 + \|\mathbf w\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w .
\end{aligned}
$$

These agree only when $\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf w = 0$, and then the second line is the Pythagorean theorem.
</Concept>
</Pair>

<Definition term="Orthogonal vectors">
Two vectors $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ in $\mathbb R^n$ are **orthogonal** if $\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0$. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

**Pythagorean Theorem.** $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ are orthogonal if and only if $\|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\|^2 = \|\mathbf u\|^2 + \|\mathbf v\|^2$.
</Definition>

Balance the two distances yourself and watch the dot product.

## Orthogonal complements

<Problem>
Find every vector $\mathbf x = (x_1, x_2)$ that is orthogonal to $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$. Write the condition as a matrix equation.

<Solution>
The condition is $\yellow{\mathbf v} \cdot \mathbf x = 3x_1 + 2x_2 = 0$. That is one equation, which says $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ for the $1 \times 2$ matrix $A = [\,3\ \ 2\,]$.

Solve it as on Day 17: $x_1 = -\tfrac23 x_2$ with $x_2$ free. Taking $x_2 = 3$ gives $(-2, 3)$, so the answer is every multiple of $\blue{\mathbf u} = (-2, 3)$. That is the line through the origin perpendicular to $\yellow{\mathbf v}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="The perpendicular space is a null space">
If $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$, its **orthogonal complement** $W^\perp$ is the set of all vectors orthogonal to every vector in $W$. To test a vector you only need to check it against a spanning set of $W$. Put that spanning set into the rows of a matrix. Entry $i$ of $A\mathbf x$ is (row $i$)$\,\cdot\,\mathbf x$, so $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ says exactly that $\mathbf x$ is orthogonal to every row:

$$
\begin{aligned}
(\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp &= \operatorname{Nul}A, \\
(\operatorname{Col}A)^\perp &= \operatorname{Nul}A^T .
\end{aligned}
$$

By the rank theorem, $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = n$. In the problem, $1 + 1 = 2$.
</Concept>

Find a nonzero $\mathbf x$ that both rows send to zero.

The same idea works in $\mathbb R^4$, where no picture can help.

<Problem>
Let $W$ be spanned by $(1, 2, -1, 3)$ and $(2, 4, 1, 0)$. Find a basis for $W^\perp$.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Put the spanning vectors in the rows: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $W^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}A$.
</Step>
<Step>
Row reduce. Subtract twice row 1 from row 2 to get $(0, 0, 3, -6)$, divide by $3$ to get $(0, 0, 1, -2)$, then add it to row 1: $A \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Read off the solutions: $x_1 = -2x_2 - x_4$ and $x_3 = 2x_4$, with $x_2$ and $x_4$ free. Setting one free variable to 1 and the other to 0 gives $(-2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, 2, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Check against a row: $(1, 2, -1, 3) \cdot (-1, 0, 2, 1) = -1 + 0 - 2 + 3 = 0$. The dimensions add up, since $2 + 2 = 4$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
The spanning vectors go in the rows, and then you compute the null space. Also keep the pairs straight: $\operatorname{Nul}A$ is perpendicular to $\operatorname{Row}A$, not to $\operatorname{Col}A$.
</Warning>

## Orthogonal sets

Perpendicular vectors make coordinates easy to find, because each dot product sees only one of them.

<Problem>
The vectors $\yellow{\mathbf u_1} = (2, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf u_2} = (-1, 2)$ are orthogonal. Write $\teal{\mathbf y} = (4, 7)$ as $c_1\yellow{\mathbf u_1} + c_2\blue{\mathbf u_2}$ without row reducing.

<Solution>
Dot both sides of $\teal{\mathbf y} = c_1\yellow{\mathbf u_1} + c_2\blue{\mathbf u_2}$ with $\yellow{\mathbf u_1}$. The rules split the right side into $c_1(\yellow{\mathbf u_1} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf u_1}) + c_2(\blue{\mathbf u_2} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf u_1})$, and the second term is $0$ because the vectors are orthogonal.

That leaves $\teal{\mathbf y} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf u_1} = c_1(\yellow{\mathbf u_1} \cdot \yellow{\mathbf u_1})$, which is $8 + 7 = 15 = c_1 \cdot 5$. So $c_1 = 3$.

Dotting with $\blue{\mathbf u_2}$ instead gives $-4 + 14 = 10 = c_2 \cdot 5$, so $c_2 = 2$. Check: $3(2, 1) + 2(-1, 2) = (4, 7)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Each weight is one ratio">
A set $\{\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_p\}$ is an **orthogonal set** when every pair of different vectors in it has dot product $0$. If it is a basis for $W$ and $\mathbf y$ is in $W$, dotting $\mathbf y = c_1\mathbf u_1 + \cdots + c_p\mathbf u_p$ with $\mathbf u_j$ leaves only the $j$th term:

$$
c_j = \frac{\mathbf y \cdot \mathbf u_j}{\mathbf u_j \cdot \mathbf u_j}.
$$

The same trick with $\mathbf y = \mathbf 0$ gives every $c_j = 0$, so an orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is automatically linearly independent.
</Concept>

Rebuild $\teal{\mathbf y}$ with the sliders. The dashed drops show where the two ratios come from.

</Warning>

## Orthonormal columns

<Problem>
Let $U = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.8 \\ 0.8 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix}$. Check that its columns are unit vectors and orthogonal. Then compute $U\mathbf x$ for $\mathbf x = (1, 2)$ and compare the lengths of $\mathbf x$ and $U\mathbf x$.

<Solution>
Column 1 has squared length $0.36 + 0.64 = 1$, and so does column 2. Their dot product is $(0.6)(-0.8) + (0.8)(0.6) = -0.48 + 0.48 = 0$.

$U\mathbf x = (0.6 - 1.6,\ 0.8 + 1.2) = (-1, 2)$. Its squared length is $1 + 4 = 5$, and $\mathbf x$ has squared length $1 + 4 = 5$ as well. $U$ turned the vector without stretching it.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Orthonormal columns keep lengths">
An **orthonormal set** is an orthogonal set of unit vectors. A matrix $U$ has orthonormal columns exactly when $U^TU = I$, because entry $(i, j)$ of $U^TU$ is the dot product of columns $i$ and $j$. Such a matrix keeps dot products, since $(U\mathbf x) \cdot (U\mathbf y) = \mathbf x^TU^TU\mathbf y = \mathbf x \cdot \mathbf y$, and so it keeps lengths and right angles too.

A square matrix with orthonormal columns is an **orthogonal matrix**, and its inverse is $U^T$. Day 34's matrix $P$ of unit eigenvectors was one, and on Day 40 the singular value decomposition writes every matrix using two of them.
</Concept>

<Check>
Suppose $\mathbf u_1, \mathbf u_2, \mathbf u_3$ are nonzero and every pair is orthogonal. Show that they are linearly independent. (Modeled on Lay §6.2, Theorem 4.)

<Hint>
Start from $c_1\mathbf u_1 + c_2\mathbf u_2 + c_3\mathbf u_3 = \mathbf 0$ and dot both sides with $\mathbf u_1$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Suppose $c_1\mathbf u_1 + c_2\mathbf u_2 + c_3\mathbf u_3 = \mathbf 0$. Dot both sides with $\mathbf u_1$. The right side gives $0$.
