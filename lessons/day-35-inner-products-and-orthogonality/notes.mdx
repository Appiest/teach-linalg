Day 1's vectors $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (3, 2)$ and $\blue{\mathbf w} = (-1, 2)$ come back today with one new operation. The **dot product** (or **inner product**) of two vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ multiplies matching entries and adds the results, so it turns two vectors into a single number:

$$
\yellow{\mathbf v}\cdot\blue{\mathbf w} = \mathbf v^T\mathbf w = v_1w_1 + v_2w_2 + \cdots + v_nw_n, \qquad \yellow{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}\cdot\blue{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}} = (3)(-1) + (2)(2) = 1.
$$

Order does not matter, since $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u$. The dot product also splits over sums, $(\mathbf u + \mathbf v)\cdot\mathbf w = \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w + \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w$, and pulls out scalars, $(c\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v = c(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$.

## Length and distance

A vector dotted with itself is a sum of squares, so it is never negative. In $\mathbb R^2$ it is exactly the Pythagorean formula for the squared length of the arrow. The **length** (or **norm**) of $\mathbf v$ is therefore

$$
\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + \cdots + v_n^2}, \qquad \|\yellow{\mathbf v}\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{13}.
$$

Scaling multiplies length by the absolute value of the scalar, $\|c\mathbf v\| = |c|\,\|\mathbf v\|$. A **unit vector** has length 1, and dividing any nonzero $\mathbf v$ by $\|\mathbf v\|$ gives the unit vector pointing the same way. This step is called **normalizing** $\mathbf v$.

<UnitCircleScale vector={[3, 4]} start={0.5} />

The **distance** between $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ is the length of their difference, $\operatorname{dist}(\mathbf u, \mathbf v) = \|\mathbf u - \mathbf v\|$. The tips of $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ and $\blue{\mathbf w}$ sit at the same height, and the formula agrees with the picture: $\yellow{\mathbf v} - \blue{\mathbf w} = (4, 0)$, so the distance between them is $4$.

## The angle between two vectors

<Figure src="shadow" alt="Three copies of the same yellow vector v = (3, 2) with its faint line, and a blue vector w of length root 5 at 40, 90 and 140 degrees from v. A dashed drop runs from the tip of w to the line of v. At 40 degrees the shadow along v is a teal bar and v·w = 6.18. At 90 degrees there is no shadow, an orange right-angle mark sits at the origin, and v·w = 0.00. At 140 degrees the shadow is a pink bar pointing backward from the origin and v·w = −6.18." wide />

Drop a perpendicular from the tip of $\blue{\mathbf w}$ onto the line through $\yellow{\mathbf v}$. The signed length of that shadow is $\|\mathbf w\|\cos\theta$, and the dot product is the length of $\yellow{\mathbf v}$ times that shadow:

$$
\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w = \|\mathbf v\|\,\|\mathbf w\|\cos\theta .
$$

The sign reads off the angle. A positive dot product means $\theta$ is acute, a negative one means $\theta$ is obtuse and the shadow points backward, and zero means a right angle. For Day 1's vectors, $\cos\theta = 1/(\sqrt{13}\sqrt5) \approx 0.124$, so $\theta \approx 82.9^\circ$, which is just short of perpendicular.

<Definition term="Orthogonal vectors">
Two vectors $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ in $\mathbb R^n$ are **orthogonal** if $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = 0$. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector.

**Pythagorean Theorem.** $\mathbf u$ and $\mathbf v$ are orthogonal if and only if $\|\mathbf u + \mathbf v\|^2 = \|\mathbf u\|^2 + \|\mathbf v\|^2$.
</Definition>

<Pair>
<Figure src="equal-distances" alt="Yellow v = (3, 2) with blue w at a right angle to it and gray −w pointing the opposite way. A blue dotted segment labelled ‖v − w‖ joins the tip of v to the tip of w, and a gray dotted segment labelled ‖v + w‖ joins the tip of v to the tip of −w. An orange right-angle mark sits between v and w." />

The definition matches the picture for the following reason. The line through $\mathbf w$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf v$ exactly when the tip of $\mathbf v$ is as far from $\mathbf w$ as it is from $-\mathbf w$. Expanding both squared distances with the rules above gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf v - \mathbf w\|^2 &= \|\mathbf v\|^2 + \|\mathbf w\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w, \\
\|\mathbf v + \mathbf w\|^2 &= \|\mathbf v\|^2 + \|\mathbf w\|^2 + 2\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w .
\end{aligned}
$$

These agree only when $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w = 0$, and the second line then becomes the Pythagorean Theorem.
</Pair>

## Orthogonal complements

If $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$, its **orthogonal complement** $W^\perp$ is the set of all vectors orthogonal to every vector in $W$. It is a subspace too, and to test a vector you only need to check it against a spanning set of $W$. The row–column rule for $A\mathbf x$ says that entry $i$ of $A\mathbf x$ is (row $i$)$\,\cdot\,\mathbf x$, so $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ says exactly that $\mathbf x$ is orthogonal to every row:

$$
(\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}A \qquad\text{and}\qquad (\operatorname{Col}A)^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}A^T .
$$

So finding $W^\perp$ takes one null-space computation. Put a spanning set of $W$ in the rows of a matrix and solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$. With the rank theorem, $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = n$. In the video, $W$ is the line through $\yellow{\mathbf v}$, the matrix is $A = [\,3\ \ 2\,]$, and $W^\perp$ is the line through $\blue{\mathbf u} = (-2, 3)$, so $1 + 1 = 2$.
</Pair>

Here is the same idea in $\mathbb R^4$, where no picture can help. Take $W$ to be spanned by the rows of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
A &\sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} && x_1 = -2x_2 - x_4,\ \ x_3 = 2x_4 \\
W^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}A &= \operatorname{Span}\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\} && \text{free variables } x_2, x_4 \\
(1, 2, -1, 3)\cdot(-1, 0, 2, 1) &= -1 + 0 - 2 + 3 = 0 && \text{check against a row}
\end{aligned}
$$

The dimensions add up as they should, since $\dim W + \dim W^\perp = 2 + 2 = 4$.

## Orthogonal and orthonormal sets

A set $\{\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_p\}$ is an **orthogonal set** when every pair of different vectors in it has dot product $0$. An orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is automatically linearly independent. If $\mathbf 0 = c_1\mathbf u_1 + \cdots + c_p\mathbf u_p$, dotting both sides with $\mathbf u_1$ kills every other term and leaves $0 = c_1(\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_1)$, so $c_1 = 0$, and the same works for every weight.

<Pair>
<Figure src="orthogonal-weights" alt="Yellow v = (3, 2) and blue u = (−2, 3) with an orange right-angle mark between them. A blue arrow labelled −u runs from the tip of v down to the tip of teal y = v − u = (5, −1). Beside the grid, c1 = (y·v)/(v·v) = 13/13 = 1 and c2 = (y·u)/(u·u) = −13/13 = −1." />

The same trick finds coordinates. If $\{\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_p\}$ is an orthogonal basis for $W$ and $\mathbf y$ is in $W$, dotting $\mathbf y = c_1\mathbf u_1 + \cdots + c_p\mathbf u_p$ with $\mathbf u_j$ leaves only the $j$th term:

$$
c_j = \frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_j}{\mathbf u_j\cdot\mathbf u_j}.
$$

For $\teal{\mathbf y} = (5, -1)$ in the basis $\{\yellow{\mathbf v}, \blue{\mathbf u}\}$, the weights are $13/13 = 1$ and $-13/13 = -1$, with no row reduction.
</Pair>

An **orthonormal set** is an orthogonal set of unit vectors, such as $\{\yellow{\mathbf v}/\sqrt{13},\ \blue{\mathbf u}/\sqrt{13}\}$. A matrix $U$ has orthonormal columns exactly when $U^TU = I$, because entry $(i, j)$ of $U^TU$ is the dot product of columns $i$ and $j$. Such a matrix keeps lengths and dot products, since $(U\mathbf x)\cdot(U\mathbf y) = \mathbf x^TU^TU\mathbf y = \mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y$. A square matrix with orthonormal columns is called an **orthogonal matrix**, and its inverse is $U^T$.
