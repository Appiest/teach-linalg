Day 34 showed that every symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvectors, and today puts that fact to work. On Day 30 the symmetric matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ stretched the yellow line through $(1, 1)$ by $\yellow{3}$ and the blue line through $(-1, 1)$ by $\blue{1}$. Today the same matrix does a different job. It turns every vector $\mathbf x$ into a single number $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$, and squared lengths and variances are both numbers of this kind. Day 38's matrix $X^TX$ came from one, since $\|X\boldsymbol\beta\|^2 = \boldsymbol\beta^T(X^TX)\boldsymbol\beta$. We want to know what shape this function has, whether it can go negative, and which unit vector makes it largest. The eigenvectors and eigenvalues of $A$ answer all three questions, and Day 40 needs the last answer to find how far a matrix can stretch a vector.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The box after each walkthrough names the idea you just used.

## The matrix of a form

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute the number $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$. Then do the same computation with letters, $\mathbf x = (x_1, x_2)$, and write the answer as a polynomial.

<Solution>
Read $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$ from right to left. First $A$ acts on $\mathbf x$, then the row $\mathbf x^T$ dots with the result.

<Steps>
<Step>
$A\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 2 \\ 1 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
$\mathbf x^T(A\mathbf x) = 1\cdot 4 + 2\cdot 5 = \teal{14}$.
</Step>
<Step>
With letters, $A\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1 + x_2 \\ x_1 + 2x_2 \end{bmatrix}$, and dotting with $(x_1, x_2)$ gives

$$
x_1(2x_1 + x_2) + x_2(x_1 + 2x_2) = 2x_1^2 + \glow{1}\,x_1x_2 + \glow{1}\,x_2x_1 + 2x_2^2 = 2x_1^2 + \glow{2}\,x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Check at $(1, 2)$: $2 + 2\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 4 = 2 + 4 + 8 = 14$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The diagonal entries of $A$ became the coefficients of the squares. The two off-diagonal $1$'s each contributed one copy of $x_1x_2$, so together they made the cross coefficient $2$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Quadratic form">
A **quadratic form** on $\mathbb R^n$ is a function $Q(\mathbf x) = \mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$, where $A$ is a symmetric $n \times n$ matrix called the **matrix of the form**. Every term of $Q$ has degree two: a square $x_i^2$ or a **cross term** $x_ix_j$ with $i \ne j$. The coefficient of $x_ix_j$ is $a_{ij} + a_{ji} = 2a_{ij}$.
</Definition>

<Problem>
Find the symmetric matrix of $Q(\mathbf x) = 5x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 - x_2^2$.

<Solution>
Run Problem 1 backward. The square coefficients go on the diagonal, so $a_{11} = 5$ and $a_{22} = -1$. The cross coefficient $6$ has to come from two equal entries $a_{12} = a_{21}$, and $2a_{12} = 6$ gives $a_{12} = 3$:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check by expanding: $5x_1^2 + 3x_1x_2 + 3x_2x_1 - x_2^2 = 5x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 - x_2^2$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
Split each cross coefficient in half. Putting the full $6$ in both off-diagonal spots would give $12x_1x_2$, twice the form you started with. Expanding back is the quick check.
</Warning>

All the points where $Q$ has the same value form a curve. Find another point on the curve through Problem 1's $(1, 2)$.

## Principal axes

The curve $Q = 14$ is a tilted ellipse, and the cross term is what tilts it. The goal of this section is a set of axes in which the cross term disappears, because then the shape can be read straight from the coefficients.

<Problem>
Measure positions along the unit eigenvectors of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ instead of along the usual axes. Write $\mathbf x = y_1\yellow{\mathbf u_1} + y_2\blue{\mathbf u_2}$ with $\yellow{\mathbf u_1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ for eigenvalue $\yellow 3$ and $\blue{\mathbf u_2} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-1, 1)$ for eigenvalue $\blue 1$. Show that $Q = 3y_1^2 + y_2^2$, and check it at $\mathbf x = (1, 2)$.

<Solution>
Expand $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x = y_1\mathbf u_1 + y_2\mathbf u_2$. The eigenvector equations do most of the work.

<Steps>
<Step>
Apply $A$. Since $A\mathbf u_1 = 3\mathbf u_1$ and $A\mathbf u_2 = \mathbf u_2$, we get $A\mathbf x = 3y_1\mathbf u_1 + y_2\mathbf u_2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Dot with $\mathbf x = y_1\mathbf u_1 + y_2\mathbf u_2$. Because $\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_1 = \mathbf u_2\cdot\mathbf u_2 = 1$ and $\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2 = 0$, the mixed products vanish:

$$
\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x = 3y_1^2\,(\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_1) + 3y_1y_2\,(\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2) + y_1y_2\,(\mathbf u_2\cdot\mathbf u_1) + y_2^2\,(\mathbf u_2\cdot\mathbf u_2) = 3y_1^2 + y_2^2.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Find the new coordinates of $(1, 2)$. For an orthonormal basis each coordinate is a dot product: $y_1 = \mathbf x\cdot\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}$ and $y_2 = \mathbf x\cdot\mathbf u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Evaluate: $3\cdot\tfrac92 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac{28}{2} = \teal{14}$, the same value as Problem 1.
</Step>
</Steps>

The cross term vanished because the eigenvectors are perpendicular. The coefficients that remain are the eigenvalues.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="principal-axes" alt="The teal ellipse 2x1² + 2x1x2 + 2x2² = 14, tilted 45 degrees, under a purple-gray grid turned by 45 degrees. The yellow y1 axis runs along (1, 1) and the blue y2 axis along (−1, 1). A thick yellow half-axis reaches the ellipse along y1 and a longer thick blue half-axis reaches it along y2. Beside it, the equation is written both ways: with the orange cross term 2x1x2, and as 3y1² + 1y2² = 14." />

In the new axes the ellipse is $3y_1^2 + y_2^2 = 14$, which sits in **standard position**. Along $\yellow{y_1}$ the other coordinate is $0$, so $3y_1^2 = 14$ and the half-axis is $\sqrt{14/\yellow{3}}$. Along $\blue{y_2}$ the half-axis is $\sqrt{14/\blue{1}}$. A bigger eigenvalue always gives a shorter axis.
</Pair>

Day 34 guarantees the right axes always exist. A symmetric $A$ factors as $A = PDP^T$, where the columns of $P$ are orthonormal eigenvectors and $D$ holds the eigenvalues. Substituting $\mathbf x = P\mathbf y$ does Problem 3 for every matrix at once:

$$
\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x = (P\mathbf y)^TA(P\mathbf y) = \mathbf y^T(P^TAP)\mathbf y = \mathbf y^TD\mathbf y = \lambda_1y_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_ny_n^2.
$$

<Definition term="The principal axes theorem">
For every symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$ there is an orthogonal change of variable $\mathbf x = P\mathbf y$ that turns $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$ into $\mathbf y^TD\mathbf y$ with no cross terms. The columns of $P$, which are unit eigenvectors of $A$, are the **principal axes** of the form.
</Definition>

The widget uses $A = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Turn the axes until the orange cross term reads zero.

<QuadraticAxisTurner matrix={[[6, 2], [2, 3]]} level={14} start={[1, 0]} />

<Problem>
The widget's ellipse is $6x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2 = 14$. Find its half-axis lengths and the directions they point.

<Solution>
The half-axes lie along the eigenvectors, and their lengths come from the eigenvalues, so find those first.

<Steps>
<Step>
The characteristic polynomial is $(6 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 14 = (\lambda - 7)(\lambda - 2)$, so the eigenvalues are $7$ and $2$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $\lambda = 7$, $A - 7I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$, whose null space is spanned by $(2, 1)$. For $\lambda = 2$, $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, whose null space is spanned by $(1, -2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
In principal axes the ellipse is $7y_1^2 + 2y_2^2 = 14$. The half-axis along $(2, 1)$ is $\sqrt{14/7} = \sqrt2$, and the half-axis along $(1, -2)$ is $\sqrt{14/2} = \sqrt7$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
Normalize the eigenvectors before you put them in $P$. With $P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ the columns have length $\sqrt5$, so $P^TAP = 5D$ instead of $D$, and $\mathbf y$ no longer has the same length as $\mathbf x$.
</Warning>

## Definiteness

The next question is whether $Q$ ever goes negative, which decides whether the origin sits at the bottom of a bowl.

<Problem>
Every coefficient of $Q(\mathbf x) = x_1^2 + 4x_1x_2 + x_2^2$ is positive. Is $Q(\mathbf x) > 0$ for every nonzero $\mathbf x$?

<Solution>
Plus signs make it tempting to say yes, so test a point where the cross term might fight the squares. The cross term $4x_1x_2$ is negative when $x_1$ and $x_2$ have opposite signs, so try $(1, -1)$:

$$
Q(1, -1) = 1 + 4(1)(-1) + 1 = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2.
$$

So the answer is no. The eigenvalues explain it. The matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, with characteristic polynomial $(1 - \lambda)^2 - 4$, so $\lambda = 1 \pm 2$, which gives $3$ and $-1$. In principal axes $Q = 3y_1^2 - y_2^2$, and moving along the eigenvector for $-1$ makes $Q$ negative. That eigenvector is $(1, -1)$, the point we tried.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<NegativeDirectionHunt matrix={[[1, 2], [2, 1]]} start={[1, 1]} />

In principal axes $Q = \lambda_1y_1^2 + \lambda_2y_2^2$, and each squared term is never negative. So the signs of the eigenvalues decide the signs of $Q$. When both are positive, every nonzero $\mathbf x$ gives a positive value and the graph is a bowl. When one is $0$, the graph stays on the floor along that eigenvector. When one is negative, the graph dips below the floor in that direction and becomes a saddle.

<Figure src="bowl-trough-saddle" alt="Three graphs of z = Q(x) over a disk, each with the yellow and blue eigen-lines on the floor and the unit circle lifted onto the surface in white. With eigenvalues 3 and 1 the surface is a teal bowl, labelled positive definite. With 3 and 0 it is a teal trough that stays on the floor along the blue line, labelled positive semidefinite. With 3 and −2 it is a saddle, teal where it rises along yellow and pink where it drops below the floor along blue, labelled indefinite." wide />

<Definition term="Classifying a form by its eigenvalues">
A quadratic form $Q$ is **positive definite** if $Q(\mathbf x) > 0$ for every $\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0$, **negative definite** if $Q(\mathbf x) < 0$ for every $\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0$, and **indefinite** if it takes both positive and negative values. It is **positive semidefinite** if $Q(\mathbf x) \ge 0$ for all $\mathbf x$. For symmetric $A$, the form $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$ is positive definite exactly when every eigenvalue is positive, negative definite exactly when every eigenvalue is negative, and indefinite exactly when $A$ has eigenvalues of both signs.
</Definition>

Slide the off-diagonal entry and watch the bowl flatten and then turn into a saddle.

<QuadraticSurfaceShaper a={1} d={4} start={0} />

On Day 40 the form $\mathbf x^T(A^TA)\mathbf x$ equals $\|A\mathbf x\|^2$, which is never negative, so every eigenvalue of $A^TA$ is at least $0$.

  options={["Positive definite", "Positive semidefinite", "Indefinite", "Negative definite"]}

>
The matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $3 \pm 1$, which are $4$ and $2$. Both are positive, so $Q > 0$ for every nonzero $\mathbf x$.
</Choice>

## The unit circle constraint

The last question is which unit vector makes $Q$ largest. Start with a form that has no cross term, where the answer can be read off.

<Problem>
Find the largest and smallest values of $Q(\mathbf x) = 3x_1^2 + 7x_2^2$ over all unit vectors, $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1$.

<Solution>
Compare $Q$ with multiples of $x_1^2 + x_2^2$, which we know equals $1$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q &= 3x_1^2 + 7x_2^2 \le 7x_1^2 + 7x_2^2 = 7(x_1^2 + x_2^2) = 7 \\
Q &= 3x_1^2 + 7x_2^2 \ge 3x_1^2 + 3x_2^2 = 3(x_1^2 + x_2^2) = 3
\end{aligned}
$$

Both bounds are reached. At $(0, 1)$, $Q = 7$, and at $(1, 0)$, $Q = 3$. So the largest value is $7$ and the smallest is $3$. They are the largest and smallest coefficients, which here are the eigenvalues of the diagonal matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="unit-circle-heights" alt="A graph of Q(cos θ, sin θ) = 2 + sin 2θ for θ from 0 to 360 degrees. The teal curve waves between 1 and 3. Yellow dots mark its tops at 45 and 225 degrees, labelled M = λ1 = 3, and blue dots mark its bottoms at 135 and 315 degrees, labelled m = λ2 = 1." />

With a cross term, switch to principal axes first. Because $P$ is orthogonal, $\|\mathbf y\| = \|\mathbf x\| = 1$ too, so $y_1^2 + y_2^2 = 1$ and Problem 6's argument applies to $\lambda_1y_1^2 + \lambda_2y_2^2$. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ the values on the unit circle run from $1$ to $3$, as the graph shows.
</Pair>

<Concept title="Largest value on the unit sphere">
Over unit vectors, the largest value of $\mathbf x^TA\mathbf x$ is the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of $A$, reached at its unit eigenvector $\mathbf u_1$. The smallest value is the smallest eigenvalue, reached at its unit eigenvector. A one-line check: if $A\mathbf u = \lambda\mathbf u$ and $\|\mathbf u\| = 1$, then $\mathbf u^TA\mathbf u = \mathbf u^T(\lambda\mathbf u) = \lambda$.
</Concept>

<QuadraticCircleMax matrix={[[5, 2], [2, 2]]} start={[1, -1]} />

<Warning>
The maximizer is $\mathbf u_1$, written in the original coordinates. For $Q = 2x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2$ the maximum $3$ happens at $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, not at $(1, 0)$, even though $\mathbf y = (1, 0)$ maximizes $3y_1^2 + y_2^2$. Also, "largest" means furthest to the right on the number line. With eigenvalues $2$ and $-4$ the maximum is $2$.
</Warning>

In $\mathbb R^n$ this works one eigenvalue at a time. If you also require $\mathbf x$ to be perpendicular to the top unit eigenvector $\mathbf u_1$, then $y_1 = 0$ and the largest term drops out. The maximum becomes the second largest eigenvalue $\lambda_2$, reached at $\mathbf u_2$.

A constraint shaped like an ellipse can be turned into a circle by rescaling each variable.

<Problem>
Maximize $q = xy$ subject to $9x^2 + 16y^2 = 144$ with $x, y \ge 0$.

<Solution>
The theory above needs the constraint to be a unit circle, so rescale the variables until it is one.

<Steps>
<Step>
Divide by $144$: $\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$, which is $\left(\frac x4\right)^2 + \left(\frac y3\right)^2 = 1$. Set $x_1 = \frac x4$ and $x_2 = \frac y3$, so the constraint is $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step>
Rewrite the target: $xy = (4x_1)(3x_2) = 12x_1x_2$. Its matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 6 \\ 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, because the cross coefficient $12$ splits into two $6$'s.
</Step>
<Step>
The eigenvalues are $\pm 6$, and the eigenvector for $6$ is $(1, 1)$. So the maximum is $6$, at $x_1 = x_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Translate back: $x = 4x_1 = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83$ and $y = 3x_2 = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt2} \approx 2.12$. Check: $xy = 2\sqrt2\cdot\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2} = 6$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

Let $A$ be any $m \times n$ matrix. Show that the form $\mathbf x^T(A^TA)\mathbf x$ is positive semidefinite, and conclude that every eigenvalue of $A^TA$ is at least $0$.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer>
$\mathbf x^T(A^TA)\mathbf x = (A\mathbf x)^T(A\mathbf x) = \|A\mathbf x\|^2 \ge 0$ for every $\mathbf x$, so the form is positive semidefinite. $A^TA$ is symmetric, since $(A^TA)^T = A^TA$. If $\mathbf u$ is a unit eigenvector with $A^TA\mathbf u = \lambda\mathbf u$, then $\lambda = \mathbf u^T(A^TA)\mathbf u = \|A\mathbf u\|^2 \ge 0$. Day 40 takes square roots of these eigenvalues to get the singular values of $A$.
