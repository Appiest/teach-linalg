Day 21 showed that one linear map has a different matrix in every basis, and on Day 22 the map $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ got a triangular matrix in the basis $\{(2, 1), (-1, 2)\}$. That raises a question: which basis gives a map its simplest possible matrix? Day 32 factored matrices as $A = PDP^{-1}$, and today reads that factorization as the answer. If you describe a map in a basis made of its own eigenvectors, its matrix is diagonal, because the map only stretches each basis vector.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The box after each walkthrough names the idea you just used.

## The matrix in a basis

We start by rebuilding Day 21's recipe on a small example, since everything today is that recipe with a well-chosen basis.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathcal B = \{\yellow{\mathbf b_1}, \blue{\mathbf b_2}\}$ with $\yellow{\mathbf b_1} = (1, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf b_2} = (1, 0)$. Write $A\yellow{\mathbf b_1}$ and $A\blue{\mathbf b_2}$ as combinations of $\yellow{\mathbf b_1}$ and $\blue{\mathbf b_2}$. Then build the matrix $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ of $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$.

<Solution>
Day 21's rule says column $j$ of $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ holds the $\mathcal B$-coordinates of $T(\mathbf b_j)$. So we need the two images and then their coordinates.

<Steps>
<Step>
Compute the images: $A\yellow{\mathbf b_1} = (5 - 2,\ 4 - 1) = (3, 3)$ and $A\blue{\mathbf b_2} = (5, 4)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Write $(3, 3)$ in the basis. It is $3\yellow{\mathbf b_1} + 0\blue{\mathbf b_2}$, since $\yellow{\mathbf b_1} = (1, 1)$. So column 1 is $(3, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Write $(5, 4)$ in the basis. We need $c_1(1, 1) + c_2(1, 0) = (5, 4)$. The second entry says $c_1 = 4$, and then the first says $4 + c_2 = 5$, so $c_2 = 1$. Column 2 is $(4, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Put the columns side by side: $[T]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The first column came out with a single nonzero entry because $\yellow{\mathbf b_1}$ is an eigenvector: $A\yellow{\mathbf b_1} = 3\yellow{\mathbf b_1}$. The second basis vector is not an eigenvector, so its column needs both entries.
</Solution>
</Problem>

Build the second column yourself on the slanted grid of $\yellow{\mathbf b_1}$ and $\blue{\mathbf b_2}$.

<BasisColumnBuilder matrix={[[5, -2], [4, -1]]} b1={[1, 1]} b2={[1, 0]} column={1} />

<Warning>
Column $j$ holds the coordinates $[A\mathbf b_j]_{\mathcal B}$, not the vector $A\mathbf b_j$ itself. Here $A\blue{\mathbf b_2} = (5, 4)$, but the column is $(4, 1)$. The two agree only when $\mathcal B$ is the standard basis.
</Warning>

## An eigenvector basis

<Problem>
Keep the same $A$ and $\yellow{\mathbf b_1} = (1, 1)$, but replace the second basis vector with $\blue{\mathbf b_2} = (1, 2)$. Find the new $[T]_{\mathcal B}$.

<Solution>
Again we need the images and their coordinates. $A\yellow{\mathbf b_1} = (3, 3) = 3\yellow{\mathbf b_1}$ as before. Now $A\blue{\mathbf b_2} = (5 - 4,\ 4 - 2) = (1, 2)$, which is $1\blue{\mathbf b_2}$.

Both images are multiples of their own basis vector, so each column has one nonzero entry and it sits on the diagonal:

$$
[T]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Changing one basis vector into an eigenvector cleared the off-diagonal entry.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Eigenvectors give a diagonal matrix">
When every basis vector is an eigenvector, $T(\mathbf b_j) = \lambda_j\mathbf b_j$, so column $j$ of $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ is $\lambda_j$ times the $j$th standard column. The matrix is diagonal with the eigenvalues in basis order. That is exactly the $D$ from Day 32.
</Concept>

<Definition term="Diagonal matrix representation">
Suppose $A = PDP^{-1}$, where $D$ is a diagonal $n \times n$ matrix. If $\mathcal B$ is the basis of $\mathbb R^n$ formed from the columns of $P$, then $D$ is the $\mathcal B$-matrix of the transformation $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ (Lay §5.4, Theorem 8).
</Definition>

The proof is Day 22's formula read column by column, with $P = [\,\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,]$ and $[\mathbf y]_{\mathcal B} = P^{-1}\mathbf y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[T]_{\mathcal B} &= \big[\,[A\mathbf b_1]_{\mathcal B}\ \cdots\ [A\mathbf b_n]_{\mathcal B}\,\big] \\
&= \big[\,P^{-1}A\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ P^{-1}A\mathbf b_n\,\big] \\
&= P^{-1}A\,[\,\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,] \\
&= P^{-1}AP = D.
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line is the definition of $[T]_{\mathcal B}$. The second uses $[\mathbf y]_{\mathcal B} = P^{-1}\mathbf y$, the third pulls $P^{-1}A$ out of every column, and the last uses $A = PDP^{-1}$.

In the picture, the same arrow $\yellow{\mathbf x}$ goes to the same arrow $\teal{A\mathbf x}$ on both sides. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, standard coordinates say $(1, 2) \mapsto (5, 1)$ and show no pattern. In the eigenvector basis $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$, $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (-1, 1)$, the same move is $(1, 1) \mapsto (2, -1)$, which doubles the first coordinate and flips the second.

On the right, the purple-gray grid of v1 = (2, 1) and v2 = (−1, 1) still lines up with itself: yellow v1 has doubled and blue v2 has flipped, and Ax is two yellow steps and one backward blue step. Try it with the same map. Drag $\mathbf x$ in standard coordinates on the left and watch its eigen-coordinates on the right, where $A$ only scales each axis.

<TwoCoordinateMap matrix={[[1, 2], [1, 0]]} b1={[2, 1]} b2={[-1, 1]} target={[2, 1]} />

For this map, $P = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{2} & \blue{-1} \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{1} \end{bmatrix}$ has determinant $3$, and the check comes out diagonal:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P^{-1}AP &= \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
&= \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{2} & 0 \\ 0 & \blue{-1} \end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

You never need $P^{-1}$ on its own. Row reducing $[\,P \ \ AP\,]$ to $[\,I \ \ P^{-1}AP\,]$ gives the same answer with less arithmetic.

Now find an eigenvector basis for a different matrix. The readout computes $[T]_{\mathcal B} = P^{-1}AP$ for whatever basis you drag, and the two off-diagonal entries glow orange while they are not zero.

Swapping $\mathbf b_1$ and $\mathbf b_2$ swaps the two eigenvalues on the diagonal. Both answers are correct as long as the order of $D$ matches the order of $\mathcal B$.

## Similar matrices

<Problem>
Three matrices describe $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$: $A$ itself in the standard basis, $C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ in Day 22's basis $\{(2, 1), (-1, 2)\}$, and $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ in the eigenvector basis. Compute the trace and determinant of each.

<Solution>
The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries, and the $2 \times 2$ determinant is $ad - bc$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A&: \quad \operatorname{tr} = 1 + 0 = 1, \qquad \det = 1 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 1 = -2 \\
C&: \quad \operatorname{tr} = 2 + (-1) = 1, \qquad \det = 2 \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot 0 = -2 \\
D&: \quad \operatorname{tr} = 2 + (-1) = 1, \qquad \det = 2 \cdot (-1) - 0 = -2
\end{aligned}
$$

All three agree. They describe one map, so they share every number that does not depend on the choice of coordinates, including the eigenvalues $2$ and $-1$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="square" alt="A square diagram. The top edge sends x to Ax by A. The left edge goes up from [x]_B to x by P, the right edge goes down from Ax to [Ax]_B by P inverse, and the bottom edge sends [x]_B to [Ax]_B by D. Below it, one third of [1 1; −1 2] times [1 2; 1 0] times [2 −1; 1 1] equals [2 0; 0 −1]." />

<Concept title="Similar means same map">
The proof above never used that $D$ is diagonal. For any invertible $P$, the matrix $C = P^{-1}AP$ is the matrix of $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ in the basis formed by the columns of $P$. So **similar** matrices are one linear map written in different coordinates. Going around the square, $P$ turns coordinates into a vector, $A$ acts, and $P^{-1}$ turns the result back into coordinates.
</Concept>
</Pair>

<Choice
  question="Which matrix cannot be a matrix of $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ in any basis?"
  options={["$\\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 5 \\\\ 0 & -1 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{bmatrix}$"]}
  answer={2}
>
Every matrix of this map has trace $1$ and determinant $-2$. The last matrix has trace $3$ and determinant $2$, so it describes a different map. The first is $D$ with the basis order swapped, and the second is triangular with the right eigenvalues, which some basis produces.
</Choice>

Not every map has a diagonal matrix. For $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -9 \\ 4 & -8 \end{bmatrix}$, whose only eigenvalue is $-2$ with a one-dimensional eigenspace, the best basis is a Jordan chain like Day 31's. The chain $\mathbf b_2 = (2, 1) \to \mathbf b_1 = (3, 2)$ gives the Jordan form $\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Warning>
The $\mathcal B$-matrix is $P^{-1}AP$, not $PAP^{-1}$. Read it right to left: $P$ turns $\mathcal B$-coordinates into a vector, $A$ acts on the vector, and $P^{-1}$ turns the answer back into $\mathcal B$-coordinates.
</Warning>

## A map on polynomials

The same idea works in any vector space, as long as we use coordinates.

<Problem>
Let $T: \mathbb P_1 \to \mathbb P_1$ be $T(a_0 + a_1t) = (a_0 + a_1) + 2a_1t$. Find its matrix in the basis $\{1, t\}$. Then find two polynomials that $T$ only rescales.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Apply $T$ to each basis polynomial. $T(1)$ has $a_0 = 1, a_1 = 0$, so $T(1) = 1$. $T(t)$ has $a_0 = 0, a_1 = 1$, so $T(t) = 1 + 2t$.
</Step>
<Step>
Record the coordinates as columns: $T(1) = 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot t$ and $T(t) = 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot t$, so the matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
The matrix is triangular, so its eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$. For $1$, the shifted matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ forces $a_1 = 0$, which gives the polynomial $1$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $2$, the shifted matrix $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ says $a_0 = a_1$, which gives $1 + t$. Check: $T(1 + t) = 2 + 2t = 2(1 + t)$.
</Step>
</Steps>

In the basis $\mathcal E = \{1,\ 1 + t\}$, the matrix of $T$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

An eigenvector here is a polynomial whose graph $T$ only stretches vertically, so the graph of $p$ and the graph of $T(p)$ cross the $t$-axis at the same point. Find both.

<EigenPolynomialHunt matrix={[[1, 1], [0, 2]]} />

Some maps have no eigenvector basis. The derivative $\tfrac{d}{dt}$ on $\mathbb P_2$ has only the constants as eigenvectors, so no basis of three eigenvectors exists, and Day 31's chain $t^2 \to 2t \to 2$ is the best basis available.

## Powers and dynamical systems

On Day 30, repeated multiplication swung a vector toward one eigenvector's line. Eigen-coordinates let us predict that motion exactly without multiplying any matrices.

<Problem>
Keep $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$ for $\lambda = 2$ and $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (-1, 1)$ for $\lambda = -1$. Let $\mathbf x_0 = (1, 2)$ and $\mathbf x_{k+1} = A\mathbf x_k$. Find $\mathbf x_3$ without multiplying by $A$ three times.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Write $\mathbf x_0$ in the eigenvector basis. $c_1(2, 1) + c_2(-1, 1) = (1, 2)$ gives $2c_1 - c_2 = 1$ and $c_1 + c_2 = 2$. Adding them gives $3c_1 = 3$, so $c_1 = 1$ and $c_2 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step>
Each step multiplies the first coordinate by $2$ and the second by $-1$. After three steps the coordinates are $(2^3 \cdot 1,\ (-1)^3 \cdot 1) = (8, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Convert back: $\mathbf x_3 = 8\yellow{\mathbf v_1} - \blue{\mathbf v_2} = (16, 8) - (-1, 1) = (17, 7)$.
</Step>
</Steps>

Check the slow way: $A(1, 2) = (5, 1)$, then $A(5, 1) = (7, 5)$, then $A(7, 5) = (17, 7)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Powers act one coordinate at a time">
If $\mathbf x_0 = c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2$ in an eigenvector basis, then

$$
\mathbf x_k = A^k\mathbf x_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\,\mathbf v_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\,\mathbf v_2, \qquad A^k = PD^kP^{-1}.
$$

In the long run, the eigenvalue with the largest absolute value decides where the system heads.
</Concept>

<Pair>
<Figure src="orbit" alt="The purple-gray eigen-grid of v1 = (2, 1) and v2 = (−1, 1). Teal dots x0 through x4 zigzag across the yellow line through v1, joined by dashed segments, and a teal arrow points to x4 = (7, 5). Beside the plane, x0 = ¼v1 + v2, x1 = ½v1 − v2, x2 = v1 + v2, x3 = 2v1 − v2, x4 = 4v1 + v2, and A^k = PD^kP⁻¹." />

With $\mathbf x_0 = \tfrac14\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + \blue{\mathbf v_2}$, the yellow part doubles and the blue part flips at every step. The points jump back and forth across the line through $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$. The yellow part keeps growing while the blue part stays the same size, so the direction of $\mathbf x_k$ swings closer to that line each time.
</Pair>

In the next widget the two eigenvectors are the same, but the eigenvalues are $1$ and $\tfrac12$. The $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ part now halves at each step and dies out, so every orbit settles at $c_1\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$.

<EigenOrbitSettle v1={[2, 1]} v2={[-1, 1]} lambdas={[1, 0.5]} target={[4, 2]} />

### Challenge

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.75 & 0.25 \\ 0.25 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ for $\lambda = 1$ and $(1, -1)$ for $\lambda = \tfrac12$. Where does $\mathbf x_k = A^k\mathbf x_0$ settle as $k$ grows, for $\mathbf x_0 = (3, 1)$? (Modeled on Lay §5.2, Example 5.)

<Check>
The trace satisfies $\operatorname{tr}(FG) = \operatorname{tr}(GF)$ for any $n \times n$ matrices $F$ and $G$. Use this to show that similar matrices have the same trace. (Modeled on Lay §5.4 #25.)

<Hint>
Write $A = PBP^{-1}$ and group it as $(PB)P^{-1}$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Suppose $A = PBP^{-1}$. Take $F = PB$ and $G = P^{-1}$. Then

$$
\operatorname{tr}A = \operatorname{tr}\big((PB)P^{-1}\big) = \operatorname{tr}\big(P^{-1}(PB)\big) = \operatorname{tr}\big((P^{-1}P)B\big) = \operatorname{tr}B.

This matches the picture: $A$ and $B$ are one map in two coordinate systems, and the trace belongs to the map.
