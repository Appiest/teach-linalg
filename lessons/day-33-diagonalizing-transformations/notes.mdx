On Day 22 the map $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ got a triangular matrix in the basis $\{(2, 1), (-1, 2)\}$, and Day 32 factored matrices as $A = PDP^{-1}$. Today puts the two ideas together. If you describe a map in a basis made of its own eigenvectors, its matrix is diagonal, because the map only stretches each basis vector.

Both halves show the same arrow $\yellow{\mathbf x}$ going to the same arrow $\teal{A\mathbf x}$. Only the coordinates differ. In standard coordinates, $(1, 2) \mapsto (5, 1)$ and it is hard to see a pattern. In the eigenvector basis $\mathcal B = \{\yellow{\mathbf v_1}, \blue{\mathbf v_2}\}$ with $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (-1, 1)$, the same move is $(1, 1) \mapsto (2, -1)$, which doubles the first coordinate and flips the sign of the second.

## The matrix in an eigenvector basis

Recall from Day 21 that the **$\mathcal B$-matrix** $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ has the coordinate vectors $[T(\mathbf b_j)]_{\mathcal B}$ as its columns, so that $[T(\mathbf x)]_{\mathcal B} = [T]_{\mathcal B}[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$. When the basis vectors are eigenvectors, each $T(\mathbf b_j)$ is a multiple of $\mathbf b_j$ alone, so each column has a single nonzero entry, and it sits on the diagonal.

<Definition term="Diagonal matrix representation">
Suppose $A = PDP^{-1}$, where $D$ is a diagonal $n \times n$ matrix. If $\mathcal B$ is the basis of $\mathbb R^n$ formed from the columns of $P$, then $D$ is the $\mathcal B$-matrix of the transformation $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$.
</Definition>

The proof is Day 22's formula read column by column, with $P = [\,\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,]$ and $[\mathbf y]_{\mathcal B} = P^{-1}\mathbf y$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[T]_{\mathcal B} &= \big[\,[A\mathbf b_1]_{\mathcal B}\ \cdots\ [A\mathbf b_n]_{\mathcal B}\,\big] && \text{definition of } [T]_{\mathcal B} \\
&= \big[\,P^{-1}A\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ P^{-1}A\mathbf b_n\,\big] && \text{change of coordinates} \\
&= P^{-1}A\,[\,\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,] = P^{-1}AP = D. && \text{since } A = PDP^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

For the video's matrix, $P = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{2} & \blue{-1} \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{1} \end{bmatrix}$ has determinant $3$, and the check comes out diagonal:

$$
P^{-1}AP = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac13\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{2} & 0 \\ 0 & \blue{-1} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

You never need $P^{-1}$ on its own. Row reducing $[\,P \ \ AP\,]$ to $[\,I \ \ P^{-1}AP\,]$ gives the same answer with less arithmetic.

Order matters only for the diagonal. Swapping $\mathbf b_1$ and $\mathbf b_2$ swaps the two eigenvalues on the diagonal, and both answers are correct as long as the order of $D$ matches the order of $\mathcal B$.

## Similar matrices

Nothing in the proof above used that $D$ is diagonal. For any invertible $P$, the matrix $C = P^{-1}AP$ is the matrix of $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ in the basis formed by the columns of $P$. Read the other way, every matrix of this map in some basis is similar to $A$.

The left edge goes up from [x]_B to x by P, the right edge goes down from Ax to [Ax]_B by P inverse, and the bottom edge sends [x]_B to [Ax]_B by D. Below it, one third of [1 1; −1 2] times [1 2; 1 0] times [2 −1; 1 1] equals [2 0; 0 −1]." />

So **similar** matrices are the same linear map written in different coordinates. The video shows three of them for one map: $A$ in the standard basis, the triangular $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ in Day 22's basis $\{(2, 1), (-1, 2)\}$, and $D$ in the eigenvector basis. Since they describe one map, they share everything that does not depend on coordinates, such as the eigenvalues $2$ and $-1$, the trace $1$ and the determinant $-2$.
</Pair>

Not every map has a diagonal matrix. The derivative $\tfrac{d}{dt}$ on $\mathbb P_2$ has only the constants as eigenvectors, so no basis of three eigenvectors exists. For $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -9 \\ 4 & -8 \end{bmatrix}$, whose only eigenvalue is $-2$ with a one-dimensional eigenspace, the best basis is Day 31's chain, and it gives the Jordan form $\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

## A map on polynomials

The same idea works in any vector space. Let $T: \mathbb P_1 \to \mathbb P_1$ be $T(a_0 + a_1t) = (a_0 + a_1) + 2a_1t$. In the basis $\{1, t\}$ its matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, which is triangular with eigenvalues $1$ and $2$. An eigenvector here is a polynomial that $T$ only rescales, so its graph and the graph of $T(p)$ cross the $t$-axis at the same point.

<EigenPolynomialHunt matrix={[[1, 1], [0, 2]]} />

The two eigenpolynomials are $1$ and $1 + t$, since $T(1) = 1$ and $T(1 + t) = 2 + 2t$. In the basis $\mathcal E = \{1,\ 1 + t\}$, the matrix of $T$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

## Powers and dynamical systems

In eigenvector coordinates, applying $A$ once multiplies coordinate $i$ by $\lambda_i$, so applying it $k$ times multiplies coordinate $i$ by $\lambda_i^k$. If $\mathbf x_0 = c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2$, then

$$
\mathbf x_k = A^k\mathbf x_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\,\mathbf v_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k\,\mathbf v_2, \qquad A^k = PD^kP^{-1}.
$$

Teal dots x0 through x4 zigzag across the yellow line through v1, joined by dashed segments, and a teal arrow points to x4 = (7, 5). Beside the plane, x0 = ¼v1 + v2, x1 = ½v1 − v2, x2 = v1 + v2, x3 = 2v1 − v2, x4 = 4v1 + v2, and A^k = PD^kP⁻¹." />

With the video's matrix and $\mathbf x_0 = \tfrac14\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + \blue{\mathbf v_2}$, the yellow part doubles and the blue part flips at every step. The points jump back and forth across the line through $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$, and because the yellow part keeps growing while the blue part stays the same size, the direction of $\mathbf x_k$ swings closer to that line each time. In the long run, the eigenvalue with the largest absolute value decides where the system heads.
</Pair>

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathcal B = \{(1, 1), (1, 0)\}$. Find $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ for $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ and enter its entries in reading order. (Modeled on Lay §5.4, Exercises 9–12.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 4, 0, 1]} labels={["top left", "top right", "bottom left", "bottom right"]} />

<Answer>
Form $AP$ and row reduce $[\,P \ \ AP\,]$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
AP &= \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ 4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \\
\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 & 4 \end{array}\right] &\sim \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

So $[T]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The first column is $(3, 0)$ because $(1, 1)$ is an eigenvector with $\lambda = 3$. The second vector $(1, 0)$ is not an eigenvector, so the matrix is not diagonal. Replacing it with the other eigenvector $(1, 2)$, which has $\lambda = 1$, gives $\operatorname{diag}(3, 1)$.
</Answer>
</Check>

With $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ from the video and $\mathbf x_0 = (1, 2)$, compute $A^5\mathbf x_0$ without multiplying matrices.

<VectorAnswer answer={[65, 31]} />

<Answer>
In the eigenvector basis, $\mathbf x_0 = \mathbf v_1 + \mathbf v_2$ with $\mathbf v_1 = (2, 1)$ for $\lambda = 2$ and $\mathbf v_2 = (-1, 1)$ for $\lambda = -1$. So

$$
A^5\mathbf x_0 = 2^5\,\mathbf v_1 + (-1)^5\,\mathbf v_2 = 32\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 65 \\ 31 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
</Answer>
</Check>
