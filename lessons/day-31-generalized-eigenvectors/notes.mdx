Day 28 found eigenvectors one line at a time, and Day 29 found every eigenvalue as a root of $\det(A - \lambda I)$. Day 30 showed that different eigenvalues give independent eigenvectors, but a repeated root gets no such promise. It raises a question those lessons left open: does a root counted twice always come with two independent eigenvectors? The shear $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ from Day 5 says no, and today builds what takes the missing eigenvector's place. The question matters now because Day 32 builds a basis out of eigenvectors, and a matrix that runs short of them needs a substitute before any such basis exists.

## Two multiplicities

To tell whether a matrix has enough eigenvectors, count each eigenvalue in two ways and compare the counts. The shear's characteristic polynomial is $\det(S - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)^2$, so $\lambda = 1$ is a double root. Yet $S - I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1, so its null space is only the line through $\green{\mathbf e_1}$. Every other direction gets tipped over, because the shear slides each point sideways by its height.

<Definition term="Algebraic and geometric multiplicity">
Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$. Its **algebraic multiplicity** is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial. Its **geometric multiplicity** is $\dim \operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda I)$, the dimension of its eigenspace. The geometric multiplicity is always at least 1 and at most the algebraic multiplicity (Lay §5.3, Theorem 7a). A matrix is **defective** when some eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity smaller than its algebraic multiplicity.
</Definition>

The geometric multiplicity is $n - \operatorname{rank}(A - \lambda I)$, so you find it by row reducing, never by reading exponents off the polynomial. Since eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent (Day 30), a defective matrix has too few independent eigenvectors to form a basis of $\mathbb R^n$. On Day 32 this count decides which matrices can be diagonalized. Being defective has nothing to do with being invertible: $S$ has determinant 1 and is defective, while $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is singular and not defective.

The matrices $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $2I$ share the characteristic polynomial $(\lambda - 2)^2$, but they are not similar. Every $P^{-1}(2I)P$ equals $2I$ again, so nothing else is similar to $2I$. The characteristic polynomial records the algebraic multiplicities only, and the eigenspace dimensions carry information it misses.

## Jordan chains

What we want here is a second vector that completes the basis and still belongs to the eigenvalue. When eigenvectors run short, apply $A - \lambda I$ to a vector that is not an eigenvector. For the shear, take $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (1, 2)$. The push from $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ to $S\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (3, 2)$ is $(S - I)\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (2, 0)$, which lies on the eigenline, and one more push sends it to $\mathbf 0$.

<Definition term="Generalized eigenvector and Jordan chain">
A nonzero $\mathbf v$ is a **generalized eigenvector** of $A$ for $\lambda$ if $(A - \lambda I)^k\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$ for some $k \ge 1$. A **Jordan chain** is a list $\mathbf v_k, \dots, \mathbf v_1$ with $\mathbf v_{j-1} = (A - \lambda I)\mathbf v_j$ and $\mathbf v_1 \ne \mathbf 0$, $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf 0$:

$$
\blue{\mathbf v_2} \xrightarrow{\;A - \lambda I\;} \yellow{\mathbf v_1} \xrightarrow{\;A - \lambda I\;} \mathbf 0.
$$
</Definition>
</Pair>

The bottom of every chain is an ordinary eigenvector. The vectors of a chain are linearly independent: applying $(A - \lambda I)^{k-1}$ to a dependence relation kills every term except the one with $\mathbf v_k$, so its weight is 0, and the same step repeats down the chain.

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix with a double eigenvalue $\lambda$, the matrix $N = A - \lambda I$ has trace 0 and determinant 0, and every $2 \times 2$ matrix satisfies $N^2 - (\operatorname{tr}N)N + (\det N)I = 0$. So $N^2 = 0$, and any $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ off the eigenline starts a chain of length 2. Here is the recipe with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A - \lambda I) &= (5 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) + 1 = (\lambda - 4)^2 \\
A - 4I &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \operatorname{rank} = 1 \\
\blue{\mathbf v_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad &\Rightarrow \quad \yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (A - 4I)\blue{\mathbf v_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \\
(A - 4I)\yellow{\mathbf v_1} &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 - 1 \\ -1 + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Both multiplicities show up: $\lambda = 4$ is a double root, and its eigenspace is the single line through $(1, -1)$. The direction of the chain matters. You start at the top with $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ and push down, because pushing an eigenvector gives $\mathbf 0$.

## Longer chains

The goal is still a basis, now built from chains whose lengths add up to each eigenvalue's algebraic multiplicity. In larger matrices a chain can need more pushes, and $\dim \operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda I)^k$ counts how many independent vectors $k$ pushes can finish. These dimensions grow with $k$ and stop exactly at the algebraic multiplicity. The null space where they stop is the **generalized eigenspace** of $\lambda$. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 0 \\ -3 & 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, whose characteristic polynomial is $-(\lambda - 2)^2(\lambda + 1)$, watch the dimension for $\lambda = 2$ climb.

<NullPowerClimb matrix={[[1, 1, 0], [-1, 3, 0], [-3, 3, -1]]} lambda={2} multiplicity={2} />

To build the chain, pick $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ in $\operatorname{Nul}(A - 2I)^2$ but outside $\operatorname{Nul}(A - 2I)$. Here $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (0, 1, 1)$ gives $(A - 2I)\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (1, 1, 0) = \yellow{\mathbf v_1}$, and $(0, 0, 1)$ is the eigenvector for $\lambda = -1$. Differentiation on polynomials of degree at most 2 is a chain of length 3, since $t^2 \to 2t \to 2 \to 0$, and there $\dim\operatorname{Nul}$ climbs $1, 2, 3$.

## Jordan form

The chains were built to serve as a basis, and in that basis the matrix of $A$ is as close to diagonal as $A$ allows. Unpacking the chain gives two equations about $A$:

$$
A\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = \lambda\yellow{\mathbf v_1}, \qquad A\blue{\mathbf v_2} = 1\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + \lambda\blue{\mathbf v_2}.
$$

With $P = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{\mathbf v_1} & \blue{\mathbf v_2} \end{bmatrix}$, Day 22 says column $j$ of $P^{-1}AP$ holds the coordinates of $A\mathbf v_j$ in the basis $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}, \blue{\mathbf v_2}$. Reading those coordinates off the two equations gives

$$
P^{-1}AP = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{\lambda} & \blue{1} \\ \yellow{0} & \blue{\lambda} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The 1 above the diagonal is the push: $A$ scales $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ by $\lambda$ and adds one copy of $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$. For the worked example above, $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ gives $P^{-1}AP = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Definition term="Jordan form (stated without proof)">
A **Jordan block** $J_k(\lambda)$ is the $k \times k$ matrix with $\lambda$ on the diagonal, 1 directly above it, and 0 everywhere else. If the characteristic polynomial of $A$ factors into linear factors, then $A$ is similar to a block diagonal matrix $J$ of Jordan blocks, its **Jordan form**. For each eigenvalue, the number of blocks equals the geometric multiplicity and the block sizes add up to the algebraic multiplicity. The columns of $P$ are Jordan chains, each listed from $\mathbf v_1$ up.
</Definition>

For the $3 \times 3$ matrix above, $P = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ lists $(0, 0, 1)$, then $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$, then $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$, and $P^{-1}AP = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has a block of size 1 and a block of size 2. Tomorrow covers the case with no chains at all, where every block has size 1 and $J$ is diagonal. Day 33 comes back to a Jordan chain as the best basis for a map that has too few eigenvectors.

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. It has a single eigenvalue $\lambda = 2$. Starting from $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = \mathbf e_1$, find $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (A - 2I)\blue{\mathbf v_2}$. (Modeled on Lay §5.3, Exercise 9.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-1, 1]} />

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A - \lambda I) &= (1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) + 1 = (\lambda - 2)^2 \\
A - 2I &= \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
\yellow{\mathbf v_1} &= (A - 2I)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: $A(-1, 1) = (-2, 2) = 2(-1, 1)$, so $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$ is an eigenvector. The rank of $A - 2I$ is 1, so the geometric multiplicity is 1 while the algebraic multiplicity is 2, and $A$ is defective.
</Answer>
</Check>

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Give the algebraic and geometric multiplicities of $\lambda = 2$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 2]} labels={["Algebraic", "Geometric"]} />

<Answer>
$A$ is triangular with 2 three times on the diagonal, so the algebraic multiplicity is 3. Then

$$
A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

has rank 1, so the eigenspace $\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2\}$ has dimension $3 - 1 = 2$. One chain $\mathbf e_3 \to \mathbf e_1 \to \mathbf 0$ and the lone eigenvector $\mathbf e_2$ give a Jordan form with blocks of sizes 2 and 1.
</Answer>
</Check>

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Starting from $\mathbf v_3 = \mathbf e_3$, find $\mathbf v_2 = (A - 2I)\mathbf v_3$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 1, 0]} />

$$

$$

One more push sends $\mathbf v_1$ to $\mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf e_3$ is a generalized eigenvector of rank 3 and the chain has length 3. The dimensions of $\operatorname{Nul}(A - 2I)^k$ are 1, 2, 3.
</Answer>
