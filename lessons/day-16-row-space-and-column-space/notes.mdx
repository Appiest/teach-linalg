On Day 4 you saw that $A\mathbf x$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, with the entries of $\mathbf x$ as weights. Today collects every possible output into one set, because that set decides which equations $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ have a solution. Days 14 and 15 gave you bases and dimension for any subspace, so now you can describe that set exactly. It is a subspace, it has a basis you can read straight off a row reduction, and a second subspace built from the rows comes along with it.

## Column space

<Definition term="Column space">
The **column space** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A = [\,\mathbf a_1 \ \cdots \ \mathbf a_n\,]$ is the set of all linear combinations of its columns:

$$
\operatorname{Col}A = \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf a_1, \dots, \mathbf a_n\} = \{\, A\mathbf x : \mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n \,\}.
$$

It is the **range** of the map $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$, and it is a subspace of $\mathbb R^m$, because every span is a subspace.
</Definition>

The column space lives in $\mathbb R^m$, where $m$ is the number of rows, because each column has $m$ entries. The inputs $\mathbf x$ live in $\mathbb R^n$ instead, so a $3 \times 2$ matrix takes the plane to space.

<Pair>
<Figure src="input-to-output" alt="Left: the input plane with a teal square of grid lines and the basis arrows e1 in green and e2 in red. Right: a 3D view where the same grid lines have landed on a tilted teal plane through the origin, with the columns a1 = (1, 0, 1) in green and a2 = (0, 1, 1) in red. The matrix A with rows (1, 0), (0, 1), (1, 1) sits above the input plane." />

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} \green{1} & \red{0} \\ \green{0} & \red{1} \\ \green{1} & \red{1} \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
A\mathbf x = x_1 \green{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + x_2 \red{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} = \teal{\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_1 + x_2 \end{bmatrix}}
$$

Every output has third entry equal to the sum of the first two, so $\operatorname{Col}A$ is the plane $z = x + y$ in $\mathbb R^3$.
</Pair>

<OutputReach columns={[[1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]]} target={[-1, 2, 1]} />

Any set written with free parameters is a column space in disguise. For example, $\{(a + 2b,\ -a,\ 3b) : a, b \in \mathbb R\}$ splits as $a(1, -1, 0) + b(2, 0, 3)$, so it equals $\operatorname{Col}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and is automatically a subspace.

## Testing a vector

This test answers a yes-or-no question about one vector, so the row reduction only needs to reach an echelon form that shows whether the system is consistent. A vector $\mathbf b$ is in $\operatorname{Col}A$ exactly when some $\mathbf x$ gives $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$, which means the system with augmented matrix $[\,A \ \ \mathbf b\,]$ is consistent. Row reduce it and look for a row of the form $[\,0 \ \cdots \ 0 \mid \text{nonzero}\,]$.

<Pair>
<Figure src="column-space" alt="The tilted plane Col A with columns a1 in green and a2 in red. The yellow point b = (1, −1, 0) lies on the plane. The yellow point c = (1, 1, 0) lies below it, with an orange dashed line rising from c up to the plane." />

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\,A \ \ \mathbf b\,] &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt]
[\,A \ \ \mathbf c\,] &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \glow{-2} \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The first system is consistent with $\mathbf x = (1, -1)$, so $\mathbf b \in \operatorname{Col}A$. The second ends in $0 = -2$, so no input reaches $\mathbf c$.
</Pair>

Row reducing $A$ alone answers a different question. If $A$ has a pivot in every row, then $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ is consistent for every $\mathbf b$, and $\operatorname{Col}A$ is all of $\mathbb R^m$. The $3 \times 2$ matrix above has only two pivots for three rows, so its column space is a plane and not all of $\mathbb R^3$.

## Basis for the column space

A matrix can have more columns than its column space needs, the way Day 14's spanning sets could. The goal here is to keep just enough columns of $A$ to span $\operatorname{Col}A$, with none to spare.

<Definition term="Pivot columns form a basis">
The pivot columns of $A$ form a basis for $\operatorname{Col}A$. Find the pivot positions from any echelon form, then take those columns **from $A$ itself**.
</Definition>

The reason is that $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ and $B\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ have the same solutions whenever $A$ row reduces to $B$. A solution $\mathbf x$ is a list of weights that combine the columns to zero, so the columns of $A$ satisfy exactly the same dependence relations as the columns of $B$. In the reduced form each non-pivot column visibly equals a combination of the pivot columns, and the pivot columns are independent, so the same is true in $A$.

<Pair>
<Figure src="pivot-columns" alt="The matrix A with columns (1, 0, 1), (0, 1, 1) and (−1, 1, 0) beside its reduced form with columns (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0) and (−1, 1, 0). Orange frames mark columns 1 and 2 of A; dashed frames mark the same columns of the reduced form. Below, the relation a3 = −a1 + a2." />

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{reduced: } \ \mathbf b_3 &= -\mathbf b_1 + \mathbf b_2 \\
\text{so in } A\text{: } \ \pink{\mathbf a_3} &= -\green{\mathbf a_1} + \red{\mathbf a_2} \\
&= -\green{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + \red{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} = \pink{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}
\end{aligned}
$$

The columns of the reduced form all end in $0$, so they lie on the floor $z = 0$ and are not even in $\operatorname{Col}A$. That is why the basis vectors must come from $A$.
</Pair>

The number of pivot columns is therefore $\dim\operatorname{Col}A$, and Day 18 names that number the rank of $A$.

## Row space

<Definition term="Row space">
The **row space** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, written $\operatorname{Row}A$, is the span of its rows, read as vectors in $\mathbb R^n$. It equals $\operatorname{Col}A^T$. If $A$ row reduces to $B$, then $\operatorname{Row}A = \operatorname{Row}B$, and when $B$ is in echelon form its nonzero rows are a basis for $\operatorname{Row}A$.
</Definition>

On Day 18 the row space turns out to have the same dimension as the column space. Each row operation replaces a row by a combination of rows, so every new row stays inside the old row space. Every row operation can also be undone by another one, so the old rows are combinations of the new ones, and the span cannot shrink either. In an echelon form, each nonzero row has its leading entry in a column where every row below it has a zero, so no nonzero row is a combination of the others, and they are independent.

<Pair>
<Figure src="row-space" alt="A tilted teal plane labelled Row B with the green row r1 = (1, 0, 1). A faded red arrow shows the old row r2 = (1, 1, 2), and an orange dashed path inside the plane leads to the bright red arrow r2 − r1 = (0, 1, 1)." />

$$
B = \begin{bmatrix} \green{1} & \green{0} & \green{1} \\ \red{1} & \red{1} & \red{2} \end{bmatrix}
\ \xrightarrow{\ R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - R_1\ } \
\begin{bmatrix} \green{1} & \green{0} & \green{1} \\ \red{0} & \red{1} & \red{1} \end{bmatrix}
$$

The red row moves, but it slides within the same plane, so $\operatorname{Row}B$ does not change.
</Pair>

<RowSlide rows={[[1, 0, 1], [2, 1, 3]]} />

Row operations keep the dependence relations among the columns, but they change the relations among the rows. That is why the rows of the basis come from the echelon form, while the columns come from $A$. For example,

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

has an echelon form with two nonzero rows, yet the first two rows of $A$ are dependent because row 2 is twice row 1.

## Practice

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf b = (3, 1, 5)$. Find $\mathbf x$ with $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$, which shows that $\mathbf b$ is in $\operatorname{Col}A$. (Modeled on Lay §2.8, Example 4.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 1]} labels={["x₁", "x₂"]} />

<Answer>
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}
\sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}
\sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The system is consistent with $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 1$, so $\mathbf b = \mathbf a_1 + \mathbf a_2$. By contrast, $[\,A \ \ (0, 0, 1)\,]$ reduces to a last row $[\,0 \ \ 0 \mid 1\,]$, so $(0, 0, 1)$ is not in $\operatorname{Col}A$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$. Which two columns of $A$ are its pivot columns? Enter their positions. (Modeled on Lay §4.6, Exercise 1.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 3]} labels={["first pivot column", "second pivot column"]} />

<Answer>
$$
A \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The pivots sit in columns 1 and 3. A basis for $\operatorname{Col}A$ is $\{(1, 2, 3),\ (0, 1, 1)\}$, taken from $A$. A basis for $\operatorname{Row}A$ is $\{(1, 2, 0, 3),\ (0, 0, 1, -2)\}$, taken from the echelon form. Here row 3 of $A$ equals row 1 plus row 2, so rows 1 and 2 of $A$ would also work, but that is luck rather than a rule.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
The set $W = \{(a + 2b,\ -a,\ 3b) : a, b \in \mathbb R\}$ is $\operatorname{Col}A$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Find $a$ and $b$ that produce $(6, -2, 6)$. (Modeled on Lay §4.2, Example 4.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[2, 2]} labels={["a", "b"]} />

<Answer>
The second entry gives $-a = -2$, so $a = 2$. The third gives $3b = 6$, so $b = 2$. Then the first entry is $a + 2b = 6$, which matches, so $(6, -2, 6) \in W$. The same steps show that $(4, -2, 6)$ is not in $W$, because $a = 2$ and $b = 2$ force a first entry of $6$ instead of $4$.
</Answer>
</Check>
