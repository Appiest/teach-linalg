On Day 3 you used three row operations to solve systems, and yesterday you met $A^{-1}$, the matrix that undoes $A$. Yesterday's formula only works for $2 \times 2$ matrices, so today asks how to find the inverse of a matrix of any size. The answer connects those two ideas: each row operation is itself a matrix, and running row reduction on $A$ and $I$ side by side computes $A^{-1}$.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. You will need Day 6's row rule, $\text{row}_i(EA) = \text{row}_i(E)\,A$, more than once.

## Elementary matrices

<Problem>
Start with the identity $I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and do the row operation "subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2". Call the result $E$. Then compute $EA$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ and enter it row by row. How does it compare with doing the same row operation to $A$ directly?

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 3, 0, 1]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
Row 1 of $I$ stays $(1, 0)$. Row 2 becomes $(0, 1) - 2(1, 0) = (-2, 1)$. So

$$
E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \glow{-2} & \glow{1} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

To compute $EA$ we use Day 6's row rule, which says each row of $EA$ is a row of $E$ times $A$. A row times $A$ is a combination of the rows of $A$, with the row's entries as weights.

<Steps>
<Step>
Row 1 of $E$ is $(1, 0)$, so row 1 of $EA$ is $1\cdot(1, 3) + 0\cdot(2, 7) = (1, 3)$. Row 1 of $A$ is unchanged.
</Step>
<Step>
Row 2 of $E$ is $(-2, 1)$, so row 2 of $EA$ is $-2\cdot(1, 3) + 1\cdot(2, 7) = (-2 + 2,\; -6 + 7) = (0, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
So $EA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Doing "subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2" to $A$ directly gives the same matrix.
</Step>
</Steps>

Row 2 of $E$ is a recipe that says "take $-2$ of row 1 and $1$ of row 2". Multiplying by $E$ follows that recipe on whatever matrix sits to its right.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Elementary matrix">
An **elementary matrix** is a matrix you get by doing one elementary row operation to an identity matrix. There is one kind for each row operation: replacement, interchange and scaling.

If you do a row operation to an $m \times n$ matrix $A$, the result is $EA$, where $E$ is the $m \times m$ elementary matrix made by doing the same operation to $I_m$.
</Definition>

Writing a row operation as a matrix turns row reduction into ordinary matrix algebra. Day 9 uses that to factor $A = LU$, and Day 26 uses it to track how each row operation changes a determinant.

Try writing a recipe yourself. The picture draws each row of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ as an arrow, and row 2 of $E$ says how much of each to mix.

## Undoing a row operation

<Problem>
Which row operation undoes "subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2"? Write its elementary matrix $F$, enter it row by row, and check that $FE = I$ for the $E$ of Problem 1.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 0, 2, 1]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
Subtracting $2R_1$ from row 2 is undone by adding $2R_1$ back. Doing "add 2 times row 1 to row 2" to $I$ gives

$$
F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Now check with the row rule. Row 1 of $FE$ is row 1 of $E$, which is $(1, 0)$. Row 2 of $FE$ is $2\cdot(1, 0) + 1\cdot(-2, 1) = (0, 1)$. So $FE = I$, and in the same way $EF = I$. That makes $F = E^{-1}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Every elementary matrix is invertible">
Every row operation can be undone by another row operation of the same kind, so every elementary matrix has an inverse that is elementary too. You can write it down without any computation.

- A replacement is undone by flipping the sign of the multiple: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
- A swap undoes itself.
- Scaling a row by $c$ is undone by scaling it by $\tfrac1c$. That is why scaling by $0$ is not a row operation.
</Concept>

<Choice
  question="What is the inverse of the elementary matrix for $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - 4R_1$?"
  options={["The matrix for $R_3 \\leftarrow R_3 + 4R_1$", "The matrix for $R_3 \\leftarrow R_3 - \\tfrac14 R_1$", "The matrix for $R_3 \\leftarrow R_3 + \\tfrac14 R_1$", "It has no inverse"]}
  answer={0}
>
Taking away $4R_1$ is undone by putting $4R_1$ back. Only a scaling uses a reciprocal. In the matrix, the $-4$ in row 3, column 1 becomes $+4$.
</Choice>

## Inverting by row reduction

On Day 3, row reduction aimed for reduced echelon form so you could read off the solution. For an invertible square matrix that form is $I$. Here the goal is to reduce $A$ all the way to $I$ while keeping a record of the steps, because their product turns out to be $A^{-1}$.

<Problem>
Reduce $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ to $I$ with two row replacements. Write the elementary matrix for each step, and multiply them together, putting the later step on the left. Enter the product row by row.

<VectorAnswer answer={[7, -3, -2, 1]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
Problem 1 already did the first step. $E_1$ is "subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2", and it turned $A$ into $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

To finish, we clear the $3$ above the second pivot. $E_2$ is "subtract 3 times row 2 from row 1", which gives row 1 as $(1, 3) - 3(0, 1) = (1, 0)$ and leaves $I$. Its matrix is $I$ with that step done to it:

$$
E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

$E_1$ acts first, so the product is $E_2E_1$. By the row rule, row 1 is $1\cdot(1, 0) - 3\cdot(-2, 1) = (7, -3)$, and row 2 is $0\cdot(1, 0) + 1\cdot(-2, 1) = (-2, 1)$:

$$
E_2E_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Day 7's formula gives $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{7 - 6}\begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, the same matrix. The product of the steps that reduce $A$ to $I$ is $A^{-1}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Theorem 7">
An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is invertible if and only if it is row equivalent to $I_n$. In that case, any sequence of row operations that reduces $A$ to $I_n$ also turns $I_n$ into $A^{-1}$.
</Definition>

The reason is the same as in Problem 3. If $E_p \cdots E_1 A = I$, multiplying both sides on the right by $A^{-1}$ gives $E_p \cdots E_1 = A^{-1}$. To collect that product without multiplying matrices, write $I$ next to $A$ and do every operation to the whole row of $[\,A \;\; I\,]$. When the left half reaches $I$, the right half is $E_p \cdots E_1 I = A^{-1}$.

Here is the example from the video, with $A = \begin{bmatrix} \green{2} & \red{1} \\ \green{1} & \red{1} \end{bmatrix}$. Each step says which entry it is trying to fix.

<Steps>
<Step>
We want a $1$ in the top-left corner, and row 2 already starts with $1$, so swap the rows ($E_1$: $R_1 \leftrightarrow R_2$).

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} \green{2} & \red{1} & 1 & 0 \\ \green{1} & \red{1} & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} \green{1} & \red{1} & 0 & 1 \\ \green{2} & \red{1} & 1 & 0 \end{array}\right]
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Clear the $2$ below the pivot by subtracting 2 times row 1 from row 2 ($E_2$). Row 2 becomes $(2, 1, 1, 0) - 2(1, 1, 0, 1) = (0, -1, 1, -2)$.

$$
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} \green{1} & \red{1} & 0 & 1 \\ \green{0} & \red{-1} & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right]
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Turn the $-1$ into a pivot of $1$ by scaling row 2 by $-1$ ($E_3$).

$$
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} \green{1} & \red{1} & 0 & 1 \\ \green{0} & \red{1} & -1 & 2 \end{array}\right]
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Clear the $1$ above the second pivot by subtracting row 2 from row 1 ($E_4$). Row 1 becomes $(1, 1, 0, 1) - (0, 1, -1, 2) = (1, 0, 1, -1)$.

$$
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} \green{1} & \red{0} & \teal{1} & \teal{-1} \\ \green{0} & \red{1} & \teal{-1} & \teal{2} \end{array}\right]
$$
</Step>
<Step>
The left half is $I$, so the teal block is $A^{-1}$. Check with one multiplication: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\teal{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = I$.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Products of elementary matrices

The reduction steps take $A$ to $I$. Running them backwards, last one first, should take $I$ back to $A$.

<Problem>
In Problem 3, $E_2E_1A = I$. Write $A$ as a product of two elementary matrices, and check your answer by multiplying.

<Solution>
We want $A$ alone, so we remove $E_2$ first, because it sits farthest to the left. Multiply both sides on the left by $E_2^{-1}$, then by $E_1^{-1}$:

$$
E_1A = E_2^{-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = E_1^{-1}E_2^{-1}.
$$

Each inverse flips the sign of its multiple, so

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3\cdot 2 + 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The order is the reverse of the reduction, with $E_1^{-1}$ on the left. It matches Day 7's socks and shoes rule.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="product-steps" alt="Five small grids from left to right: the square grid I, then the grid after E4 inverse, after E3 inverse E4 inverse, after E2 inverse E3 inverse E4 inverse, and finally the slanted grid of A = E1 inverse E2 inverse E3 inverse E4 inverse." wide />

<Concept title="Invertible means a product of elementary matrices">
If $E_p \cdots E_1A = I$, then $A = E_1^{-1}E_2^{-1}\cdots E_p^{-1}$, and every factor is elementary. In the other direction, a product of elementary matrices is always invertible, because each factor is. So **$A$ is invertible exactly when it is a product of elementary matrices.** Geometrically, every invertible move of the plane is a chain of shears, swaps and stretches. Day 27 uses this fact to prove that $\det(AB) = (\det A)(\det B)$.
</Concept>

## When there is no inverse

<Problem>
Try to row reduce $[\,B \;\; I\,]$ for $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. What goes wrong?

<Solution>
The natural first step clears the $1$ below the first pivot by subtracting row 1 from row 2:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right]
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|rr} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right]
$$

The left half now has a row of zeros. Every later row operation only mixes the rows $(1, 2)$ and $(0, 0)$, so every row it can produce is a multiple of $(1, 2)$. The row $(0, 1)$ of $I$ is not such a multiple, so the left half can never become $I$. By Theorem 7, $B$ has no inverse, and we can stop here. Its determinant $1 \cdot 2 - 2 \cdot 1 = 0$ agrees.
</Solution>
</Problem>

A zero row on the left is the signal to stop. Geometrically, $B$ flattens the plane onto a line, and no sequence of invertible moves can unflatten it.

## One column of the inverse

Sometimes you need only part of $A^{-1}$. Because $AA^{-1} = I$, column $j$ of $A^{-1}$ is the vector that $A$ sends to $\mathbf e_j$, which is exactly what Day 7 found with the pink columns.

<Problem>
For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$, find the second column of $A^{-1}$ by row reducing $[\,A \;\; \mathbf e_2\,]$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[-3, 1]} />

<Solution>
The second column solves $A\mathbf x = \mathbf e_2 = (0, 1)$, so we row reduce $A$ with only that one column beside it, using the same two steps as Problem 3:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 7 & 1 \end{array}\right]
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{array}\right]
\sim \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 1 & 0 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{array}\right]
$$

The second column of $A^{-1}$ is $(-3, 1)$, which matches the second column of the $A^{-1}$ found in Problem 3.
</Solution>
</Problem>

Tomorrow the same record of steps, stopped at an echelon form instead of at $I$, gives the factorization $A = LU$, which solves $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ quickly for many different $\mathbf b$.

>
The only step needed is "scale row 1 by $\tfrac12$". Applied to $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac12 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, it gives $\begin{bmatrix} \tfrac14 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, not $I$. The steps multiply out to $A^{-1}$, so applying them to $A^{-1}$ gives $A^{-1}A^{-1}$, which is $I$ only in special cases.
</Choice>

<Hint>
Multiply out what the steps do. If $E_p \cdots E_1 = A^{-1}$, what is $E_p \cdots E_1 A^{-1}$?
</Hint>

<Answer>
$E_p \cdots E_1 A^{-1} = A^{-1}A^{-1}$. To reduce $A^{-1}$ to $I$ you need steps whose product is $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$ instead.
</Answer>
