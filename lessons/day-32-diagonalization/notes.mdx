On Day 28 the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ kept two lines, doubling the yellow one through $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$ and leaving the blue one through $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (1, 1)$ alone. Yesterday's shear could not supply two independent eigenvectors, and today asks what a matrix gains when it can. Those two eigenvectors become the axes of a new grid. On that grid $A$ does nothing except stretch each axis, and that picture turns into a factorization $A = PDP^{-1}$ that makes $A$ easy to understand and easy to raise to powers.

## The eigenbasis

Because $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$ and $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$ are independent, every $\mathbf x$ in $\mathbb R^2$ has one address $\mathbf x = c_1\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + c_2\blue{\mathbf v_2}$. Applying $A$ and using linearity gives

$$
A\mathbf x = c_1 A\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + c_2 A\blue{\mathbf v_2} = \glow{2}\,c_1\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + \glow{1}\,c_2\blue{\mathbf v_2}.
$$

In these eigen-coordinates $A$ multiplies the first coordinate by $2$ and the second by $1$. No coordinate leaks into the other, so $A$ acts like the diagonal matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

A basis of $\mathbb R^n$ made of eigenvectors of $A$ is called an **eigenvector basis**, or eigenbasis. The picture shows why it is the right grid for $A$. Every yellow step doubles and every blue step stays the same length, so each grid cell stretches along one axis and nothing turns.

## The diagonalization theorem

The goal here is to rewrite the picture above as matrix algebra, so that the stretch in eigen-coordinates becomes a single diagonal factor. Put the eigenvectors into the columns of $P = [\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1}\ \blue{\mathbf v_2}\,]$ and the eigenvalues, in the same order, on the diagonal of $D$. Multiplying $A$ into $P$ one column at a time gives

$$
AP = [\,A\yellow{\mathbf v_1}\ \ A\blue{\mathbf v_2}\,] = [\,\glow{2}\yellow{\mathbf v_1}\ \ \glow{1}\blue{\mathbf v_2}\,] = [\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1}\ \ \blue{\mathbf v_2}\,]\begin{bmatrix} \glow{2} & 0 \\ 0 & \glow{1} \end{bmatrix} = PD.
$$

The columns of $P$ are independent, so $P$ is invertible, and multiplying $AP = PD$ on the right by $P^{-1}$ gives $A = PDP^{-1}$. Read right to left, the factorization is three moves: $P^{-1}$ converts $\mathbf x$ into eigen-coordinates, $D$ stretches each coordinate, and $P$ converts back.

<Definition term="The diagonalization theorem">
A square matrix $A$ is **diagonalizable** if $A = PDP^{-1}$ for some invertible $P$ and diagonal $D$. An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. In that case the columns of $P$ are those eigenvectors, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are their eigenvalues in the same order.
</Definition>

The argument above runs in both directions. If $AP = PD$ with $D$ diagonal, column $j$ says $A\mathbf p_j = d_j\mathbf p_j$, so the columns of any such $P$ must be eigenvectors.

Not every matrix has enough eigenvectors. The shear $S$ from Day 31 has eigenvalue $1$ twice, but its only eigenvectors lie on the $x$-axis. One line cannot supply two independent columns for $P$, so $S$ is not diagonalizable.

Distinct eigenvalues always avoid this problem. Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent (Day 30), so an $n \times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable. The converse fails: $2I$ has only one eigenvalue and is already diagonal.
</Pair>

## Diagonalizing step by step

For a repeated eigenvalue, count: $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when the eigenspace dimensions add up to $n$. The recipe aims for $n$ independent eigenvectors to fill the columns of $P$, and it can stop early when the count comes up short. Here is the full recipe with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the eigenvalues, find a basis for each eigenspace, and build $P$ and $D$ in matching order:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A - \lambda I) &= (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2) \\
A - 5I &= \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \yellow{\mathbf v_1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
A - 2I &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \blue{\mathbf v_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} \\
P &= \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{1} & \blue{1} \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{-2} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{bmatrix} \glow{5} & 0 \\ 0 & \glow{2} \end{bmatrix} \\
AP &= \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = PD.
\end{aligned}
$$

Checking $AP = PD$ avoids computing $P^{-1}$. It proves nothing unless $P$ is invertible, and here $\det P = -3 \ne 0$.

## Powers of A

Multiplying $A$ by itself ten times is slow, and the entries of $A^k$ are hard to predict by looking at $A$. The factorization fixes both problems. Squaring $A = PDP^{-1}$ puts $P^{-1}P = I$ in the middle, and the same cancellation happens for every power:

$$
A^2 = PD(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1} = PD^2P^{-1}, \qquad A^k = PD^kP^{-1}.
$$

$$
D^k = \begin{bmatrix} \glow{d_1^k} & 0 \\ 0 & \glow{d_2^k} \end{bmatrix}
$$

Only $D$ takes the power, and a diagonal matrix raises each diagonal entry separately. For the video's matrix, $P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, so

$$
A^k = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2^k & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2^{k+1} - 1 & 2 - 2^{k+1} \\ 2^k - 1 & 2 - 2^k \end{bmatrix}.
$$

At $k = 1$ this gives back $A$, and at $k = 10$ it gives $\begin{bmatrix} 2047 & -2046 \\ 1023 & -1022 \end{bmatrix}$ without nine separate multiplications. Tomorrow reads $D$ as the matrix of the same map in the eigenvector basis, and uses $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ to track where $A^k\mathbf x$ heads.
