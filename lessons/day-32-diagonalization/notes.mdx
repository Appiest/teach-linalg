On Day 28 the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ kept two lines, doubling the yellow one through $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$ and leaving the blue one through $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (1, 1)$ alone. Yesterday's shear could not supply two independent eigenvectors, and today asks what a matrix gains when it can. The answer is a factorization $A = PDP^{-1}$ with a diagonal matrix in the middle. It makes $A$ easy to understand and easy to raise to powers, which is what Day 33 needs to predict where $A^k\mathbf x$ heads.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The box after each walkthrough names the idea you just used.

## Diagonal matrices

We start with the easiest matrices of all, because the whole lesson is about turning other matrices into these.

<Problem>
Let $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $D\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$, $D^2$ and $D^3$. Then guess $D^k$.

<Solution>
$D$ has zeros off the diagonal, so each entry of the output uses only one entry of the input: $D\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot 4 + 0 \cdot 5 \\ 0 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 15 \end{bmatrix}$. The first entry is doubled and the second is tripled.

Squaring uses the row-times-column rule, and every off-diagonal product has a zero in it:

$$
D^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 0 & 2 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 3 \\ 0 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 0 & 0 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D^3 = D^2 D = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 0 \\ 0 & 27 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Each diagonal entry is raised to the power on its own, so $D^k = \begin{bmatrix} 2^k & 0 \\ 0 & 3^k \end{bmatrix}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

A diagonal matrix scales each axis by its own number and never mixes the axes. Most matrices do mix them, like $A$ above. The plan for today is to find a different set of axes on which $A$ does no mixing.

## The eigenbasis

<Problem>
Keep $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with $A\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = 2\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$ and $A\blue{\mathbf v_2} = 1\blue{\mathbf v_2}$. Write $\mathbf u = (0, 1)$ as $c_1\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + c_2\blue{\mathbf v_2}$. Then compute $A\mathbf u$ two ways, directly and by using the weights.

<Solution>
First we want the weights, because the eigenvectors are the vectors whose behavior we already know.

<Steps>
<Step>
Match entries in $c_1(2, 1) + c_2(1, 1) = (0, 1)$. That gives $2c_1 + c_2 = 0$ and $c_1 + c_2 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step>
Subtract the second equation from the first to get $c_1 = -1$. Then $c_2 = 1 - c_1 = 2$. So $\mathbf u = -1\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + 2\,\blue{\mathbf v_2}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Directly, $A\mathbf u = (3 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 1,\ 1 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1) = (-2, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Using the weights and linearity, $A\mathbf u = -1\,A\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + 2\,A\blue{\mathbf v_2} = -1 \cdot \glow{2}\,\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + 2 \cdot \glow{1}\,\blue{\mathbf v_2}$. That is $(-4, -2) + (2, 2) = (-2, 0)$, the same answer.
</Step>
</Steps>

In the weights, $A$ turned $(-1, 2)$ into $(-2, 2)$. It multiplied the first weight by $2$ and the second by $1$, which is exactly what the diagonal matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ does.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="eigen-grid-stretch" alt="Two copies of the slanted eigen-grid of v1 = (2, 1) and v2 = (1, 1) with one shaded cell. After A acts, the yellow arrow reaches (4, 2), the blue arrow is unchanged, and the shaded cell is twice as long along the yellow line." />

<Concept title="Eigenvectors make the right grid">
A basis of $\mathbb R^n$ made of eigenvectors of $A$ is an **eigenvector basis**, or eigenbasis. Measured in it, $A$ multiplies each weight by its eigenvalue and nothing else:

$$
A(c_1\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + c_2\blue{\mathbf v_2}) = \glow{\lambda_1}c_1\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + \glow{\lambda_2}c_2\blue{\mathbf v_2}.
$$

On the slanted grid, every yellow step doubles and every blue step stays the same, so each cell stretches along one axis and nothing turns.
</Concept>
</Pair>

Use the weights to aim. The readout shows $\mathbf x$ and $A\mathbf x$ in eigen-coordinates, so you can work out which $\mathbf x$ lands on the ring.

## The diagonalization theorem

The next step writes the weight picture as matrix algebra, so the stretch becomes a single diagonal factor.

<Problem>
Put the eigenvectors into the columns of $P = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{2} & \blue{1} \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{1} \end{bmatrix}$ and the eigenvalues, in the same order, into $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $AP$ and $PD$ and compare them.

<Solution>
A matrix product can be computed one column at a time: column $j$ of $AP$ is $A$ times column $j$ of $P$. That is convenient here, because the columns of $P$ are eigenvectors.

$$
AP = \begin{bmatrix} A\yellow{\mathbf v_1} & A\blue{\mathbf v_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Column $j$ of $PD$ is $P$ times column $j$ of $D$. Column 1 of $D$ is $(2, 0)$, which picks $2$ copies of $\yellow{\mathbf v_1}$, and column 2 is $(0, 1)$, which picks one copy of $\blue{\mathbf v_2}$:

$$
PD = \begin{bmatrix} 2\yellow{\mathbf v_1} & 1\blue{\mathbf v_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

They match, because each column says the same thing as $A\mathbf v_j = \lambda_j\mathbf v_j$. The columns of $P$ are independent, so $P$ is invertible. Multiplying $AP = PD$ on the right by $P^{-1}$ gives $A = PDP^{-1}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="The diagonalization theorem">
A square matrix $A$ is **diagonalizable** if $A = PDP^{-1}$ for some invertible $P$ and diagonal $D$. An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. In that case the columns of $P$ are those eigenvectors, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are their eigenvalues in the same order.
</Definition>

The argument runs in both directions. If $AP = PD$ with $D$ diagonal, column $j$ says $A\mathbf p_j = d_j\mathbf p_j$, so the columns of any such $P$ must be eigenvectors. Try building $P$ yourself.

Read right to left, $A = PDP^{-1}$ is three moves. $P^{-1}$ converts $\mathbf x$ into eigen-coordinates, $D$ stretches each coordinate, and $P$ converts back. The figure shows the three moves carrying $\mathbf u = (0, 1)$ from the problem above to $A\mathbf u = (-2, 0)$.

## Too few eigenvectors

<Problem>
Decide whether each matrix is diagonalizable: the shear $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
By the theorem, the question for each matrix is whether it has two independent eigenvectors.

On Day 31 the shear had the double eigenvalue $1$, and $S - I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1, so every eigenvector lies on the $x$-axis. One line cannot supply two independent columns for $P$, so $S$ is not diagonalizable.

$T$ is triangular, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries $2$ and $-1$. They are different, and eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent (Day 30). One eigenvector from each gives two independent columns, so $T$ is diagonalizable, and we did not need to find a single eigenvector to know it.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="enough-eigenvectors" alt="Left: the matrix A = [3 −2; 1 0] with its yellow and blue eigen-lines and the slanted eigen-grid they make. Right: the shear S = [1 1; 0 1] with a single yellow eigen-line along the x-axis. A gray arrow (0, 2) is carried to the teal arrow (2, 2), off its dashed line." />

<Concept title="Count the eigenvectors">
An $n \times n$ matrix with $n$ different eigenvalues is always diagonalizable. With a repeated eigenvalue, add up the eigenspace dimensions. $A$ is diagonalizable exactly when that sum is $n$, which happens when every eigenvalue's geometric multiplicity equals its algebraic multiplicity (Lay §5.3, Theorem 7).
</Concept>
</Pair>

The first test is only a shortcut. A repeated eigenvalue does not rule out diagonalizing, since $2I$ has only the eigenvalue $2$ and is already diagonal.

<Choice
  question="Which matrix is diagonalizable?"
  options={["$\\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 4 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 5 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\\\ 0 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$"]}
  answer={1}
>
The middle matrix is triangular with the different eigenvalues $4$ and $5$, so it has two independent eigenvectors. The other two each have a double eigenvalue whose shifted matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1, so each has only one eigenline.
</Choice>

<Warning>
Diagonalizable and invertible are separate questions. The shear is invertible and not diagonalizable. The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has the different eigenvalues $1$ and $0$, so it is diagonalizable, and its determinant is $0$, so it is not invertible.
</Warning>

## Diagonalizing step by step

Now we can run the whole recipe. Its goal is $n$ independent eigenvectors to fill the columns of $P$, and it can stop early if an eigenspace comes up short.

<Problem>
Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Find the eigenvalues, since each one tells us which null space to search. $\det(A - \lambda I) = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$, so $\lambda = 5$ and $\lambda = 2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Find an eigenvector for $5$. $A - 5I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, and its first row says $-x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (1, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Find an eigenvector for $2$. $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and its first row says $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (1, -2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Build $P$ and $D$ in matching order: $P = \begin{bmatrix} \yellow{1} & \blue{1} \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{-2} \end{bmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{bmatrix} \glow{5} & 0 \\ 0 & \glow{2} \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Check with $AP = PD$ instead of computing $P^{-1}$. $AP = \begin{bmatrix} 4 + 1 & 4 - 2 \\ 2 + 3 & 2 - 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$, and $PD = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \cdot 1 & 2 \cdot 1 \\ 5 \cdot 1 & 2 \cdot (-2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 5 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$. Finally $\det P = -2 - 1 = -3 \ne 0$, so $P$ is invertible.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

The order of the columns in $P$ decides the order of the entries in $D$. Here $P$ lists $(1, -2)$ first, so find the diagonal of $D$ that makes $PD$ match $AP$.

<DiagonalEntryMatch matrix={[[4, 1], [2, 3]]} first={[1, -2]} second={[1, 1]} />

<Warning>
$AP = PD$ holds for any eigenvectors, even two copies of the same one. It proves $A = PDP^{-1}$ only when $P$ is invertible, so always check $\det P \ne 0$ as well.
</Warning>

A repeated eigenvalue can still work, as long as its eigenspace is big enough.

<Problem>
Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$, with $3$ repeated.

<Solution>
The risk is the repeated eigenvalue, so count its eigenspace first.

<Steps>
<Step>
$A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has one pivot, so its null space has dimension $3 - 1 = 2$. That equals the multiplicity of $3$, so the count will reach $3$.
</Step>
<Step>
The single equation $-2x_1 + 2x_2 + 2x_3 = 0$ says $x_1 = x_2 + x_3$, with $x_2$ and $x_3$ free. Setting one free variable to 1 and the other to 0 gives $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 0, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $\lambda = 1$, $A - I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ forces $x_2 = x_3 = 0$ with $x_1$ free, so the eigenvector is $(1, 0, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
$P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Both $AP$ and $PD$ equal $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\det P = 1$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

## Powers of A

Multiplying $A$ by itself ten times is slow, and the entries of $A^k$ are hard to predict by looking at $A$. The factorization fixes both problems.

<Problem>
Use $A = PDP^{-1}$ to write $A^2$ with only one $P$ and one $P^{-1}$. Then find a formula for $A^k$ when $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Write the product out: $A^2 = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD(P^{-1}P)DP^{-1}$. The middle pair $P^{-1}P$ is $I$, so $A^2 = PD^2P^{-1}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Each extra factor of $A$ brings another $P^{-1}P$ pair that cancels, so $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$. Only $D$ takes the power, and Problem 1 showed how easy that is.
</Step>
<Step>
Find $P^{-1}$ with the $2 \times 2$ formula. $\det P = 2 - 1 = 1$, so $P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Multiply $PD^k$ first: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2^k & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \cdot 2^k & 1 \\ 2^k & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Then multiply by $P^{-1}$: $\begin{bmatrix} 2^{k+1} & 1 \\ 2^k & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2^{k+1} - 1 & 2 - 2^{k+1} \\ 2^k - 1 & 2 - 2^k \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

Check at $k = 1$: the formula gives $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, which is $A$. At $k = 10$ it gives $\begin{bmatrix} 2047 & -2046 \\ 1023 & -1022 \end{bmatrix}$ without nine separate multiplications.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Only the middle factor takes the power">
If $A = PDP^{-1}$, then

$$
A^k = PD^kP^{-1}, \qquad D^k = \begin{bmatrix} \glow{d_1^k} & 0 \\ 0 & \glow{d_2^k} \end{bmatrix}.
$$

In eigen-coordinates, $k$ applications of $A$ multiply each weight by its eigenvalue $k$ times.
</Concept>

<Choice
  question="If $A = PDP^{-1}$, which expression equals $A^3$?"
  options={["$P^3D^3P^{-3}$", "$PD^3P^{-1}$", "$PD^3P$", "$P^{-1}D^3P$"]}
  answer={1}
>
Writing out $(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})$, the two inner $P^{-1}P$ pairs cancel. One $P$ stays on the left and one $P^{-1}$ stays on the right, so $A^3 = PD^3P^{-1}$.
</Choice>

Tomorrow reads $D$ as the matrix of the same map in the eigenvector basis, and uses $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$ to track where $A^k\mathbf x$ heads.

Write $A = PDP^{-1}$. Since $A$ is invertible, $0$ is not an eigenvalue, so every diagonal entry of $D$ is nonzero. Then $D^{-1}$ exists and is the diagonal matrix of reciprocals $1/d_j$.

The inverse of a product is the product of inverses in reverse order:

$$
A^{-1} = (PDP^{-1})^{-1} = (P^{-1})^{-1}D^{-1}P^{-1} = PD^{-1}P^{-1}.
$$

That is a diagonalization of $A^{-1}$ with the same $P$. So $A^{-1}$ has the same eigenvectors as $A$, with eigenvalues $1/d_j$.
