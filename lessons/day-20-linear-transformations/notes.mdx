On Day 5 a matrix moved the plane and kept sums and multiples. Day 10 showed that polynomials, functions and matrices add and scale just like arrows. Today puts the two together: a linear transformation can go from any vector space to any other, as long as it respects adding and scaling. Differentiation is the first one you will meet that is not a matrix.

## The definition

<Definition term="Linear transformation">
A **linear transformation** $T$ from a vector space $V$ into a vector space $W$ assigns to each $\mathbf x$ in $V$ exactly one vector $T(\mathbf x)$ in $W$, such that for all $\mathbf u, \mathbf v$ in $V$ and all scalars $c$

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{(i)} & T(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) &= T(\mathbf u) + T(\mathbf v) \\
&\text{(ii)} & T(c\,\mathbf u) &= c\,T(\mathbf u) 
\end{aligned}
$$

$V$ is the **domain**, $W$ is the **codomain**, $T(\mathbf x)$ is the **image** of $\mathbf x$, and the set of all images is the **range**.
</Definition>

The plus sign on the left of (i) is addition in $V$, and the one on the right is addition in $W$. They can be completely different operations, such as adding polynomials on one side and adding columns on the other.

Two facts follow straight from the rules. First, every linear map sends zero to zero, because (ii) with $c = 0$ gives $T(\mathbf 0) = T(0\,\mathbf u) = 0\,T(\mathbf u) = \mathbf 0$. Second, (i) and (ii) together are the same as the single test

$$
T(c\,\mathbf u + d\,\mathbf v) = c\,T(\mathbf u) + d\,T(\mathbf v) \quad \text{for all } \mathbf u, \mathbf v \text{ and all } c, d.
$$

Setting $c = d = 1$ recovers (i), and setting $d = 0$ recovers (ii). Repeating the test gives **superposition**: $T$ of any linear combination is the same combination of the images.

Here is the test at work on the map $T : \mathbb P_2 \to \mathbb R^2$ that evaluates a polynomial at two points, $T(\mathbf p) = (\mathbf p(0), \mathbf p(1))$ (modeled on Lay §4.2 #31). For any $\mathbf p, \mathbf q$ in $\mathbb P_2$ and scalars $c, d$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(c\,\mathbf p + d\,\mathbf q) &= \begin{bmatrix} (c\,\mathbf p + d\,\mathbf q)(0) \\ (c\,\mathbf p + d\,\mathbf q)(1) \end{bmatrix} && \text{definition of } T \\
&= \begin{bmatrix} c\,\mathbf p(0) + d\,\mathbf q(0) \\ c\,\mathbf p(1) + d\,\mathbf q(1) \end{bmatrix} && \text{pointwise} \\
&= c \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf p(0) \\ \mathbf p(1) \end{bmatrix} + d \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf q(0) \\ \mathbf q(1) \end{bmatrix} && \text{arithmetic in } \mathbb R^2 \\
&= c\,T(\mathbf p) + d\,T(\mathbf q).
\end{aligned}
$$

A proof like this has to use arbitrary $\mathbf p$, $\mathbf q$, $c$ and $d$. Checking one pair of polynomials proves nothing, although one failing pair is enough to prove a map is not linear.

## Differentiation

The derivative $D(\mathbf p) = \mathbf p'$ takes $\mathbb P_3$ to $\mathbb P_3$. The rules $(\mathbf f + \mathbf g)' = \mathbf f' + \mathbf g'$ and $(c\,\mathbf f)' = c\,\mathbf f'$ from calculus are exactly (i) and (ii), so $D$ is linear. The figure checks (i) on two specific polynomials by going around the square both ways.

$$
\begin{aligned}
D(\yellow{\mathbf p} + \blue{\mathbf q}) &= D(1 + 3t + t^2 - t^3) \\
&= \teal{3 + 2t - 3t^2} \\[4pt]
D\yellow{\mathbf p} + D\blue{\mathbf q} &= \yellow{(2 + 2t)} + \blue{(1 - 3t^2)} \\

Both routes reach the same polynomial. Scaling works the same way: $D(3\yellow{\mathbf p}) = D(3 + 6t + 3t^2) = 6 + 6t = 3\,D\yellow{\mathbf p}$.

The transpose $T(A) = A^T$ on $2 \times 2$ matrices is linear for the same kind of reason. It only moves entries to new positions, so $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$ and $(cA)^T = c\,A^T$. Matrix maps $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ are linear because $A(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = A\mathbf u + A\mathbf v$ and $A(c\,\mathbf u) = c\,A\mathbf u$.

## Maps that are not linear

The quickest test is $T(\mathbf 0)$. A **translation** $T(\mathbf x) = \mathbf x + \mathbf b$ with $\mathbf b \neq \mathbf 0$ moves the origin to $\mathbf b$, so it cannot be linear. The same goes for any affine map $A\mathbf x + \mathbf b$ and for $f(x) = mx + b$ with $b \neq 0$, even though its graph is a straight line.

<Pair>
<Figure src="translation-misses" alt="A teal parallelogram shifted by b = (1, −1), with a glowing orange dot at its corner T(0) = (1, −1). From the true origin, yellow T(v) = (4, 1) and blue T(w) = (0, 1) are added tip to tail, and the pink sum lands at (4, 2). The teal corner T(v + w) sits at (3, 3), joined to the pink tip by an orange dashed gap." />

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\yellow{\mathbf v}) + T(\blue{\mathbf w}) &= \yellow{\mathbf v} + \blue{\mathbf w} + 2\mathbf b \\
T(\yellow{\mathbf v} + \blue{\mathbf w}) &= \yellow{\mathbf v} + \blue{\mathbf w} + \mathbf b
\end{aligned}
$$

The two sides differ by $\mathbf b$, which is exactly the orange gap. Rule (i) holds only when $\mathbf b = \mathbf 0$.
</Pair>

<TranslationGap v={[2, 1]} w={[-1, 2]} start={[1, -1]} />

Passing $T(\mathbf 0) = \mathbf 0$ does not prove a map is linear. For $T(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 - x_2,\ 2|x_2|)$ you get $T(\mathbf 0) = \mathbf 0$, but with $\mathbf u = (0, 1)$ and $c = -1$ the rule (ii) fails, since $T(0, -1) = (1, 2)$ while $-T(0, 1) = (1, -2)$.

## Counting preimages

A vector $\mathbf x$ with $T(\mathbf x) = \mathbf b$ is a **preimage** of $\mathbf b$. Two questions ask how many preimages each $\mathbf b$ in $W$ has.

<Definition term="Onto and one-to-one">
$T : V \to W$ is **onto** $W$ if every $\mathbf b$ in $W$ is the image of **at least one** $\mathbf x$ in $V$. This is an existence question, and it means the range is all of $W$.

$T$ is **one-to-one** if every $\mathbf b$ in $W$ is the image of **at most one** $\mathbf x$ in $V$. This is a uniqueness question, and it means $T(\mathbf u) = T(\mathbf v)$ forces $\mathbf u = \mathbf v$.
</Definition>

<Pair>
<Figure src="shared-derivative" alt="Left: the parabolas t² + 1 in blue, t² in yellow and t² − 2 in pink, with parallel orange tangent lines at t = 1. An arrow labelled D points right. Right: the single teal line 2t that all three parabolas differentiate to, and a gray dashed cubic t³ circled in orange, which no polynomial in P3 reaches." />

The derivative $D : \mathbb P_3 \to \mathbb P_3$ fails both tests. It is not one-to-one because $t^2$, $t^2 + 1$ and $t^2 - 2$ all have derivative $2t$. It is not onto because differentiating lowers the degree, so no polynomial in $\mathbb P_3$ has derivative $t^3$.

The codomain matters. With the same rule written as $D : \mathbb P_3 \to \mathbb P_2$, every $a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2$ is the derivative of $a_0 t + \tfrac{a_1}{2}t^2 + \tfrac{a_2}{3}t^3$, so that version is onto.
</Pair>

<SharedDerivativeHunt p={[1, 2, 1]} />

The transpose on $M_{2\times2}$ passes both tests. If $A^T = B^T$ then transposing again gives $A = B$, and any $B$ is the image of $B^T$.

For a matrix map $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ from $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$, the pivots of $A$ answer both questions (Lay §1.9, Theorem 12). The map is onto $\mathbb R^m$ exactly when $A$ has a pivot in every row, because then $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ is consistent for every $\mathbf b$. It is one-to-one exactly when $A$ has a pivot in every column, because then $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has no free variables. For example, a $3 \times 2$ matrix with independent columns gives a one-to-one map that cannot be onto $\mathbb R^3$, since two pivots cannot fill three rows.

A linear map $T : \mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R^2$ sends $\mathbf u$ to $(1, 2)$ and $\mathbf v$ to $(-1, 3)$. Find $T(3\mathbf u - 2\mathbf v)$. (Modeled on the superposition property in Lay §1.8, p. 67.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[5, 0]} />

<Answer>
By superposition, $T(3\mathbf u - 2\mathbf v) = 3\,T(\mathbf u) - 2\,T(\mathbf v) = 3(1, 2) - 2(-1, 3) = (3 + 2,\ 6 - 6) = (5, 0)$. You never need to know $\mathbf u$ or $\mathbf v$ themselves.
</Answer>
