Yesterday's column space collected every output $A\mathbf x$, so it tells you whether $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has a solution at all. The other half of solving a system is knowing how many solutions there are, and that depends on the opposite question: which inputs does $A$ send to $\mathbf 0$? Those inputs form a second subspace that comes with every matrix. By the end of today you will see that every solution of $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ is one fixed solution plus a vector from this subspace.

Each section opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway.

## The null space

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$, $A\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Which inputs land on $\mathbf 0$? Then describe every input that lands on $\mathbf 0$.

<Solution>
Multiply each input by $A$, one row at a time.

$$
A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 2 \\ 4 - 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
A\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 4 \\ 8 - 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The first two land on $\mathbf 0$ and the third does not.

To find every such input, we need $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$. Row 2 of $A$ is twice row 1, so both equations say the same thing, $x_1 + 2x_2 = 0$. That means $x_1 = -2x_2$, and $x_2$ can be anything. Writing $x_2 = -t$ gives $\mathbf x = t(2, -1)$.

So the inputs that land on $\mathbf 0$ are the multiples of $(2, -1)$. They fill a line through the origin, and $(4, -2) = 2(2, -1)$ was one of them.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Null space">
The **null space** of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is the set of all solutions of the homogeneous equation $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$:

$$
\operatorname{Nul}A = \{\, \mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n : A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0 \,\}.
$$

It lives in $\mathbb R^n$, where $n$ is the number of **columns**, because only vectors with $n$ entries can be multiplied by $A$.
</Definition>

Find two of these inputs yourself. Each input you settle on that lands on $\mathbf 0$ gets stamped in pink.

<LandingHunt matrix={[[1, 2], [2, 4]]} start={[1, 1]} prompt="Find two different nonzero inputs $\mathbf x$ that this $A$ sends to $\mathbf 0$." success="Both land on $\mathbf 0$, and so does every point of the pink line through them. That line is $\operatorname{Nul}A = \operatorname{Span}\{(2, -1)\}$." />

<Pair>
<Figure src="collapse" alt="A plane with the teal diagonal line Col P and the pink anti-diagonal line Nul P crossing at the origin. Gray arrows show several points sliding in the direction (1, −1) until they land on the teal line. The pink dots on the anti-diagonal all head to the origin, which is circled in orange. A panel shows P = ½[1 1; 1 1] and P(1, −1) = (0, 0)." />

$$
P = \tfrac12 \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
P\,\pink{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}} = \teal{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}
$$

Here is a second example. The projection $P$ slides every point along $(1, -1)$ onto the diagonal. Points already on the anti-diagonal slide all the way to the origin, so $\operatorname{Nul}P = \operatorname{Span}\{(1, -1)\}$.
</Pair>

<Concept title="Testing is easy and listing is work">
To test one vector, multiply once. For example, $P(2, 1) = (1.5, 1.5) \ne \mathbf 0$, so $(2, 1)$ is not in $\operatorname{Nul}P$. To list the whole null space, you have to solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$. This is the reverse of Day 16's column space, where listing members is easy and testing one takes a row reduction.
</Concept>

  options={["$(1, 1, 1)$", "$(1, -1, 1)$", "$(2, 0, 0)$"]}
  answer={0}
>
Multiply each one by the row. $(1, 1, 1)$ gives $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$, so it is in the null space. $(1, -1, 1)$ gives $1 - 1 - 2 = -2$ and $(2, 0, 0)$ gives $2$.
</Choice>

## Why it is a subspace

The null space of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ came out as a line through the origin, which is a subspace. The next problem shows that this always happens.

<Problem>
For the same $A$, take $\mathbf u = (2, -1)$ and $\mathbf v = (-4, 2)$, which are both in $\operatorname{Nul}A$. Compute $A(\mathbf u + \mathbf v)$ and $A(3\mathbf u)$. Could you have predicted the answers without multiplying?

<Solution>
First do it directly. The sum is $\mathbf u + \mathbf v = (-2, 1)$ and the multiple is $3\mathbf u = (6, -3)$.

$$
A\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 + 2 \\ -4 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
A\begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 - 6 \\ 12 - 12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Now predict them. Matrix multiplication splits over sums and lets scalars move out front, so

$$
A(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = A\mathbf u + A\mathbf v = \mathbf 0 + \mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0, \qquad A(3\mathbf u) = 3A\mathbf u = 3\cdot\mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0.
$$

The prediction used nothing about this particular $A$, $\mathbf u$ or $\mathbf v$ except that $A\mathbf u$ and $A\mathbf v$ were $\mathbf 0$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Theorem">
The null space of an $m \times n$ matrix is a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$.
</Definition>

The proof runs Day 11's three checks. Suppose $A\mathbf u = \mathbf 0$ and $A\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$, and let $c$ be any scalar.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{(a)} \quad A\mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0 \\
&\text{(b)} \quad A(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = A\mathbf u + A\mathbf v = \mathbf 0 + \mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0 \\
&\text{(c)} \quad A(c\,\mathbf u) = c\,A\mathbf u = c\,\mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Check (b) with the picture. The readouts show where each input lands.

<NullSumCheck matrix={[[1, 2], [2, 4]]} />

<Problem>
Is $H = \{(a, b, c) : a + 2b = c,\ 3a = b\}$ a subspace of $\mathbb R^3$? What about $K = \{(a, b, c) : a + b + c = 1\}$?

<Solution>
For $H$, move every variable to the left side so each equation reads "something $= 0$". That gives $a + 2b - c = 0$ and $3a - b = 0$, which is the system

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \\ 3 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \\ c \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

So $H$ is the null space of that matrix, and the theorem says it is a subspace.

For $K$, try the zero vector: $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$, which is not $1$. A subspace must contain $\mathbf 0$, so $K$ is not a subspace.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Homogeneous equations cut out subspaces">
A set described by **homogeneous** linear equations, meaning equations whose right side is $0$, is a null space and therefore a subspace. A single nonzero right side ruins it, because $\mathbf 0$ no longer satisfies the equations. Day 28 leans on this theorem. The eigenvectors of $A$ for one eigenvalue $\lambda$ fill $\operatorname{Nul}(A - \lambda I)$, so they form a subspace without any extra proof.
</Concept>

## Basis from free variables

Testing members one at a time never shows the whole null space, so the goal here is a basis. We want a few vectors whose combinations give every solution of $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$.

<Problem>
Find a basis for $\operatorname{Nul}A$, where $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & -4 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
The null space is the solution set of $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$, so we solve that system and then split the solution into pieces, one per free variable.

<Steps>
<Step>
Row reduce $A$ to **reduced** echelon form. We can skip the augmented column because it is all zeros and no row operation changes it. The move $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & -4 & 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The pivots are in columns 1 and 3, so $x_1$ and $x_3$ are basic and $\yellow{x_2}$ and $\blue{x_4}$ are free.
</Step>
<Step>
Solve each row for its basic variable. Row 1 says $x_1 - 2x_2 + 3x_4 = 0$, so $x_1 = 2\yellow{x_2} - 3\blue{x_4}$. Row 2 says $x_3 - 2x_4 = 0$, so $x_3 = 2\blue{x_4}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Write the whole vector using only the free variables, then split it by free variable.

$$
\mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} 2x_2 - 3x_4 \\ x_2 \\ 2x_4 \\ x_4 \end{bmatrix} = \yellow{x_2} \yellow{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}} + \blue{x_4} \blue{\begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}
$$

Every solution is a combination of $\yellow{\mathbf u} = (2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $\blue{\mathbf v} = (-3, 0, 2, 1)$, so these two vectors span $\operatorname{Nul}A$.
</Step>
<Step>
Check that they are independent. Entry 2 of $x_2\mathbf u + x_4\mathbf v$ is $x_2$ and entry 4 is $x_4$. If the combination is $\mathbf 0$, both of those entries are $0$, so both weights are $0$. That makes $\{\mathbf u, \mathbf v\}$ independent, and so it is a basis.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="free-variables" alt="The reduced form of A with rows (1, −2, 0, 3) and (0, 0, 1, −2), the equations x1 = 2x2 − 3x4 and x3 = 2x4, and the vector x written as x2 times the yellow vector u = (2, 1, 0, 0) plus x4 times the blue vector v = (−3, 0, 2, 1). Orange boxes mark entries 2 and 4 of u and v, which read 1, 0 and 0, 1." />

The orange boxes mark the free-variable rows. There $\yellow{\mathbf u}$ reads $1, 0$ and $\blue{\mathbf v}$ reads $0, 1$, which is why neither vector can be built from the other.
</Pair>

<Concept title="One basis vector per free variable">
To find a basis for $\operatorname{Nul}A$, solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ from the reduced echelon form, write $\mathbf x$ in terms of the free variables, and pull out one vector per free variable. Those vectors are automatically independent, because each has a $1$ in its own free slot and $0$ in the other free slots.
</Concept>

The sliders below set the two free variables. Watch rows 2 and 4 of the result.

<FreeWeights u={[2, 1, 0, 0]} v={[-3, 0, 2, 1]} target={[-7, -2, 2, 1]} />

<Warning>
The two spaces of this $A$ live in different places. $\operatorname{Nul}A$ sits in $\mathbb R^4$ because $A$ has four columns, and $\operatorname{Col}A$ sits in $\mathbb R^2$ because each column has two entries. A vector's length alone tells you which of the two it could belong to.
</Warning>

<Choice
  question="$A$ is $2 \times 4$. Which space could the vector $(1, 0, 3, -1)$ belong to?"
  options={["Only $\\operatorname{Nul}A$", "Only $\\operatorname{Col}A$", "Both"]}
  answer={0}
>
The vector has four entries. $\operatorname{Nul}A$ is inside $\mathbb R^4$ and $\operatorname{Col}A$ is inside $\mathbb R^2$, so only the null space is possible. Whether it actually is in $\operatorname{Nul}A$ still takes one multiplication to check.
</Choice>

## Solutions of Ax = b

The opening promised a link between the null space and the solutions of $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ when $\mathbf b \ne \mathbf 0$. Here it is.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf b = (1, 2)$. Check that $(1, 0)$, $(3, -1)$ and $(-1, 1)$ all solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$. Then subtract $(1, 0)$ from each of the other two. What do you notice?

<Solution>
Multiply each candidate by $A$.

$$
A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
A\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 2 \\ 6 - 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad
A\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 + 2 \\ -2 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

All three solve it. Now subtract:

$$
(3, -1) - (1, 0) = (2, -1), \qquad (-1, 1) - (1, 0) = (-2, 1).
$$

Both differences are multiples of $(2, -1)$, so both are in $\operatorname{Nul}A$ from the first problem. The reason is that $A(\mathbf w - \mathbf p) = A\mathbf w - A\mathbf p = \mathbf b - \mathbf b = \mathbf 0$ whenever $\mathbf w$ and $\mathbf p$ both solve the system.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Solution sets are shifted null spaces">
If $A\mathbf p = \mathbf b$ for one vector $\mathbf p$, then the solutions of $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ are exactly the vectors $\mathbf p + \mathbf v$ with $\mathbf v$ in $\operatorname{Nul}A$.
</Definition>

The problem showed one direction: any solution minus $\mathbf p$ is in $\operatorname{Nul}A$. The other direction is just as short. If $A\mathbf v = \mathbf 0$, then $A(\mathbf p + \mathbf v) = \mathbf b + \mathbf 0 = \mathbf b$.

<LandingHunt matrix={[[1, 2], [2, 4]]} target={[1, 2]} start={[2, 2]} prompt="On Day 7 this matrix sent three different points to $\mathbf b = (1, 2)$. Find two inputs with $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$." success="Both solve it, and their difference is in $\operatorname{Nul}A$. The yellow line of all solutions is the dashed pink null space shifted off the origin." />

<Pair>
<Figure src="shifted-copy" alt="The pink line Nul P through the origin and a parallel yellow line through the tip of the yellow arrow p = (4, 0). The yellow line crosses the teal line Col P at b = (2, 2), which is circled in orange. A second orange circle marks the origin, which the yellow line misses." />

For the projection $P$ and $\mathbf b = (2, 2)$, one solution is $\yellow{\mathbf p} = (4, 0)$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\{\mathbf x : P\mathbf x = \mathbf b\} &= \yellow{\mathbf p} + \pink{\operatorname{Nul}P} \\
&= \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This line is parallel to $\operatorname{Nul}P$, but it misses $\mathbf 0$.
</Pair>

A shifted line is not a subspace. Slide the right side $k$ of the equation $x_1 + 2x_2 = k$ and watch what happens to the sum of two solutions.

<RightSideShift row={[1, 2]} direction={[2, -1]} />

<Warning>
The shifted-copy picture needs at least one solution $\mathbf p$ to start from. When $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ is inconsistent, the solution set is empty and there is nothing to shift.
</Warning>

The theorem also answers the question from the opening. A consistent system has exactly one solution when $\operatorname{Nul}A = \{\mathbf 0\}$, and infinitely many otherwise. Day 19 adds $\operatorname{Nul}A = \{\mathbf 0\}$ to its list of tests for an invertible matrix.

<Check>
Find the value of $h$ for which $\operatorname{Nul}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ is a line instead of just $\{\mathbf 0\}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[3]} labels={["h"]} prefix="h =" />

<Hint>
The null space has a nonzero vector exactly when the reduced form has a free variable.
</Hint>

<Answer>
The move $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$ gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 6 - 2h \end{bmatrix}.
$$

If $6 - 2h \neq 0$ both columns have pivots, there are no free variables, and $\operatorname{Nul}A = \{\mathbf 0\}$. When $h = 3$ the second row is zero, $x_2$ is free, and $\operatorname{Nul}A = \operatorname{Span}\{(-3, 1)\}$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Show that if $\operatorname{Nul}A = \{\mathbf 0\}$, then $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has at most one solution for every $\mathbf b$.

<Hint>
Suppose $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf q$ are both solutions, and look at $\mathbf p - \mathbf q$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Suppose $A\mathbf p = \mathbf b$ and $A\mathbf q = \mathbf b$. Then

$$
A(\mathbf p - \mathbf q) = A\mathbf p - A\mathbf q = \mathbf b - \mathbf b = \mathbf 0,
$$

so $\mathbf p - \mathbf q$ is in $\operatorname{Nul}A$. The only vector there is $\mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf p = \mathbf q$. Any two solutions are equal, which means there is at most one. There may be none, since the null space says nothing about whether $\mathbf b$ is in $\operatorname{Col}A$.
</Answer>
</Check>
