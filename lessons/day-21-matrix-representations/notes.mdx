Day 20 showed that many maps are linear, including differentiation, but a rule like $\mathbf p \mapsto \mathbf p'$ gives you nothing to row reduce, invert or multiply. Today asks whether every linear map can be written as a matrix, so that the matrix tools from Days 3 to 19 apply to it. Two earlier facts supply the answer. On Day 5 the columns of a matrix turned out to be the landing spots of $\mathbf e_1$ and $\mathbf e_2$, and on Day 15 a basis turned every vector, polynomials included, into a column of coordinates. Once you pick a basis for the input space and one for the output space, any linear map can be written as a matrix, even a map like differentiation that never mentions arrows.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway.

## The standard matrix

<Problem>
A linear map $T:\mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R^2$ sends $\mathbf e_1 = (1, 0)$ to $\green{(2, 1)}$ and $\mathbf e_2 = (0, 1)$ to $\red{(-1, 1)}$. Nobody tells you anything else about $T$. Find $T(1, 2)$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[0, 3]} />

<Solution>
We know $T$ only on $\mathbf e_1$ and $\mathbf e_2$, so the natural first move is to write the input in terms of those two vectors:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1\,\mathbf e_1 + 2\,\mathbf e_2.
$$

A linear map keeps sums and scalar multiples (Day 20), so $T$ of this combination is the same combination of the images:

$$
T(1, 2) = 1\,\green{T(\mathbf e_1)} + 2\,\red{T(\mathbf e_2)} = 1\green{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + 2\red{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 2 \\ 1 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \teal{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}.
$$

The middle expression is a combination of two columns with weights $1$ and $2$. Day 4 gave that a shorter name: it is a matrix times a vector,

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \green{2} & \red{-1} \\ \green{1} & \red{1} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The two images were enough to find $T(1, 2)$, and the same steps work for any input. So two vectors of data pin down the whole map.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Columns are landing spots">
Put the images of $\mathbf e_1$ and $\mathbf e_2$ side by side as columns, and that matrix does whatever $T$ does. The reason is the one from the problem. Every $\mathbf x$ is $x_1\mathbf e_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf e_n$, and a linear $T$ keeps that combination:

$$
T(\mathbf x) = x_1\,T(\mathbf e_1) + \cdots + x_n\,T(\mathbf e_n).
$$
</Concept>

<Figure src="standard-matrix" alt="The plane after the map T. The moved blue grid is skewed, the green arrow T(e1) ends at (2, 1) and the red arrow T(e2) at (−1, 1), each with its landing column. One green step and two red steps lead tip to tail to the teal arrow T(x) = (0, 3). A panel shows A with columns (2, 1) and (−1, 1), and the product A times (1, 2) equal to (0, 3)." />

<Definition term="Standard matrix">
Let $T:\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ be linear. There is exactly one matrix $A$ with $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ for every $\mathbf x$, and it is the $m \times n$ matrix whose $j$th column is the image of $\mathbf e_j$:

$$
A = \big[\,T(\mathbf e_1)\ \ T(\mathbf e_2)\ \ \cdots\ \ T(\mathbf e_n)\,\big].
$$

It is called the **standard matrix** of $T$.
</Definition>

Try it with a different input. The widget draws only the two columns, and you place the output.

## A matrix from a formula

<Problem>
Let $T(x_1, x_2) = (x_1 + 2x_2,\ 3x_1 - x_2,\ 4x_2)$. Find the standard matrix of $T$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 2, 3, -1, 0, 4]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
The definition says the columns are $T(\mathbf e_1)$ and $T(\mathbf e_2)$, so we compute those two outputs.

<Steps>
<Step>
Put $x_1 = 1$ and $x_2 = 0$: $\;T(\mathbf e_1) = (1 + 0,\ 3 - 0,\ 0) = (1, 3, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Put $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = 1$: $\;T(\mathbf e_2) = (0 + 2,\ 0 - 1,\ 4) = (2, -1, 4)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Stand them up as columns: $\;A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

Now look at the rows. Row 1 is $(1, 2)$, which are the coefficients in the first output entry $x_1 + 2x_2$. Row 2 holds the coefficients of $3x_1 - x_2$, and row 3 holds those of $0x_1 + 4x_2$. So you can read the matrix off the formula without plugging anything in.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Read rows off a formula">
When $T$ is given by a formula, row $i$ of its standard matrix holds the coefficients of output entry $i$. The matrix has one column per input variable and one row per output entry, so a map $\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ has an $m \times n$ matrix.
</Concept>

<Choice
  question="What is the standard matrix of $T(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (x_1 - x_3,\ 2x_2)$?"
  options={["$\\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ 0 & 2 \\\\ -1 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\\\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\\\ 2 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$"]}
  answer={1}
>
The map goes from $\mathbb R^3$ to $\mathbb R^2$, so the matrix is $2 \times 3$. Row 1 holds the coefficients of $1x_1 + 0x_2 - 1x_3$ and row 2 holds those of $0x_1 + 2x_2 + 0x_3$. The missing variables become zeros, which is what keeps every coefficient in the right column.
</Choice>

<Warning>
The images go in as columns, not rows. A quick check is the size. For $T:\mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R^4$, each image has 4 entries, so the matrix is $4 \times 2$. If you wrote a $2 \times 4$ matrix, you put the images in rows.
</Warning>

## A matrix from a picture

Some maps come with no formula at all, only a description like "turn the plane" or "flip it over an axis." The recipe still works, because all it asks is where $\mathbf e_1$ and $\mathbf e_2$ land.

<Problem>
Let $T$ turn the plane $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin. Find its standard matrix.

<VectorAnswer answer={[0, -1, 1, 0]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
We need the two landing spots, so we follow each basis vector through the turn. The vector $\mathbf e_1 = (1, 0)$ points right. A quarter turn counterclockwise makes it point straight up, so $T(\mathbf e_1) = (0, 1)$. The vector $\mathbf e_2 = (0, 1)$ points up, and a quarter turn makes it point left, so $T(\mathbf e_2) = (-1, 0)$.

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} \green{0} & \red{-1} \\ \green{1} & \red{0} \end{bmatrix}
$$

As a check, $A$ sends $(3, 1)$ to $(-1, 3)$. The first vector points right and a little up, and the second points up and a little left, which is what a quarter turn should do.
</Solution>
</Problem>

For a turn through any angle $\varphi$, the same tracking works. The vector $\mathbf e_1$ moves to the point at angle $\varphi$ on the unit circle, $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, and $\mathbf e_2$ stays a quarter turn ahead of it at $(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$. With $\varphi = 90^\circ$ these are $(0, 1)$ and $(-1, 0)$, the columns you just found. So the **rotation matrix** is

$$
\begin{bmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Use the rings to work backward from a matrix to the turn it describes.

<Problem>
Let $T$ first shear the plane so that $\mathbf e_1$ stays put and $\mathbf e_2$ moves to $\mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_1$, and then reflect everything across the horizontal axis. Find the standard matrix of $T$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 1, 0, -1]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
There are two moves, so we follow each basis vector through both of them, in the order they happen.

<Steps>
<Step>
Follow $\mathbf e_1$. The shear leaves it at $(1, 0)$. Reflecting across the horizontal axis flips the sign of the second entry, and $0$ stays $0$, so $T(\mathbf e_1) = (1, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Follow $\mathbf e_2$. The shear moves it to $\mathbf e_2 + \mathbf e_1 = (1, 1)$. The reflection flips the second entry, giving $T(\mathbf e_2) = (1, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Stand the two landing spots up as columns: $\;A = \begin{bmatrix} \green{1} & \red{1} \\ \green{0} & \red{-1} \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

You never needed the shear matrix or the reflection matrix on their own. Tracking the two vectors through both moves gives the final columns directly.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
Order matters when you combine moves. If you reflect first and shear second, $\mathbf e_2$ goes to $(0, -1)$ and then to $(-1, -1)$, which is a different matrix. Follow the vectors in the order the description gives.
</Warning>

## Matrices for any bases

A polynomial is not a column, so $d/dt$ on $\mathbb P_2$ has no standard matrix of its own. The goal of this section is a matrix that differentiates a polynomial when you multiply it by the polynomial's coordinate column. Coordinates from Day 15 make that possible, and the next problem shows how.

<Problem>
A space $V$ has basis $\mathcal B = \{\mathbf b_1, \mathbf b_2\}$ and a space $W$ has basis $\mathcal C = \{\mathbf c_1, \mathbf c_2, \mathbf c_3\}$. A linear map $T:V \to W$ has

$$
T(\mathbf b_1) = \mathbf c_1 + 4\mathbf c_3, \qquad T(\mathbf b_2) = -2\mathbf c_1 + \mathbf c_2 - \mathbf c_3.
$$

Find the $\mathcal C$-coordinates of $T(3\mathbf b_1 + \mathbf b_2)$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 1, 11]} labels={["c₁", "c₂", "c₃"]} />

<Solution>
We know $T$ only on $\mathbf b_1$ and $\mathbf b_2$, so we use linearity exactly as in Problem 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(3\mathbf b_1 + \mathbf b_2) &= 3\,T(\mathbf b_1) + T(\mathbf b_2) \\
&= (3\mathbf c_1 + 12\mathbf c_3) + (-2\mathbf c_1 + \mathbf c_2 - \mathbf c_3) \\
&= \mathbf c_1 + \mathbf c_2 + 11\mathbf c_3.
\end{aligned}
$$

So the $\mathcal C$-coordinates are $(1, 1, 11)$.

Now write each image as its column of $\mathcal C$-coordinates, $T(\mathbf b_1) \to (1, 0, 4)$ and $T(\mathbf b_2) \to (-2, 1, -1)$. Remember the $0$, since $T(\mathbf b_1)$ has no $\mathbf c_2$ in it. With those as columns,

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 - 2 \\ 0 + 1 \\ 12 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The input's $\mathcal B$-coordinates $(3, 1)$ went in, and the output's $\mathcal C$-coordinates came out. The vectors $\mathbf b_j$ and $\mathbf c_i$ could be arrows, polynomials or anything else, and the matrix never needs to know.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Matrix of T relative to two bases">
Let $T: V \to W$ be linear, let $\mathcal B = \{\mathbf b_1, \dots, \mathbf b_n\}$ be a basis for $V$, and let $\mathcal C$ be a basis for $W$ with $m$ vectors. The **matrix of $T$ relative to $\mathcal B$ and $\mathcal C$** is the $m \times n$ matrix

$$
[T]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B} = \big[\,[T(\mathbf b_1)]_{\mathcal C}\ \ [T(\mathbf b_2)]_{\mathcal C}\ \ \cdots\ \ [T(\mathbf b_n)]_{\mathcal C}\,\big],
$$

and it satisfies $[T(\mathbf x)]_{\mathcal C} = [T]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}\,[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$ for every $\mathbf x$ in $V$. When $W = V$ and $\mathcal C = \mathcal B$, it is written $[T]_{\mathcal B}$.
</Definition>

The input basis sits on the right of the arrow and the output basis on the left, in the same order as the product $[T]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$. The formula works for the same reason the standard matrix does. If $[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B} = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$, then $T(\mathbf x) = r_1 T(\mathbf b_1) + \cdots + r_n T(\mathbf b_n)$, and taking $\mathcal C$-coordinates keeps that combination because the coordinate map is linear. The notation $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ comes back on Day 22, where the same map gets matrices in two different bases, and on Day 33, where the goal is a basis that makes $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ diagonal.

Now the polynomial case. The recipe is the same: send each basis vector through $T$, then write the result in output coordinates.

<Problem>
Let $T = d/dt$ on $\mathbb P_2$, with $\mathcal B = \{\green{1}, \red{t}, \blue{t^2}\}$ for both input and output. Find $[T]_{\mathcal B}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 2, 0, 0, 0]} columns={3} />

<Solution>
We need one column per basis polynomial, so we differentiate each one and write the answer in $\mathcal B$-coordinates.

<Steps>
<Step>
Differentiate $\green{1}$. The derivative of a constant is $0 = 0\cdot 1 + 0\cdot t + 0\cdot t^2$, so column 1 is $(0, 0, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Differentiate $\red{t}$. The derivative is $1 = 1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot t + 0\cdot t^2$, so column 2 is $(1, 0, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Differentiate $\blue{t^2}$. The derivative is $2t = 0\cdot 1 + 2\cdot t + 0\cdot t^2$, so column 3 is $(0, 2, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Stand the three columns side by side: $\;[T]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} \green{0} & \red{1} & \blue{0} \\ \green{0} & \red{0} & \blue{2} \\ \green{0} & \red{0} & \blue{0} \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The first column is all zeros because $T(1) = 0$. It still takes up a slot, since dropping it would slide every other column one place to the left.
</Solution>
</Problem>

A second arrow labelled with the B-coordinate brackets turns 2t into the blue column (0, 2, 0). <Warning>
Each column holds the *coordinates* of $T(\mathbf b_j)$, not $T(\mathbf b_j)$ itself. With polynomials that is obvious, since $2t$ is not a column. With a nonstandard basis of $\mathbb R^m$ it is easy to forget, and the raw output vector gives the wrong column.
</Warning>

The widget uses a different map on $\mathbb P_2$, the shift $T(\mathbf p)(t) = \mathbf p(t + 1)$. For example, $T(t^2) = (t + 1)^2 = 1 + 2t + t^2$, so its column is $(1, 2, 1)$. Its dashed curve shows $T(\mathbf b_j)$, and your column is right when the curve it describes lands on it.

## Two routes to the same column

<Problem>
Let $\mathbf p(t) = 3 + 5t - 2t^2$. Find $[\mathbf p']_{\mathcal B}$ in two ways: by differentiating $\mathbf p$ directly, and by multiplying $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ from Problem 6 by $[\mathbf p]_{\mathcal B}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[5, -4, 0]} />

<Solution>
Directly, $\mathbf p'(t) = 5 - 4t$, and its coordinates in $\{1, t, t^2\}$ are $(5, -4, 0)$.

Through the matrix, first write $\mathbf p$ as a column. Its coefficients in $\{1, t, t^2\}$ are $(3, 5, -2)$. Then multiply:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\yellow{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 + 5 + 0 \\ 0 + 0 - 4 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \teal{\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ -4 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}.
$$

Both routes give $(5, -4, 0)$. The $3$ in $\mathbf p$ never reached the answer, because it multiplied the zero column.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="two-routes" alt="A square diagram. Top left, p(t) = 3 + 5t − 2t² in yellow; top right, p′(t) = 5 − 4t in teal, joined by a d/dt arrow. Arrows labelled with B-coordinate brackets point down to the yellow column (3, 5, −2) and the teal column (5, −4, 0). The bottom arrow carries the matrix of T relative to B, and the teal column has an orange frame." />

<Concept title="The square commutes">
Going across the top means applying $T$ and then taking coordinates. Going down and across the bottom means taking coordinates and then multiplying by the matrix. For a general $\mathbf p = a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2$ both routes give the same column:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[T(\mathbf p)]_{\mathcal B} &= [a_1 + 2a_2 t]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ 2a_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \\
[T]_{\mathcal B}[\mathbf p]_{\mathcal B} &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ 2a_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$
</Concept>

So you can differentiate a polynomial by multiplying its coefficient column by $[T]_{\mathcal B}$. The entry $a_0$ never reaches the output, which is the matrix's way of saying that constants have derivative zero.

<DerivativeRoutes target={[4, -6, 0]} />

The matrix also works in the other direction. If someone hands you only the matrix, multiplying it by a general coordinate column recovers the formula for $T$.

<Problem>
A linear map $T:\mathbb P_1 \to \mathbb P_1$ has $[T]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ for $\mathcal B = \{1, t\}$. Find $T(3 + 2t)$, and then find a formula for $T(a_0 + a_1 t)$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[8, -2]} labels={["1", "t"]} />

<Solution>
The matrix acts on coordinates, so we convert the input first. The polynomial $3 + 2t$ has $\mathcal B$-coordinates $(3, 2)$, and

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 + 2 \\ 0 - 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Those are output coordinates, so we turn them back into a polynomial: $T(3 + 2t) = 8 - 2t$.

For the general formula, repeat the same multiplication with letters: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a_0 \\ a_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2a_0 + a_1 \\ -a_1 \end{bmatrix}$, so $T(a_0 + a_1 t) = (2a_0 + a_1) - a_1 t$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

Every derivative of a quadratic lies in $\mathbb P_1$. With the smaller output basis $\mathcal C = \{1, t\}$, the zero row disappears and the matrix of $d/dt$ becomes $2 \times 3$:

$$
[T]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The size always reads (dimension of the output) by (dimension of the input).

<Choice
  question="Take $V = \mathbb R^2$ and $W = \mathbb R^3$ with their standard bases. What is $[T]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ then?"
  options={["The standard matrix of $T$", "Its transpose", "Something new that needs its own recipe"]}
  answer={0}
>
In the standard basis, the coordinates of a vector are just its entries, so $[T(\mathbf e_j)]_{\mathcal C} = T(\mathbf e_j)$. The columns are the images of $\mathbf e_j$, which is the standard matrix from the first section.
</Choice>

Because the matrix does everything $T$ does, questions about $T$ now become questions about a matrix. On Day 23, for example, a polynomial is in the kernel of $d/dt$ exactly when its coordinate column is in the null space of the derivative's matrix. Tomorrow you take $T$ to be the identity map, so that the matrix only translates coordinates from one basis to another.
