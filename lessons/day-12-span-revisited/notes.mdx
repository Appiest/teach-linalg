On Day 2 you met the span as the set of every combination $c_1\mathbf v_1 + \cdots + c_p\mathbf v_p$, and Day 11 showed that every span is a subspace. Today you'll see that it is the *smallest* subspace holding those vectors, and you'll learn a quick row-reduction test for whether a set of vectors spans all of $\mathbb R^m$.

## The smallest subspace

<Definition term="Smallest subspace">
If $H$ is any subspace of $V$ that contains $\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p$, then $H$ contains all of $\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p\}$. Since the span is a subspace itself, it is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains $\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p$.
</Definition>

The proof uses only the two closure rules. Pick any weights $c_1, \dots, c_p$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf v_i \in H &\implies c_i\mathbf v_i \in H && \text{closed under scaling} \\
&\implies c_1\mathbf v_1 + \cdots + c_p\mathbf v_p \in H && \text{closed under addition}
\end{aligned}
$$

Every combination lands in $H$, so the whole span sits inside $H$. A subspace can be bigger than the span, but it can never be smaller.

A set $\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p\}$ is a **spanning set** for a subspace $H$ when $H = \operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p\}$. Think of it as a recipe for $H$. A few vectors describe infinitely many, because every element of $H$ is some choice of weights on them.

## Redundant vectors

<Pair>
<Figure src="redundant-vector" alt="Three-dimensional axes with the teal plane spanned by the yellow vector u = (2, −1, 1) and the blue vector v = (1, 2, 1). A blue arrow 2v runs from the tip of u to the tip of the pink vector w = u + 2v, which ends on the plane at an orange dot." />

Take Day 2's plane $\operatorname{Span}\{\yellow{\mathbf u}, \blue{\mathbf v}\}$ with $\yellow{\mathbf u} = (2, -1, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf v} = (1, 2, 1)$, and add $\pink{\mathbf w} = (4, 3, 3)$. Because $\pink{\mathbf w} = \yellow{\mathbf u} + 2\blue{\mathbf v}$, any combination that uses $\pink{\mathbf w}$ folds back into $\yellow{\mathbf u}$ and $\blue{\mathbf v}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1\yellow{\mathbf u} + c_2\blue{\mathbf v} + c_3\pink{\mathbf w} &= (c_1 + c_3)\,\yellow{\mathbf u} \\
&\quad + (c_2 + 2c_3)\,\blue{\mathbf v}
\end{aligned}
$$

The span stays the same plane, so $\pink{\mathbf w}$ is **redundant**.
</Pair>

<ThirdVectorHunt start={[0, 0, 2]} />

When the new vector lies off the plane, the span grows. Change $\pink{\mathbf w}$ to $(4, 3, -1)$. Each multiple $c_3\pink{\mathbf w}$ slides the whole plane of $\yellow{\mathbf u}$ and $\blue{\mathbf v}$ to a parallel copy, and those copies stack up through every point of space.

<Figure src="new-direction" alt="The teal plane of u and v with six fainter parallel copies stacked above and below it. The pink vector w = (4, 3, −1) points below the plane, and a dashed orange segment joins its tip to the point (4, 3, 3) on the plane." wide />

In general, if $\mathbf w$ is already in $\operatorname{Span}\{\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_p\}$, adding it changes nothing. If $\mathbf w$ is not in that span, the new span is strictly bigger, because it contains $\mathbf w$.

## A pivot in every row

<Definition term="Theorem (Lay §1.4, Theorem 4)">
Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix. The columns of $A$ span $\mathbb R^m$ exactly when $A$ has a pivot position in every row. Equivalently, $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has a solution for every $\mathbf b$ in $\mathbb R^m$.
</Definition>

The reason is the last row of the echelon form. If every row of $A$ has a pivot, the augmented matrix $[\,A\ \ \mathbf b\,]$ can never end in a row $[\,0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid \text{nonzero}\,]$, so every $\mathbf b$ is reachable. If some row of $A$ reduces to zeros, you can choose a $\mathbf b$ that puts a nonzero number at the end of that row, and that $\mathbf b$ is unreachable. The test is about $A$ alone, not $[\,A\ \ \mathbf b\,]$.

<Figure src="pivot-rows" alt="Two row reductions. Top: the matrix with columns u, v and w = (4, 3, 3) reduces to an echelon form with pivots 2 and 5 circled in orange and a dashed outline around its third row of zeros, labelled a plane. Bottom: with w = (4, 3, −1), the echelon form has pivots 2, 5 and −40 circled, labelled R³." wide />

When the columns don't span, carry a general $\mathbf b$ through the reduction to find out which targets you *can* reach. With $\pink{\mathbf w} = (4, 3, 3)$, the row operations $R_2 \to 2R_2 + R_1$, then $R_3 \to 2R_3 - R_1$, then $R_3 \to 5R_3 - R_2$ give

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} \yellow{2} & \blue{1} & \pink{4} & b_1 \\ \yellow{-1} & \blue{2} & \pink{3} & b_2 \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{1} & \pink{3} & b_3 \end{array}\right]
&\sim
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 1 & 4 & b_1 \\ 0 & 5 & 10 & b_1 + 2b_2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & 2b_3 - b_1 \end{array}\right] \\
&\sim
\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 1 & 4 & b_1 \\ 0 & 5 & 10 & b_1 + 2b_2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & -6b_1 - 2b_2 + 10b_3 \end{array}\right]
\end{aligned}
$$

The system is consistent only when $-6b_1 - 2b_2 + 10b_3 = 0$, which simplifies to $3b_1 + b_2 - 5b_3 = 0$. That equation describes the plane $\operatorname{Span}\{\yellow{\mathbf u}, \blue{\mathbf v}\}$, and you can check it on $\yellow{\mathbf u}$: $6 - 1 - 5 = 0$.

<PivotHunt a1={[1, 2, 0]} a2={[0, 1, 1]} a3={[2, 1]} start={1} />

The theorem also explains why **fewer than $m$ vectors can never span $\mathbb R^m$**. A matrix has at most one pivot per column, so $n < m$ columns give at most $n$ pivots, and at least one of the $m$ rows is left without one. Having more vectors doesn't guarantee spanning either. Four vectors can still lie in one plane of $\mathbb R^3$.

## Spanning sets of polynomials

A polynomial $a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2$ in $\mathbb P_2$ is a combination of $\mathbf p_1, \mathbf p_2, \mathbf p_3$ exactly when its coefficient column $(a_0, a_1, a_2)$ is the same combination of their coefficient columns. Two polynomials are equal when their matching coefficients are equal, so the question becomes a question about vectors in $\mathbb R^3$. List the coefficients in the order $1, t, t^2$ and include the zeros.

$$
\yellow{1 + t},\ \ \blue{t + t^2},\ \ \pink{1 + t^2}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\begin{bmatrix} \yellow{1} & \blue{0} & \pink{1} \\ \yellow{1} & \blue{1} & \pink{0} \\ \yellow{0} & \blue{1} & \pink{1} \end{bmatrix}
\sim
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Every row has a pivot, so these three polynomials span $\mathbb P_2$. Any spanning set works as a recipe, including this one with $\mathbf p_1 = 1$, $\mathbf p_2 = 1 + t$ and $\mathbf p_3 = 1 + t + t^2$.

## Practice

<Check>
For what value of $h$ is $(h, 1, 3)$ in $\operatorname{Span}\{(1, 1, 0),\ (0, 1, 1)\}$? (Modeled on Lay §1.3 #17.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-2]} labels={["h"]} prefix="h =" />

<Answer>
Look for weights with $a(1, 1, 0) + b(0, 1, 1) = (h, 1, 3)$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{third entry:}\quad b &= 3 \\
\text{second entry:}\quad a + b &= 1 \implies a = -2 \\
\text{first entry:}\quad a &= h \implies h = -2
\end{aligned}
$$

Any other $h$ leaves the three equations inconsistent, so $(h, 1, 3)$ is in the span only when $h = -2$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
The vectors $(1, 0, 2)$, $(2, 1, 3)$ and $(0, 1, -1)$ do not span $\mathbb R^3$, because the third one is redundant. Find $a$ and $b$ with $(0, 1, -1) = a(1, 0, 2) + b(2, 1, 3)$. (Modeled on Lay §4.1 #13.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-2, 1]} labels={["a", "b"]} />

<Answer>
The middle entries give $0a + 1b = 1$, so $b = 1$. The first entries give $a + 2b = 0$, so $a = -2$. The last entries check out: $2(-2) + 3(1) = -1$.

So $(0, 1, -1) = -2(1, 0, 2) + (2, 1, 3)$. The span of all three is the plane $\operatorname{Span}\{(1, 0, 2), (2, 1, 3)\}$, and the matrix of the three columns has pivots in only two rows.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Write $2 + 4t$ as $c_1(1 + t) + c_2(t + t^2) + c_3(1 + t^2)$. (Modeled on Lay §4.4 #31.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 1, -1]} labels={["c₁", "c₂", "c₃"]} />

<Answer>
Match the coefficients of $1$, $t$ and $t^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1 + c_3 &= 2 \\
c_1 + c_2 &= 4 \\
c_2 + c_3 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding all three equations gives $2(c_1 + c_2 + c_3) = 6$, so $c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = 3$. Subtracting each equation from that total gives $c_2 = 1$, $c_3 = -1$ and $c_1 = 3$.

Check: $3(1 + t) + (t + t^2) - (1 + t^2) = 2 + 4t$.
</Answer>
</Check>
