Yesterday you checked a proposed eigenvalue by row reducing $A - \lambda I$ and looking for a free variable. That works once you have a candidate, but it gives no way to find one. The course spent Days 25 to 27 on determinants so that today they can turn the search into a single equation in $\lambda$, whose roots are exactly the eigenvalues.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. You need Day 28's test for an eigenvalue and the $2 \times 2$ determinant $ad - bc$.

## The characteristic equation

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Is $\lambda = 2$ an eigenvalue? Is $\lambda = 3$? Then, instead of testing numbers one at a time, find every $\lambda$ for which $A - \lambda I$ is not invertible.

<Solution>
Start with Day 28's test, one candidate at a time.

For $\lambda = 2$, $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its determinant is $(-1)(-1) - 2 \cdot 2 = -3$, which is not zero, so the matrix is invertible, every column has a pivot, and $2$ is not an eigenvalue.

For $\lambda = 3$, $A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$. Its determinant is $4 - 4 = 0$, so it is not invertible, it has a free variable, and $3$ is an eigenvalue.

Testing numbers one by one could take forever. So keep $\lambda$ as a letter and compute the determinant once:

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 1 - \lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 1 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = (1 - \lambda)^2 - 4.
$$

This is zero when $(1 - \lambda)^2 = 4$, so $1 - \lambda = 2$ or $1 - \lambda = -2$. That gives $\lambda = -1$ and $\lambda = 3$, and there are no others.
</Solution>
</Problem>

At λ = −1 the grid collapses onto the yellow line, the blue line through (1, −1) glows with an orange ring at the origin, and det(A − λI) = 0. At λ = 1 the unit square becomes a pink square of signed area −4 and both eigen-arrows stay nonzero. At λ = 3 the grid collapses onto the blue line, the yellow line through (1, 1) glows, and det(A − λI) = 0." wide />

<Concept title="Eigenvalues are roots of one equation">
On Day 28, $\lambda$ was an eigenvalue exactly when $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ had a nonzero solution. That happens exactly when $A - \lambda I$ is not invertible, by the Invertible Matrix Theorem, and that happens exactly when $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, by Day 26.

The first equation has two unknowns, $\lambda$ and $\mathbf x$. The last one has only $\lambda$, so you can solve it first and look for $\mathbf x$ afterwards.
</Concept>

<Definition term="Characteristic equation">
For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, the **characteristic polynomial** is $\det(A - \lambda I)$, a polynomial of degree $n$ in $\lambda$. A scalar $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ if and only if it satisfies the **characteristic equation**

$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.
$$
</Definition>

In pictures, $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is the signed area of the unit square after $A - \lambda I$ acts. As $\lambda$ slides, that area changes smoothly, and it hits zero only where $A - \lambda I$ squashes the plane onto a line.

## A 2 by 2 example

Computing the characteristic polynomial aims for a factored polynomial in $\lambda$, because you can read the roots straight off the factors. After that, each root gets its eigenvectors from Day 28's method.

<Problem>
Find the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and an eigenvector for each.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Subtract $\lambda$ from the diagonal only, then use $ad - bc$:

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 \cdot 1.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
Multiply out and collect terms: $(4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) = 12 - 7\lambda + \lambda^2$, so the polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10$.
</Step>
<Step>
Factor it. Two numbers that multiply to $10$ and add to $7$ are $2$ and $5$, so $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 5)$. The eigenvalues are $2$ and $5$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $\lambda = 2$, row reduce $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The equation $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$ gives the eigenvector $\yellow{(-1, 2)}$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $\lambda = 5$, row reduce $A - 5I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The equation $x_1 = x_2$ gives the eigenvector $\blue{(1, 1)}$. Check: $A(1, 1) = (5, 5)$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

Now do both halves yourself on a new matrix. First pick $\lambda$ with the slider, then drag $\mathbf x$ until $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$.

A quadratic can have two roots, one repeated root or no real roots, so a $2 \times 2$ matrix can too.

<Problem>
Find the characteristic polynomial of the quarter turn $R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. What are its real eigenvalues?

<Solution>
$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (-\lambda)(-\lambda) - (-1)(1) = \lambda^2 + 1.
$$

For every real $\lambda$, $\lambda^2 \ge 0$, so $\lambda^2 + 1 \ge 1$ and never reaches zero. The quarter turn has no real eigenvalues. That matches the picture, since a quarter turn moves every line through the origin off itself.
</Solution>
</Problem>

Slide $h$ in the matrix below and watch the two eigenvalues move toward each other, merge, and then disappear from the real line.

<RepeatedRootSlider center={3} start={4} />

<Warning>
Read roots with the right sign. The factor $(\lambda + 1)$ is zero at $\lambda = -1$, not $\lambda = 1$. A quick check for a $2 \times 2$ matrix is that the two eigenvalues add up to $a + d$ and multiply to $ad - bc$. Day 30 explains why.
</Warning>

## A 3 by 3 example

For a $3 \times 3$ matrix the goal is the same, a factored polynomial, and cofactor expansion from Day 26 is the tool.

<Problem>
Find the eigenvalues of $M = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
Write $M - \lambda I$ by subtracting $\lambda$ down the diagonal:

$$
M - \lambda I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - \lambda & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 - \lambda & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & 3 - \lambda \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row 1 has two zeros, so expand along it. Only the first term survives, with sign $+$:

$$
\det(M - \lambda I) = (4 - \lambda)\begin{vmatrix} 1 - \lambda & 2 \\ 0 & 3 - \lambda \end{vmatrix}.
$$

The minor is triangular, so its determinant is the product of its diagonal, $(1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)$. Altogether,

$$
\det(M - \lambda I) = (4 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda),
$$

and the eigenvalues are $4$, $1$ and $3$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="cofactor" alt="The matrix M − λI with rows (4 − λ, 0, 0), (−3, 1 − λ, 2) and (2, 0, 3 − λ). Row 1 is boxed in orange and its two zeros are grayed. Below, det(M − λI) = (4 − λ) det[1 − λ, 2; 0, 3 − λ] = (4 − λ)(1 − λ)(3 − λ), and λ = 4, 1, 3 in orange." />

<Warning>
Don't row reduce $A - \lambda I$ to get the polynomial. Dividing by an entry like $2 - \lambda$ quietly assumes that it is not zero, which is exactly the case you are looking for. Use cofactor expansion, and row reduce only after $\lambda$ is a number. Also leave the polynomial factored when you can: multiplying it out to $-\lambda^3 + 8\lambda^2 - 19\lambda + 12$ only hides the roots.
</Warning>

## Multiplicity

Sometimes the same factor appears more than once. The next problem counts how often.

<Problem>
Find the eigenvalues of $B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
Row 1 of $B - \lambda I$ is $(2 - \lambda, 0, 0)$, so expand along it:

$$
\det(B - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)\begin{vmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 3 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = (2 - \lambda)\left[(3 - \lambda)^2 - 1\right].
$$

The bracket is a difference of squares, $(3 - \lambda)^2 - 1^2 = (3 - \lambda - 1)(3 - \lambda + 1) = (2 - \lambda)(4 - \lambda)$. So

$$
\det(B - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda)(4 - \lambda).
$$

The factor $(2 - \lambda)$ appears twice and $(4 - \lambda)$ appears once. Listed with repeats, the eigenvalues are $2, 2, 4$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="multiplicity" alt="The graph of det(B − λI) = (2 − λ)²(4 − λ) for λ from 0.5 to 5. The curve comes down, touches the λ-axis at 2 without crossing, rises to a small hump, then crosses the axis at 4 and falls. Orange rings mark 2, labelled multiplicity 2, and 4, labelled multiplicity 1." />

<Concept title="Algebraic multiplicity">
The **algebraic multiplicity** of an eigenvalue is the number of times its factor appears in the characteristic polynomial. For $B$, the eigenvalue $2$ has multiplicity $2$ and $4$ has multiplicity $1$. A repeated root shows up in the graph as a point where the curve touches the axis without crossing it.

An $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ eigenvalues when you count them with multiplicity and allow complex numbers, so it has at most $n$ different ones.
</Concept>
</Pair>

On Day 31 this count gets a partner, the number of independent eigenvectors for the same eigenvalue, and the two numbers can disagree.

For a triangular matrix, $T - \lambda I$ is still triangular, so the characteristic polynomial is the product of $(t_{ii} - \lambda)$ down the diagonal. Every diagonal entry is an eigenvalue, repeated as often as it appears. Set the diagonal below to match the dashed graph.

## Zero and invertibility

Setting $\lambda = 0$ in the characteristic polynomial gives $\det(A - 0I) = \det A$. So $0$ is a root exactly when $\det A = 0$.

<Concept title="Two more invertibility tests">
The Invertible Matrix Theorem gains two statements. For an $n \times n$ matrix $A$, each of these is equivalent to $A$ being invertible:

- $0$ is not an eigenvalue of $A$.
- $\det A \ne 0$.
</Concept>

<Choice
  question="The characteristic polynomial of a $3 \times 3$ matrix is $-\lambda(\lambda - 2)(\lambda + 5)$. Is the matrix invertible?"
  options={["Yes", "No"]}
  answer={1}
>
The factor $-\lambda$ makes $0$ a root, so $0$ is an eigenvalue and the matrix is not invertible. Setting $\lambda = 0$ in the polynomial gives $\det A = 0$ directly.
</Choice>

### Core

<Check>
Find the characteristic polynomial and the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Enter the smaller eigenvalue first. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #1–8.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-1, 5]} labels={["λ₁", "λ₂"]} />

<Hint>
Expand $(1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 8$ and factor the quadratic.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A - \lambda I) &= (1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 4 \cdot 2 \\
&= \lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 5 \\
&= (\lambda - 5)(\lambda + 1)
\end{aligned}
$$

The eigenvalues are $-1$ and $5$. The factor $\lambda + 1$ gives the root $-1$, not $1$. Check: $A(1, 1) = (5, 5)$, so $(1, 1)$ is an eigenvector for $5$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
A $5 \times 5$ matrix has characteristic polynomial $-\lambda^5 + 6\lambda^4 - 9\lambda^3$. Find the multiplicity of each eigenvalue. Is the matrix invertible? (Modeled on Lay §5.2, Example 4.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 2]} labels={["for 0", "for 3"]} />

<Hint>
Factor out the highest power of $\lambda$ you can, then factor what is left.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$$
-\lambda^5 + 6\lambda^4 - 9\lambda^3 = -\lambda^3(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 9) = -\lambda^3(\lambda - 3)^2
$$

The eigenvalue $0$ has multiplicity $3$ and the eigenvalue $3$ has multiplicity $2$, which accounts for all five. Since $0$ is an eigenvalue, the matrix is not invertible.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Let $C = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Find its eigenvalues, then find the eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$ whose third entry is $1$. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #9–14.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-1, 0, 1]} />

<Hint>
Column 2 of $C - \lambda I$ has two zeros. Expand down it.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(C - \lambda I) &= (3 - \lambda)\det\begin{bmatrix} 2 - \lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} \\
&= (3 - \lambda)\left[(2 - \lambda)^2 - 1\right] \\
&= (3 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)
\end{aligned}
$$

The eigenvalues are $3$ with multiplicity $2$ and $1$ with multiplicity $1$. For $\lambda = 1$:

$$
C - I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf x = x_3\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check: $C(-1, 0, 1) = (-1, 0, 1)$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the eigenvector for $\lambda = 2$ whose first entry is $1$. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #11.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 1, 0]} />

<Hint>
Row reduce $A - 2I$. Its third row gives $x_3$ right away.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Expanding along row 1 gives $\det(A - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)$, so $2$ is an eigenvalue. Then

$$
A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Row 3 says $x_3 = 0$, and then row 2 says $x_1 - x_2 = 0$. With $x_1 = 1$, the eigenvector is $(1, 1, 0)$. Check: $A(1, 1, 0) = (2, 2, 0)$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
For which $h$ does $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & h \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ have a repeated eigenvalue, and what is that eigenvalue? (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #17–18.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[-1, 4]} labels={["h", "λ"]} />

<Hint>
Write the characteristic polynomial. A quadratic $\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c$ has a repeated root when $b^2 - 4c = 0$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$$
\det(A - \lambda I) = (5 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - h = \lambda^2 - 8\lambda + (15 - h).
$$

A repeated root needs $64 - 4(15 - h) = 0$, so $4 + 4h = 0$ and $h = -1$. Then the polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 8\lambda + 16 = (\lambda - 4)^2$, and the repeated eigenvalue is $4$.
</Answer>
</Check>

### Challenge

<Check>
Find the eigenvalues of $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, listed with repeats from smallest to largest.

<VectorAnswer answer={[2, 2, 5]} labels={["first", "second", "third"]} />

<Hint>
Write $a = 3 - \lambda$, so $A - \lambda I$ has $a$ on the diagonal and $1$ everywhere else. Expand along row 1 and factor the cubic in $a$. Day 28's last challenge tells you one root in advance.
</Hint>

<Answer>
With $a = 3 - \lambda$, expand along row 1:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{vmatrix} a & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & a & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & a \end{vmatrix}
&= a(a^2 - 1) - 1(a - 1) + 1(1 - a) \\
&= a^3 - 3a + 2 = (a - 1)^2(a + 2).
\end{aligned}
$$

The factoring works because $a = 1$ is a root: $1 - 3 + 2 = 0$. Putting $a = 3 - \lambda$ back gives $(2 - \lambda)^2(5 - \lambda)$, so the eigenvalues are $2, 2, 5$. The root $5$ matches Day 28: every row sums to $5$, so $A(1, 1, 1) = (5, 5, 5)$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
A $3 \times 3$ matrix has characteristic polynomial $-\lambda^3 + 4\lambda^2 + \lambda - 4$. Find $\det A$, then all three eigenvalues from smallest to largest.

<VectorAnswer answer={[-4, -1, 1, 4]} labels={["det A", "λ₁", "λ₂", "λ₃"]} />

<Hint>
Set $\lambda = 0$ to get $\det A$. To factor, group the first two terms and the last two terms.
</Hint>

<Answer>
At $\lambda = 0$ the polynomial is $\det(A - 0I) = \det A = -4$. For the roots, group terms:

$$
-\lambda^3 + 4\lambda^2 + \lambda - 4 = -\lambda^2(\lambda - 4) + (\lambda - 4) = -(\lambda - 4)(\lambda^2 - 1) = -(\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1).
$$

The eigenvalues are $-1$, $1$ and $4$. Their product is $-4$, the same as $\det A$, which Day 30 explains.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Show that $A$ and $A^T$ have the same characteristic polynomial, so they have the same eigenvalues.

<Hint>
Take the transpose of $A - \lambda I$ and use Day 26's rule $\det M^T = \det M$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Transposing a sum transposes each piece, and $I^T = I$, so $(A - \lambda I)^T = A^T - \lambda I$. Day 26 says a matrix and its transpose have the same determinant, so

$$
\det(A^T - \lambda I) = \det\big((A - \lambda I)^T\big) = \det(A - \lambda I).
$$

The two characteristic polynomials are equal for every $\lambda$, so they have the same roots. The eigenvectors are usually different, though.
</Answer>
</Check>
