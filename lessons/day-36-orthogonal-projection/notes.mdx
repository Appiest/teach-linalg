Yesterday's dot product can tell when two vectors are perpendicular. Today it answers a distance question: which point of a line or a plane sits closest to a given vector $\mathbf y$? The question matters because of Day 38. When $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has no solution, the best you can do is find the point of $\operatorname{Col}A$ closest to $\mathbf b$ and solve for that point instead. You find the closest point by dropping a perpendicular, and the dot product tells you where it lands.

## Projection onto a line

A line is the simplest subspace, so start there. The goal is a multiple of the line's direction whose leftover piece is perpendicular to the line.

A teal arrow runs from the origin to ŷ = (3, 3) on L, and a pink arrow z runs from ŷ up to the tip of y. An orange square marks the right angle between z and L. A panel reads ŷ = (y·u)/(u·u) u = 6/2 u = (3, 3)." />

Let $L$ be the line through a nonzero vector $\green{\mathbf u}$. The **orthogonal projection** of $\yellow{\mathbf y}$ onto $L$ is the point of $L$ where the perpendicular from the tip of $\yellow{\mathbf y}$ lands:

$$
\teal{\hat{\mathbf y}} = \operatorname{proj}_L \mathbf y = \frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}\,\green{\mathbf u}.
$$

The vector $\pink{\mathbf z} = \yellow{\mathbf y} - \teal{\hat{\mathbf y}}$ is the **component of $\mathbf y$ orthogonal to $\mathbf u$**.
</Pair>

The weight comes from asking for $\mathbf z \cdot \mathbf u = 0$. If $\hat{\mathbf y} = c\,\mathbf u$, then $(\mathbf y - c\,\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf u = \mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u - c\,(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u)$, and that is zero only for $c = \frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u}{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u}$. The denominator is $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u = \|\mathbf u\|^2$, not $\|\mathbf u\|$. With the square, rescaling $\mathbf u$ leaves $\hat{\mathbf y}$ unchanged, so the projection depends only on the line.

In the video, $\mathbf y = (1, 5)$ and $\mathbf u = (1, 1)$ give $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u = 6$ and $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u = 2$, so $\hat{\mathbf y} = 3\mathbf u = (3, 3)$ and $\mathbf z = (-2, 2)$. This is Day 17's matrix $P$ at work, since $P\mathbf y = (3, 3)$ too.

## Projection onto a subspace

For a bigger subspace $W$, the goal is to split $\mathbf y$ into a piece inside $W$ and a piece in Day 35's complement $W^\perp$. When $W$ has an orthogonal basis, you project onto each basis line separately and add the pieces. Each term is the same ratio Day 35 used to find coordinates in an orthogonal basis.

<Definition term="The orthogonal decomposition theorem">
Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$. Every $\mathbf y$ in $\mathbb R^n$ can be written in exactly one way as

$$
\yellow{\mathbf y} = \teal{\hat{\mathbf y}} + \pink{\mathbf z}, \qquad \teal{\hat{\mathbf y}} \in W, \quad \pink{\mathbf z} \in W^\perp.
$$

If $\{\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_p\}$ is any orthogonal basis of $W$, then

$$
\teal{\hat{\mathbf y}} = \operatorname{proj}_W \mathbf y = \frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_1}{\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_1}\,\mathbf u_1 + \cdots + \frac{\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_p}{\mathbf u_p\cdot\mathbf u_p}\,\mathbf u_p.
$$
</Definition>

The formula works because dotting $\mathbf z = \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}$ with $\mathbf u_1$ kills every term except the first, since $\mathbf u_1 \cdot \mathbf u_j = 0$ for $j \ne 1$, and the first term cancels $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_1$ exactly. The split is unique because a vector in both $W$ and $W^\perp$ is perpendicular to itself, and only $\mathbf 0$ is. So every orthogonal basis of $W$ gives the same $\hat{\mathbf y}$.

Here is the video's example, with $\green{\mathbf u_1} = (1, 1, 0)$, $\red{\mathbf u_2} = (-1, 1, 1)$ and $\yellow{\mathbf y} = (2, 2, 3)$. First check $\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2 = -1 + 1 + 0 = 0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\teal{\hat{\mathbf y}} &= \tfrac{4}{2}\,\green{\mathbf u_1} + \tfrac{3}{3}\,\red{\mathbf u_2} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
\pink{\mathbf z} &= \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \\
\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_1 &= 1 - 1 + 0 = 0, \qquad \mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_2 = -1 - 1 + 2 = 0
\end{aligned}
$$
</Pair>

The last line is the check to do every time. If $\mathbf z$ is not perpendicular to every basis vector, some arithmetic went wrong.

The formula needs the basis to be orthogonal. In the widget below, $W$ is the whole plane $\mathbb R^2$, so the correct projection of $\mathbf y$ is $\mathbf y$ itself. The formula only gets there when the two basis vectors are perpendicular.

## The closest point

<Definition term="The best approximation theorem">
Let $W$ be a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$ and let $\hat{\mathbf y} = \operatorname{proj}_W \mathbf y$. Then $\hat{\mathbf y}$ is the closest point in $W$ to $\mathbf y$:

$$
\|\mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}\| < \|\mathbf y - \mathbf v\| \quad \text{for every } \mathbf v \text{ in } W \text{ other than } \hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

The **distance from $\mathbf y$ to $W$** is $\|\mathbf z\| = \|\mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y}\|$.
</Definition>

This theorem is the reason the course studies projection. On Day 38 it is applied with $W = \operatorname{Col}A$ to find the best answer to a system with no exact solution.

<Pair>
<Figure src="closest-point" alt="The same plane W, with y, ŷ and the pink z. A blue point v sits elsewhere in W, joined to y by a blue segment and to ŷ by a dashed teal segment, which makes a right triangle with its right angle at ŷ. The panel reads ‖y − v‖² = ‖z‖² + ‖ŷ − v‖², and below it 11.25 = 6 + 5.25." />

For any $\mathbf v$ in $W$, split the trip from $\mathbf v$ to $\mathbf y$ at $\hat{\mathbf y}$. The piece $\hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf v$ lies in $W$ and the piece $\mathbf z$ is perpendicular to $W$, so Pythagoras gives

$$
\blue{\|\mathbf y - \mathbf v\|^2} = \pink{\|\mathbf z\|^2} + \teal{\|\hat{\mathbf y} - \mathbf v\|^2}.
$$

The last term is positive unless $\mathbf v = \hat{\mathbf y}$. In the example, $\|\mathbf z\|^2 = 6$, so the distance from $\mathbf y$ to $W$ is $\sqrt 6 \approx 2.45$.
</Pair>

## The projection matrix

If the basis is **orthonormal**, every $\mathbf u_j\cdot\mathbf u_j = 1$ and the denominators disappear. Put the basis vectors in the columns of $U$. The weights $\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_j$ are the entries of $U^T\mathbf y$, and multiplying by $U$ combines the columns with those weights, so

$$
\operatorname{proj}_W \mathbf y = (\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_1)\,\mathbf u_1 + \cdots + (\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u_p)\,\mathbf u_p = UU^T\mathbf y.
$$

For the line $L$ through $(1, 1)$, the unit vector is $\mathbf u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$. Then

$$
U^TU = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad UU^T = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},
$$

and $UU^T$ is exactly Day 17's projection $P$. A tall $U$ with orthonormal columns always has $U^TU = I$, but $UU^T$ is a projection. It equals $I$ only when $U$ is square. On Day 37 the same fact $Q^TQ = I$ for a matrix $Q$ with orthonormal columns is what makes the QR factorization easy to compute.

Every formula today needed an orthogonal basis, so tomorrow Gram–Schmidt builds one from any basis, and on Day 38 projection onto $\operatorname{Col}A$ becomes the method of least squares.

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf y\cdot\mathbf u &= 3 + 7 = 10, \qquad \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u = 9 + 1 = 10 \\
\hat{\mathbf y} &= \tfrac{10}{10}\,\mathbf u = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf z = \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: $\hat{\mathbf y}\cdot\mathbf z = -6 + 6 = 0$. The distance from $\mathbf y$ to the line is $\|\mathbf z\| = \sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$.
</Answer>

<Answer>
The basis is orthogonal because $\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2 = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0$.

$$
\begin{aligned}

\mathbf z &= \mathbf y - \hat{\mathbf y} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_1 = 2 - 4 + 2 = 0, \qquad \mathbf z\cdot\mathbf u_2 = 4 - 2 - 2 = 0
