Yesterday's lesson found eigenvalues as the roots of $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. Today asks what those numbers tell you once you have them. Without any row reduction, the eigenvalues of $A$ give its trace and determinant. They also give the eigenvalues of $A^2$, $A^{-1}$ and $A + cI$, and they predict where $A^k\mathbf x$ points once $k$ is large. That last fact is the reason the course studies eigenvalues at all, and Days 32 and 33 build on it to compute powers of a matrix quickly. The video uses $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, which stretches the $\yellow{\text{yellow line}}$ through $(1, 1)$ by $3$ and the $\blue{\text{blue line}}$ through $(1, -1)$ by $1$.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. Most of them need nothing more than $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$ and a little algebra.

## Trace and determinant

<Problem>
The video's matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\yellow 3$ and $\blue 1$. Add the diagonal entries of $A$ and compute $\det A$. How do these two numbers compare with the eigenvalues?

<Solution>
The diagonal entries add to $2 + 2 = 4$, and the eigenvalues add to $3 + 1 = 4$.

The determinant is $2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 3$, and the eigenvalues multiply to $3 \cdot 1 = 3$.

To see why, expand the characteristic polynomial for any $2 \times 2$ matrix and compare it with its factored form. Expanding gives

$$
\det\begin{bmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc).
$$

If the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, the same polynomial factors as

$$
(\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2.
$$

The two lines are the same polynomial, so their middle coefficients match, $a + d = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$, and their constant terms match, $ad - bc = \lambda_1\lambda_2$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Figure src="trace-determinant" alt="The matrix A = [2 1; 1 2] with its diagonal entries in orange, beside three lines: tr A = 2 + 2 = 3 + 1, det A = 2·2 − 1·1 = 3·1, and det(A − λI) = λ² − 4λ + 3, with the 4 and the 3 in orange. The eigenvalue 3 is yellow and the eigenvalue 1 is blue." wide />

<Definition term="Trace">
The **trace** of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries, $\operatorname{tr}A = a_{11} + a_{22} + \cdots + a_{nn}$. If $A$ is $n \times n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, listed with their multiplicities, then

$$
\begin{gathered}
\operatorname{tr}A = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots + \lambda_n \\
\det A = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n
\end{gathered}
$$
</Definition>

The count has to include repeated eigenvalues, and complex ones too when the characteristic polynomial has no real roots. For any size, setting $\lambda = 0$ in $\det(A - \lambda I) = (\lambda_1 - \lambda)\cdots(\lambda_n - \lambda)$ gives $\det A = \lambda_1 \cdots \lambda_n$ directly.

<Pair>
<Figure src="area-triples" alt="Two grids side by side. On the left, a small yellow parallelogram sits on the arrows (1, 1) and (1, −1) along the yellow and blue lines, labelled area = 2. After an arrow labelled A, the grid is slanted and the parallelogram is three times as long along the yellow line, labelled area = 6." />

The determinant also has a picture. Build a box on one eigenvector from each line. $A$ triples the yellow side and keeps the blue side, so the box's area grows by $3 \cdot 1 = 3$. Since $A$ scales every area by $\det A$ (Day 27), that means $\det A = 3$.
</Pair>

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, the trace and determinant pin down the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A$. Find two roots whose curve matches it.

The trace is especially handy when you already know all but one eigenvalue.

<Problem>
Day 29 showed that $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue $2$ twice. Find the third eigenvalue without factoring anything, then check it with the determinant. (Modeled on Lay §5.1, Example 4.)

<Solution>
The trace is $3 + 3 + 3 = 9$, and it equals the sum of all three eigenvalues:

$$
2 + 2 + \lambda_3 = 9, \qquad \lambda_3 = 5.
$$

For the check, the determinant should be the product $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = 20$. Expanding along row 1,

$$
\det A = 3(9 - 1) - 1(3 - 1) + 1(1 - 3) = 24 - 2 - 2 = 20.
$$

The two agree. Every row of $A$ also adds up to $5$, so $A(1, 1, 1) = 5(1, 1, 1)$ confirms the answer a third way.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
Count repeated eigenvalues as often as they repeat. Writing $2 + 5 = 9 - \lambda$ with $2$ counted once gives the wrong third eigenvalue.
</Warning>

## Powers and shifts

Once you know $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$, several related matrices act on the same $\mathbf v$ in a predictable way.

<Problem>
For the video's $A$, the vector $\yellow{\mathbf v} = (1, 1)$ satisfies $A\mathbf v = 3\mathbf v$. Without writing down $A^2$, $A^{-1}$ or $A - 2I$, find $A^2\mathbf v$, $A^{-1}\mathbf v$ and $(A - 2I)\mathbf v$.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
For $A^2\mathbf v$, apply $A$ twice. The first application gives $3\mathbf v$, and the second multiplies by $3$ again: $A^2\mathbf v = A(3\mathbf v) = 3A\mathbf v = 9\mathbf v = (9, 9)$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $A^{-1}\mathbf v$, start from $A\mathbf v = 3\mathbf v$ and apply $A^{-1}$ to both sides. That gives $\mathbf v = 3A^{-1}\mathbf v$, so $A^{-1}\mathbf v = \tfrac13\mathbf v = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13)$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $(A - 2I)\mathbf v$, distribute: $A\mathbf v - 2\mathbf v = 3\mathbf v - 2\mathbf v = 1\mathbf v = (1, 1)$.
</Step>
</Steps>

In all three, $\mathbf v$ stays an eigenvector, and only the factor changes: $3^2$, $\tfrac13$ and $3 - 2$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Same eigenvectors, new eigenvalues">
If $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$, then

$$
\begin{gathered}
A^k\mathbf v = \lambda^k\mathbf v \\
A^{-1}\mathbf v = \tfrac{1}{\lambda}\mathbf v \\
(A + cI)\mathbf v = (\lambda + c)\mathbf v \\
(cA)\mathbf v = (c\lambda)\mathbf v
\end{gathered}
$$

The rule for $A^{-1}$ needs $A$ to be invertible, and then $\lambda \ne 0$ by Day 28, so $\tfrac{1}{\lambda}$ makes sense.
</Concept>

<Figure src="same-lines" alt="Three grids labelled A, A inverse and A − 2I. Each shows the same yellow line through (1, 1) and blue line through (1, −1), with faint arrows at (1.5, 1.5) and (1.5, −1.5) and bright arrows where each matrix sends them. The stretch factors read ×3 and ×1 under A, ×1/3 and ×1 under A inverse, and ×1 and ×−1 under A − 2I, where the blue arrow points the other way." wide />

Here $A$ doubles the yellow line and flips the blue one. Slide the power $k$, including negative powers, which mean powers of $A^{-1}$.

<PowerLineStretch pairs={[{ vector: [2, 2], value: 2 }, { vector: [2, -2], value: -1 }]} factor={0.25} min={-3} max={1} />

The shift rule makes a clean invertibility test. $A + cI$ fails to be invertible exactly when one of its eigenvalues, $\lambda + c$, is zero.

<ShiftToSingular matrix={[[1, 6], [5, 2]]} eigenvalues={[7, -4]} />

<Warning>
These rules only run one way, and only for one matrix at a time. An eigenvector of $A$ is always an eigenvector of $A^2$, but $A^2$ can have extra ones: the swap matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has only two eigenlines, while its square is $I$, for which every nonzero vector is an eigenvector. Also, the eigenvalues of $A + B$ are usually not sums of eigenvalues of $A$ and $B$.
</Warning>

## Similar matrices

On Day 22, $B = P^{-1}AP$ described the same map as $A$ in the basis made of the columns of $P$. Matrices related this way are called **similar**. Since they describe the same map, we expect them to share whatever depends only on the map.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $B = P^{-1}AP$. Compare the eigenvalues, trace and determinant of $A$ and $B$.

<Solution>
Day 7's formula gives $P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, since $\det P = 1$. Multiply from the right first:

$$
\begin{aligned}
AP &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \\
P^{-1}(AP) &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Both $A$ and $B$ are triangular with diagonal $1, 3$, so both have eigenvalues $1$ and $3$, trace $4$ and determinant $3$. The matrix $B$ is simpler because the second column of $P$, $(1, 1)$, happens to be an eigenvector of $A$ for $\lambda = 3$. Day 32 makes that choice on purpose.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Theorem">
If $B = P^{-1}AP$ for an invertible $P$, then $A$ and $B$ have the same characteristic polynomial. So they have the same eigenvalues with the same multiplicities, and the same trace and determinant.
</Definition>

The proof writes $\lambda I$ as $P^{-1}(\lambda I)P$ so that $P^{-1}$ and $P$ can be pulled out, then uses Day 27's product rule and $\det(P^{-1})\det P = \det I = 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(B - \lambda I) &= \det\bigl(P^{-1}(A - \lambda I)P\bigr) \\
&= \det(P^{-1})\det(A - \lambda I)\det P \\
&= \det(A - \lambda I).
\end{aligned}
$$

<Warning>
The reverse does not hold. The matrices $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $2I$ both have characteristic polynomial $(\lambda - 2)^2$, but $P^{-1}(2I)P = 2I$ for every $P$, so the only matrix similar to $2I$ is $2I$ itself. Row reduction does not preserve eigenvalues either, because a row operation multiplies $A$ on one side only.
</Warning>

On Day 33 this theorem lets you study a map in whichever basis makes its matrix simplest without changing its eigenvalues.

## Independent eigenvectors

The last section of this lesson splits a vector into eigenvector pieces, and that only works when the eigenvectors form a basis. The next problem shows why eigenvectors for different eigenvalues can never line up.

<Problem>
Suppose $A\mathbf v_1 = 3\mathbf v_1$ and $A\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf v_2$ with both vectors nonzero, and suppose $c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0$. Show that $c_1 = c_2 = 0$.

<Solution>
We want to get rid of one vector so that we can read off one weight. Multiplying by $A$ changes the two pieces by different factors, which is the natural lever.

<Steps>
<Step>
Apply $A$ to the equation: $3c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0$.
</Step>
<Step>
Subtract the original equation $c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0$. The $\mathbf v_2$ terms cancel and leave $2c_1\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf 0$.
</Step>
<Step>
Since $\mathbf v_1 \ne \mathbf 0$, $c_1 = 0$. Then the original equation says $c_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0$, and since $\mathbf v_2 \ne \mathbf 0$, $c_2 = 0$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The factor $2$ in the second step is $3 - 1$, the difference of the eigenvalues. The argument works whenever that difference is not zero.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Theorem">
Eigenvectors $\mathbf v_1, \dots, \mathbf v_r$ that belong to distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_r$ are linearly independent.
</Definition>

The general proof repeats the problem's step on the first vector that would depend on the earlier ones. One consequence is that an $n \times n$ matrix has at most $n$ distinct eigenvalues, since $\mathbb R^n$ holds at most $n$ independent vectors. Day 32 uses this theorem to show that an $n \times n$ matrix with $n$ distinct eigenvalues can be diagonalized.

<Choice
  question="A $3 \times 3$ matrix has eigenvalues $1$, $2$ and $5$. Must its three eigenvectors form a basis of $\mathbb R^3$?"
  options={["Yes", "No, they could lie in a plane"]}
  answer={0}
>
The three eigenvalues are distinct, so one eigenvector for each gives three independent vectors in $\mathbb R^3$, which is a basis. The converse fails, though: $I$ has only the eigenvalue $1$, yet every basis is made of its eigenvectors.
</Choice>

## Repeated multiplication

The goal here is to find $A^k\mathbf x_0$ without multiplying by $A$ a full $k$ times. The trick is to split the start into eigenvector pieces, since each piece only gets scaled.

<Problem>
For the video's $A$, find $A^k\mathbf x_0$ for $\mathbf x_0 = (1, 0)$. Which line does $A^k\mathbf x_0$ point along when $k$ is large?

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Split $\mathbf x_0$ into eigenvectors. We need $c_1(1, 1) + c_2(1, -1) = (1, 0)$, so $c_1 + c_2 = 1$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 0$. That gives $c_1 = c_2 = \tfrac12$.
</Step>
<Step>
Apply $A^k$ to each piece separately. The yellow piece gets $3^k$ and the blue piece gets $1^k = 1$:

$$
A^k\mathbf x_0 = \tfrac12 \cdot 3^k\yellow{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + \tfrac12\blue{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}}.
$$
</Step>
<Step>
For $k = 2$ this is $\tfrac92(1, 1) + \tfrac12(1, -1) = (5, 4)$. Multiplying directly, $A(1, 0) = (2, 1)$ and $A(2, 1) = (5, 4)$, which agrees.
</Step>
<Step>
The yellow piece triples at every step while the blue piece stays the same size. So for large $k$ the yellow piece dominates, and $A^k\mathbf x_0$ points almost exactly along the yellow line through $(1, 1)$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

Try the first step yourself with a different start.

<Pair>
<Figure src="swing" alt="The yellow line through (1, 1) and the blue line through (1, −1). Seven teal arrows of equal length fan out from the origin: x0 points right along the x-axis, x1 and x2 point higher, and the later ones crowd against the yellow line, where the last arrow ends in an orange dot." />

<Concept title="Iterates follow the biggest eigenvalue">
When $\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2$ are independent eigenvectors, every start splits as $\mathbf x_0 = c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2$, and each piece picks up its own power:

$$
\mathbf x_k = A^k\mathbf x_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\yellow{\mathbf v_1} + c_2\lambda_2^k\blue{\mathbf v_2}.
$$

So $\mathbf x_k$ turns toward the line of the eigenvalue with the largest absolute value, unless the start has no piece on that line.
</Concept>
</Pair>

<Check>
A $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ has eigenvalues $2$, $-1$ and $3$. Find $\operatorname{tr}A$, $\det A$ and $\det(A + 4I)$. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #19.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[4, -6, 126]} labels={["tr A", "det A", "det(A + 4I)"]} />

<Hint>
The eigenvalues of $A + 4I$ are the eigenvalues of $A$ shifted by $4$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{tr}A &= 2 + (-1) + 3 = 4 \\
\det A &= 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot 3 = -6 \\
\det(A + 4I) &= 6 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 = 126
\end{aligned}
$$

The same reasoning gives the eigenvalues $4, 1, 9$ for $A^2$ and $\tfrac12, -1, \tfrac13$ for $A^{-1}$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Find $\det A$ and $\det B$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Can $A$ and $B$ be similar? (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #24.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 4]} labels={["det A", "det B"]} />

<Hint>
Both matrices are triangular. Similar matrices share their determinant.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Both matrices are triangular, so $\det A = 1 \cdot 3 = 3$ and $\det B = 2 \cdot 2 = 4$. Similar matrices have equal determinants, so $A$ and $B$ are not similar, even though both have trace $4$. Their eigenvalues also differ: $A$ has $1$ and $3$, while $B$ has $2$ twice.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
A $3 \times 3$ matrix has eigenvalues $2$ and $-1$ and one more, and its trace is $5$. Find the third eigenvalue and $\det A$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[4, -8]} labels={["λ₃", "det A"]} />

<Hint>
The trace is the sum of all three eigenvalues, and the determinant is their product.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$2 + (-1) + \lambda_3 = 5$ gives $\lambda_3 = 4$. Then $\det A = 2 \cdot (-1) \cdot 4 = -8$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvalue $3$ on $(1, 1)$ and $-1$ on $(1, -1)$, and let $\mathbf x_0 = (3, 1)$. Use the eigenvectors to find $\mathbf x_3 = A^3\mathbf x_0$. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #25.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[53, 55]} />

<Hint>
First write $(3, 1) = c_1(1, 1) + c_2(1, -1)$. Then each piece gets its own eigenvalue cubed.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf x_0 &= 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \\
\mathbf x_k &= 2 \cdot 3^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + (-1)^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \\

</Answer>

<Check>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.2 & 0.6 \end{bmatrix}$, which has eigenvalue $1$ on $(2, 1)$ and eigenvalue $0.4$ on $(1, -1)$. For $\mathbf x_0 = (1, 2)$, find $\mathbf x_1 = A\mathbf x_0$ and the vector that $\mathbf x_k$ approaches as $k$ grows. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #25.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[1.6, 1.4, 2, 1]} labels={["x₁ top", "x₁ bottom", "limit top", "limit bottom"]} />

<Hint>
Split $(1, 2)$ into the two eigenvectors. What happens to $0.4^k$ when $k$ is large?
</Hint>

<Answer>
Solve $c_1(2, 1) + c_2(1, -1) = (1, 2)$: the equations $2c_1 + c_2 = 1$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 2$ add to $3c_1 = 3$, so $c_1 = 1$ and $c_2 = -1$. Then

$$
\mathbf x_k = 1^k\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - 0.4^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.

At $k = 1$, $\mathbf x_1 = (2 - 0.4,\ 1 + 0.4) = (1.6, 1.4)$, which matches $A(1, 2)$. As $k$ grows, $0.4^k \to 0$, so $\mathbf x_k \to (2, 1)$. The eigenvalue $1$ piece survives and the smaller piece fades away.
</Answer>

### Challenge

<Check>
Let $N = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Both $N$ and $N^T$ have only the eigenvalue $0$. Find the eigenvalues of $N + N^T$, smaller first.

<VectorAnswer answer={[-1, 1]} labels={["λ₁", "λ₂"]} />

<Hint>
Write out $N + N^T$. You have seen it before in Day 28's reflection problem.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$N + N^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the reflection across $y = x$. Its trace is $0$ and its determinant is $-1$, so $\lambda^2 - 1 = 0$ and the eigenvalues are $-1$ and $1$. The eigenvalues of a sum are not the sums of the eigenvalues, which would all be $0$ here.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Show that if $A^2$ is the zero matrix, then the only eigenvalue $A$ can have is $0$. (Modeled on Lay §5.1 #26.)

<Hint>
Start from $A\mathbf x = \lambda\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0$ and apply $A$ once more.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Suppose $A\mathbf x = \lambda\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0$. The power rule gives $A^2\mathbf x = \lambda^2\mathbf x$. But $A^2$ is the zero matrix, so $\lambda^2\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$. Since $\mathbf x \ne \mathbf 0$, $\lambda^2 = 0$, so $\lambda = 0$. The matrix $N$ from the previous check is an example: $N^2 = 0$ and its only eigenvalue is $0$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Suppose $A = QR$ with $Q$ invertible. Show that $RQ$ is similar to $A$, so $RQ$ has the same eigenvalues as $A$. (Modeled on Lay §5.2 #23.)

<Hint>
Solve $A = QR$ for $R$, then substitute into $RQ$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Multiplying $A = QR$ on the left by $Q^{-1}$ gives $R = Q^{-1}A$. Then

$$
RQ = Q^{-1}AQ,
$$

which is $P^{-1}AP$ with $P = Q$. So $RQ$ is similar to $A$ and has the same characteristic polynomial. Computer programs find eigenvalues by repeating this swap many times, because each step keeps the eigenvalues while making the matrix closer to triangular.
</Answer>
</Check>
