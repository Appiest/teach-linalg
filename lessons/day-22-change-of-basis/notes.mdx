On Day 15 a basis $\mathcal B$ gave every vector a coordinate vector $[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$, and on Day 21 a pair of bases turned a linear map into a matrix. Both results depend on which basis you pick, so today asks how to switch: given a vector's coordinates in one basis, how do you find them in another, and how does a map's matrix change along with them? The question matters because a well-chosen basis can make a map's matrix much simpler, and finding that basis is the main job of Days 28 to 33. Today puts two bases on the same space at once. The vector stays exactly where it is, and a single matrix translates its address from one grid's language into the other's.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway.

## Two addresses for one vector

<Problem>
Take two bases of $\mathbb R^2$. The first is $\mathcal B = \{\green{\mathbf b_1}, \red{\mathbf b_2}\}$ with $\green{\mathbf b_1} = (2, 1)$ and $\red{\mathbf b_2} = (-1, 2)$. The second is $\mathcal C = \{\pink{\mathbf c_1}, \blue{\mathbf c_2}\}$ with $\pink{\mathbf c_1} = (1, 0)$ and $\blue{\mathbf c_2} = (-1, 1)$. A vector has $[\yellow{\mathbf x}]_{\mathcal B} = (2, 1)$. Find $[\yellow{\mathbf x}]_{\mathcal C}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[7, 4]} />

<Solution>
The $\mathcal B$-address tells us how to build $\mathbf x$, so the natural first move is to build it and see which arrow it is:

$$
\yellow{\mathbf x} = 2\green{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + 1\red{\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 1 \\ 2 + 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Now we want weights $a$ and $b$ with $a\,\pink{\mathbf c_1} + b\,\blue{\mathbf c_2} = (3, 4)$. Written out, that is $a(1, 0) + b(-1, 1) = (a - b,\ b)$. The second entry says $b = 4$, and then the first entry says $a - 4 = 3$, so $a = 7$.

So $[\yellow{\mathbf x}]_{\mathcal C} = (7, 4)$. The same arrow $(3, 4)$ has the address $(2, 1)$ on one grid and $(7, 4)$ on the other.
</Solution>
</Problem>

The yellow vector x = (3, 4) is reached by two green steps and one red step, and also by seven pink steps and four blue steps, with a dot at every step. Beside it, [x]_B = (2, 1) and [x]_C = (7, 4)." wide />

<Concept title="Coordinates name a vector relative to a grid">
Changing the basis never moves a vector. It only changes the list of weights that describe it. Neither $(2, 1)$ nor $(7, 4)$ is more correct than the other, and each one only makes sense once you know which grid it was read from.
</Concept>

## The change-of-coordinates matrix

Problem 1 went through standard coordinates, which works in $\mathbb R^2$ but needs actual vectors. The goal of this section is a single matrix that turns $[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$ into $[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal C}$ directly, for every $\mathbf x$.

<Problem>
Use the same two bases. First find $[\green{\mathbf b_1}]_{\mathcal C}$ and $[\red{\mathbf b_2}]_{\mathcal C}$. Then use them, and no standard coordinates, to redo Problem 1.

<Solution>
The idea is to translate the two building blocks once, and then let linearity translate everything built from them.

<Steps>
<Step>
Write $\green{\mathbf b_1} = (2, 1)$ in $\mathcal C$. We need $a - b = 2$ and $b = 1$, so $a = 3$. That gives $\green{\mathbf b_1} = 3\pink{\mathbf c_1} + \blue{\mathbf c_2}$ and $[\green{\mathbf b_1}]_{\mathcal C} = (3, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Write $\red{\mathbf b_2} = (-1, 2)$ in $\mathcal C$. We need $a - b = -1$ and $b = 2$, so $a = 1$. That gives $\red{\mathbf b_2} = \pink{\mathbf c_1} + 2\blue{\mathbf c_2}$ and $[\red{\mathbf b_2}]_{\mathcal C} = (1, 2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Since $\yellow{\mathbf x} = 2\green{\mathbf b_1} + \red{\mathbf b_2}$, and taking coordinates is linear (Day 15), the $\mathcal C$-address of $\mathbf x$ is the same combination of the two translated blocks: $\;[\yellow{\mathbf x}]_{\mathcal C} = 2\green{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} + 1\red{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
A combination of columns is a matrix times a vector: $\;\begin{bmatrix} \green{3} & \red{1} \\ \green{1} & \red{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \pink{7} \\ \blue{4} \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The answer matches Problem 1. The matrix in the last step works for any $\mathbf x$, because only the weights $2$ and $1$ came from this particular vector.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Change-of-coordinates matrix">
Let $\mathcal B = \{\mathbf b_1, \dots, \mathbf b_n\}$ and $\mathcal C = \{\mathbf c_1, \dots, \mathbf c_n\}$ be bases of a vector space $V$. There is exactly one $n \times n$ matrix $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ with

$$
[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal C} = P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}\,[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B} \quad \text{for every } \mathbf x \text{ in } V,
$$

and its columns are the $\mathcal C$-coordinate vectors of the vectors in $\mathcal B$:

$$
P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B} = \big[\,[\mathbf b_1]_{\mathcal C}\ \ [\mathbf b_2]_{\mathcal C}\ \ \cdots\ \ [\mathbf b_n]_{\mathcal C}\,\big].
$$
</Definition>

Read the subscript from right to left. The matrix takes $\mathcal B$-coordinates in on the right and hands $\mathcal C$-coordinates out on the left. It is also Day 21's matrix $[T]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ for the identity map $T(\mathbf x) = \mathbf x$, since its columns are $[T(\mathbf b_j)]_{\mathcal C} = [\mathbf b_j]_{\mathcal C}$.

Beside them, b1 = 3c1 + 1c2, b2 = 1c1 + 2c2 and P from B to C with a green column (3, 1) and a red column (1, 2)." />

<Choice
  question="A matrix $P$ has columns $[\mathbf u_1]_{\mathcal W}$ and $[\mathbf u_2]_{\mathcal W}$, where $\mathcal U$ and $\mathcal W$ are bases of $V$. Which equation holds for every $\mathbf x$?"
  options={["$[\\mathbf x]_{\\mathcal U} = P\\,[\\mathbf x]_{\\mathcal W}$", "$[\\mathbf x]_{\\mathcal W} = P\\,[\\mathbf x]_{\\mathcal U}$"]}
  answer={1}
>
The columns are $\mathcal W$-coordinate vectors, so $P$ hands out $\mathcal W$-coordinates. Test it on $\mathbf x = \mathbf u_1$: its $\mathcal U$-address is $(1, 0)$, and $P(1, 0)$ is the first column, $[\mathbf u_1]_{\mathcal W}$, which is correct. So $P = P_{\mathcal W \leftarrow \mathcal U}$.
</Choice>

<Warning>
The columns of $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ are the $\mathbf b$'s written in $\mathcal C$, never the $\mathbf c$'s written in $\mathcal B$. A quick test catches the mistake: $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ times $(1, 0)$ must give $[\mathbf b_1]_{\mathcal C}$, because $(1, 0)$ is the $\mathcal B$-address of $\mathbf b_1$.
</Warning>

## The reverse translation

<Problem>
A vector $\mathbf y$ has $[\mathbf y]_{\mathcal C} = (4, 3)$ for the same two bases. Find $[\mathbf y]_{\mathcal B}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 1]} />

<Solution>
We know how to go from $\mathcal B$ to $\mathcal C$: multiply by $P = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Here we know the output and want the input, so we solve $P\,\mathbf z = (4, 3)$.

The matrix is $2 \times 2$ with determinant $3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5$, so Day 7's formula gives its inverse:

$$
\mathbf z = \frac15\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac15\begin{bmatrix} 8 - 3 \\ -4 + 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

To check, $\mathbf b_1 + \mathbf b_2 = (1, 3)$ and $4\mathbf c_1 + 3\mathbf c_2 = (4 - 3,\ 3) = (1, 3)$. Both addresses describe the same arrow.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Going back is the inverse">
The columns of $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ are coordinate vectors of the independent set $\mathcal B$, so they are independent too. A square matrix with independent columns is invertible (Day 19), so the reverse translation always exists:

$$
P_{\mathcal B \leftarrow \mathcal C} = \big(P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}\big)^{-1}.
$$
</Concept>

Find the reverse address by moving the walk until it lands.

## Polynomial bases

The same idea works in $\mathbb P_2$, where the standard basis $\mathcal E = \{1, t, t^2\}$ plays the role of the ordinary grid.

<Problem>
Let $\mathcal B = \{1,\ 1 + t,\ 1 + t + t^2\}$. Find $[3 - 2t + 5t^2]_{\mathcal B}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[5, -7, 5]} />

<Solution>
Translating into $\mathcal E$ is easy, because the $\mathcal E$-coordinates of a polynomial are its coefficients. So we build $P_{\mathcal E \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ and then solve backward, as in Problem 3.

<Steps>
<Step>
Each column of $P_{\mathcal E \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ is the coefficient list of one $\mathcal B$ polynomial, constant term on top. The columns are $(1, 0, 0)$, $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(1, 1, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
We want $\mathbf y$ with $P_{\mathcal E \leftarrow \mathcal B}\,\mathbf y = (3, -2, 5)$: $\;\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf y = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
The matrix is triangular, so back substitution works from the bottom. Row 3 says $y_3 = 5$. Row 2 says $y_2 + 5 = -2$, so $y_2 = -7$. Row 1 says $y_1 - 7 + 5 = 3$, so $y_1 = 5$.
</Step>
<Step>
Check by expanding: $\;5 - 7(1 + t) + 5(1 + t + t^2) = (5 - 7 + 5) + (-7 + 5)t + 5t^2 = 3 - 2t + 5t^2$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
The coefficient lists go in as columns, one column per $\mathcal B$ polynomial. Entering them as rows gives the transpose, which translates in neither direction.
</Warning>

## Row reduction

Solving for one column at a time gets slow in $\mathbb R^3$ and beyond, so this section aims to produce every column of the matrix with one computation.

<Problem>
Let $\green{\mathbf b_1} = (1, 2)$, $\red{\mathbf b_2} = (3, 1)$, $\pink{\mathbf c_1} = (1, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf c_2} = (1, 2)$. Find $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[0, 5, 1, -2]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
Column $j$ is $[\mathbf b_j]_{\mathcal C}$, the weights $\mathbf y$ with $[\,\pink{\mathbf c_1}\ \blue{\mathbf c_2}\,]\,\mathbf y = \mathbf b_j$. Both systems have the same coefficient matrix, so we can row reduce them side by side in one augmented matrix.

<Steps>
<Step>
Put the $\mathbf c$'s on the left and both $\mathbf b$'s on the right: $\;\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 & 1 \end{array}\right]$.
</Step>
<Step>
Clear the entry below the first pivot with $R_2 - R_1$: $\;\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right]$.
</Step>
<Step>
Clear the entry above the second pivot with $R_1 - R_2$: $\;\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 0 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 \end{array}\right]$.
</Step>
<Step>
The left side is now $I$, so the right side holds the two solutions as columns: $\;P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} \green{0} & \red{5} \\ \green{1} & \red{-2} \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

To check column 2, compute $5\,\pink{\mathbf c_1} - 2\,\blue{\mathbf c_2} = (5 - 2,\ 5 - 4) = (3, 1) = \red{\mathbf b_2}$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="The target basis goes on the left">
For bases of $\mathbb R^n$, one row reduction produces the whole matrix:

$$
[\,\mathbf c_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf c_n \mid \mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,] \sim [\,I \mid P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}\,].
$$

The standard basis gives a shortcut as well. The matrix $P_{\mathcal B} = [\,\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,]$ from Day 15 is $P_{\mathcal E \leftarrow \mathcal B}$, so translating $\mathcal B \to \mathcal E \to \mathcal C$ gives $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B} = P_{\mathcal C}^{-1}P_{\mathcal B}$.
</Concept>

<Choice
  question="You row reduce $[\,\mathbf b_1\ \mathbf b_2 \mid \mathbf c_1\ \mathbf c_2\,]$ to $[\,I \mid Q\,]$. What is $Q$?"
  options={["$P_{\\mathcal C \\leftarrow \\mathcal B}$", "$P_{\\mathcal B \\leftarrow \\mathcal C}$"]}
  answer={1}
>
Each column of $Q$ solves $[\,\mathbf b_1\ \mathbf b_2\,]\,\mathbf y = \mathbf c_j$, so it is $[\mathbf c_j]_{\mathcal B}$. Those are the columns of $P_{\mathcal B \leftarrow \mathcal C}$. For the bases in Problem 5 that is $\begin{bmatrix} 2/5 & 1 \\ 1/5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the inverse of the matrix you found.
</Choice>

## Similar matrices

A change of basis also rewrites the matrix of a map, and the reason to do it is that the right basis can make that matrix far simpler.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$, and go back to $\green{\mathbf b_1} = (2, 1)$ and $\red{\mathbf b_2} = (-1, 2)$. Find $[T]_{\mathcal B}$, the matrix of $T$ from $\mathcal B$-coordinates to $\mathcal B$-coordinates.

<VectorAnswer answer={[2, 1, 0, -1]} columns={2} />

<Solution>
Day 21's recipe says column $j$ is $[T(\mathbf b_j)]_{\mathcal B}$, so we apply $A$ to each basis vector and then find its $\mathcal B$-address.

<Steps>
<Step>
Apply $A$ to $\green{\mathbf b_1}$: $\;A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 + 2 \\ 2 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$. That is $2\green{\mathbf b_1}$, so column 1 is $(2, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Apply $A$ to $\red{\mathbf b_2}$: $\;A\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 + 4 \\ -1 + 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step>
Find weights with $a(2, 1) + b(-1, 2) = (3, -1)$. That means $2a - b = 3$ and $a + 2b = -1$. Doubling the first equation and adding the second gives $5a = 5$, so $a = 1$ and $b = -1$. Column 2 is $(1, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Stand the columns up: $\;[T]_{\mathcal B} = \teal{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}}$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The standard matrix $A$ has no zeros at all, while $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ is triangular. The zero in its corner says that $T$ sends the line through $\mathbf b_1$ to itself.
</Solution>
</Problem>

There is a way to get the same matrix with no hand-solving. To apply $T$ to a vector known only by $[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$, first convert to standard coordinates with $P = [\,\mathbf b_1\ \mathbf b_2\,]$, then apply $A$, then convert back with $P^{-1}$.

<Figure src="similarity" alt="A square diagram. From [x]_B an arrow labelled P goes up to x, an arrow labelled A goes right to Ax, and an arrow labelled P inverse goes down to [Ax]_B. The bottom arrow from [x]_B to [Ax]_B is labelled [T]_B. Below it, [T]_B = P⁻¹AP, checked with numbers: one fifth of (2, 1; −1, 2) times (1, 2; 1, 0) times (2, −1; 1, 2) equals (2, 1; 0, −1)." wide />

<Definition term="Similar matrices">
Square matrices $A$ and $C$ are **similar** if $C = P^{-1}AP$ for some invertible $P$. If $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ and $\mathcal B$ is a basis of $\mathbb R^n$ with $P = [\,\mathbf b_1\ \cdots\ \mathbf b_n\,]$, then

$$
[T]_{\mathcal B} = P^{-1}AP.
$$

The matrices similar to $A$ are exactly the matrices of $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ in the different bases of $\mathbb R^n$.
</Definition>

<Warning>
The order is $P^{-1}AP$, not $PAP^{-1}$, because the first matrix to act on $[\mathbf x]_{\mathcal B}$ must be $P$. Checking column 1 confirms it: $P^{-1}AP\,\mathbf e_1 = P^{-1}A\mathbf b_1 = [A\mathbf b_1]_{\mathcal B}$.
</Warning>

Day 30 shows that similar matrices share their eigenvalues, trace and determinant, since those numbers belong to the map rather than to the basis.

<SimilarityHunt matrix={[[1, 2], [1, 0]]} b1={[2, 1]} b2={[-1, 2]} target={[1, 1]} start={[1, 1]} />

Since $A\mathbf b_1 = 2\mathbf b_1$, the vector $\mathbf b_1$ is what Day 28 calls an eigenvector, and Day 32 uses a whole basis of eigenvectors to make the matrix of a map diagonal. Tomorrow looks for the inputs a map sends to zero, and a matrix of the map turns that search into a null space computation.

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf b_1 = (1, 1)$ and $\mathbf b_2 = (1, 2)$. Find $[T]_{\mathcal B}$ for $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ by row reducing $[\,P \mid AP\,]$, and enter it row by row. (Modeled on Lay §5.4 #17(b) and its Numerical Note.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[4, -1, 0, 4]} columns={2} />

<Hint>
Row reducing $[\,P \mid AP\,]$ to $[\,I \mid C\,]$ solves $PC = AP$, so $C = P^{-1}AP$ without ever computing $P^{-1}$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
First $AP = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 & 7 \end{array}\right] \\
&\sim \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 4 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 4 \end{array}\right] \\
&\sim \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 4 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 4 \end{array}\right],
\end{aligned}
$$

using $R_2 - R_1$ and then $R_1 - R_2$. So $[T]_{\mathcal B} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Check: $A\mathbf b_2 = (3, 7) = -\mathbf b_1 + 4\mathbf b_2$.
</Answer>
</Check>

Column $j$ of $[I]_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$ is $[I(\mathbf b_j)]_{\mathcal C} = [\mathbf b_j]_{\mathcal C}$, which is column $j$ of $P_{\mathcal C \leftarrow \mathcal B}$. When both bases are $\mathcal B$, column $j$ is $[\mathbf b_j]_{\mathcal B} = \mathbf e_j$, so the matrix is $I_n$. A change-of-coordinates matrix is the matrix of a map that does nothing, written with a different basis on each side. (Modeled on Lay §5.4 #28–29.)
</Choice>

<Check>
Suppose $A$ is invertible and $C = P^{-1}AP$. Show that $C$ is invertible and that $C^{-1}$ is similar to $A^{-1}$. (Modeled on Lay §5.4 #19.)

<Hint>
A product of invertible matrices is invertible, and $(XYZ)^{-1} = Z^{-1}Y^{-1}X^{-1}$.
</Hint>

<Answer>
$C$ is a product of the three invertible matrices $P^{-1}$, $A$ and $P$, so it is invertible, and reversing the order of the inverses gives

$$
C^{-1} = (P^{-1}AP)^{-1} = P^{-1}A^{-1}(P^{-1})^{-1} = P^{-1}A^{-1}P.
$$

That is the definition of $C^{-1}$ being similar to $A^{-1}$, with the same $P$. In basis language, if $C$ is the matrix of $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ in the basis of columns of $P$, then $C^{-1}$ is the matrix of the inverse map in that same basis.
</Answer>
</Check>
