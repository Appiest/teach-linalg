Since Day 1 you have added and scaled arrows in $\mathbb{R}^n$. Span, linear combinations and linear maps never used anything else, so they should work for any objects that add and scale by the same rules. Polynomials, functions and matrices are three such objects. Today's question is what exactly counts as a vector, and it comes now because the next unit builds on the answer. A vector space is the name for any set where those two operations behave the way they do for arrows, and every theorem you prove from the rules then holds for all of these sets at once. Days 11 to 19 rebuild subspaces, span, independence, basis and dimension on this definition.

## The definition

<Definition term="Vector space">
A **vector space** is a nonempty set $V$ of objects, called **vectors**, together with an addition and a multiplication by real scalars. The ten rules below must hold for all $\mathbf u, \mathbf v, \mathbf w$ in $V$ and all scalars $c, d$.

**Closure**

- (1) The sum $\mathbf u + \mathbf v$ is in $V$.
- (6) The scalar multiple $c\,\mathbf u$ is in $V$.

**A zero and negatives**

- (4) There is a **zero vector** $\mathbf 0$ in $V$ with $\mathbf u + \mathbf 0 = \mathbf u$.
- (5) Each $\mathbf u$ has a **negative** $-\mathbf u$ in $V$ with $\mathbf u + (-\mathbf u) = \mathbf 0$.

**Ordinary algebra**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&(2) & \mathbf u + \mathbf v &= \mathbf v + \mathbf u \\
&(3) & (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) + \mathbf w &= \mathbf u + (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) \\
&(7) & c(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) &= c\,\mathbf u + c\,\mathbf v \\
&(8) & (c + d)\mathbf u &= c\,\mathbf u + d\,\mathbf u \\
&(9) & c(d\,\mathbf u) &= (cd)\,\mathbf u \\
&(10) & 1\mathbf u &= \mathbf u
\end{aligned}
$$
</Definition>

When the operations are built from ordinary arithmetic on numbers, rules 2, 3 and 7 to 10 hold automatically, because real numbers already obey them. The rules that need checking are usually closure and the existence of a zero and negatives.

## Polynomials

The set $\mathbb{P}_2$ holds every polynomial $a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2$ with real coefficients and degree at most 2. You add two polynomials by adding their values at every $t$, which is the same as adding matching coefficients. You scale one by multiplying every coefficient. On Days 12 to 15 these coefficient lists turn questions about polynomials into row reduction in $\mathbb R^3$.

## The zero vector and negatives

Every vector space has a zero vector and negatives, and you can always find them by scaling. For any $\mathbf u$ in $V$,

$$
0\,\mathbf u = \mathbf 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad (-1)\,\mathbf u = -\mathbf u.
$$

The first fact follows from the rules alone. Rule 8 splits $0\,\mathbf u$ into two copies of itself, and adding its negative to both sides leaves $\mathbf 0$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0\,\mathbf u &= (0 + 0)\,\mathbf u = 0\,\mathbf u + 0\,\mathbf u && \text{rule 8} \\
0\,\mathbf u + (-0\,\mathbf u) &= 0\,\mathbf u + \big[0\,\mathbf u + (-0\,\mathbf u)\big] && \text{rule 3} \\
\mathbf 0 &= 0\,\mathbf u + \mathbf 0 && \text{rule 5} \\
\mathbf 0 &= 0\,\mathbf u && \text{rule 4}
\end{aligned}
$$

So the zero vector of $\mathbb{P}_2$ is the zero polynomial $0 + 0t + 0t^2$, and the negative of $\yellow{\mathbf p}$ is $-1 - 2t + t^2$. The zero vector of a function space is the function that equals 0 at every $t$. A function such as $f(t) = t$ that is zero at only some points is not the zero vector.

## Functions and matrices

The real-valued functions on an interval also form a vector space, with $(\mathbf f + \mathbf g)(t) = \mathbf f(t) + \mathbf g(t)$ and $(c\,\mathbf f)(t) = c\,\mathbf f(t)$. Think of each whole function as a single point of that space. Then $\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf f$, $\mathbf g$ and $\mathbf f + \mathbf g$ sit at the corners of a parallelogram, exactly like $\mathbf 0$, $\vec v$, $\vec w$ and $\vec v + \vec w$ on Day 1.

<Figure src="functions-as-points" alt="Left: the graphs of f(t) = 1 + sin 2t in yellow, g(t) = 2 + 0.5t in blue and their sum in teal for t from 0 to 4. Right: four dots labelled 0, f, g and f + g joined by a dashed parallelogram." wide />

The $2 \times 2$ matrices $M_{2\times 2}$ form a vector space under entry-by-entry addition and scaling. Its zero vector is the zero matrix, and $-A = (-1)A$.

$$
\yellow{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}} + \blue{\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}} = \teal{\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -1 \\ -1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}}
$$

Only addition and scalar multiplication matter here. Matrix multiplication plays no part in $M_{2\times 2}$ as a vector space.

## A set that fails

To show a set is not a vector space, find one rule that breaks and give specific numbers that break it. A general feeling that "it doesn't seem closed" is not enough.

Let $W$ be the points $(x, y)$ with $xy \geq 0$, which are the first and third quadrants. Scaling can never leave $W$, because $(cx)(cy) = c^2 xy \geq 0$. Addition can leave it, and the widget asks you to find a pair of vectors that shows how.

<ClosureHunt u={[2, 1]} v={[1, 3]} />

<Figure src="closure-fails" alt="The first and third quadrants shaded. The yellow vector u = (2, 1) and the blue vector v = (−1, −3) both lie in the shaded region, but their teal sum (1, −2), ringed in orange, lands in the unshaded fourth quadrant." />

Here $\yellow{\mathbf u} = (2, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf v} = (-1, -3)$ are in $W$, since $2 \cdot 1 = 2$ and $(-1)(-3) = 3$. Their sum $\teal{(1, -2)}$ has $1 \cdot (-2) = -2 < 0$, so rule 1 fails and $W$ is not a vector space.

A set can also sit inside a vector space and be a vector space itself, like a line through the origin in $\mathbb R^2$. Tomorrow's lesson calls such a set a subspace and tests for one with three quick checks instead of all ten rules.

<Check>
On the set $\mathbb{R}^2$, keep the usual addition but define a new scaling by $c \odot (x, y) = (cx, 0)$. Compute $1 \odot (3, 4)$, then name the rule that fails.

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 0]} />

<Answer>
$1 \odot (3, 4) = (1 \cdot 3, 0) = (3, 0)$, which is not $(3, 4)$. Rule 10 requires $1\mathbf u = \mathbf u$, so rule 10 fails and this is not a vector space. Closure still holds, since every result is in $\mathbb{R}^2$, which shows that closure alone is not enough.
</Answer>

<Check>
Let $V$ be the positive real numbers with "addition" $x \oplus y = xy$ and "scaling" $c \odot x = x^c$. Find the zero vector of $V$ and the negative of $4$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, 0.25]} labels={["zero vector", "negative of 4"]} />

<Answer>
The zero vector must satisfy $x \oplus \mathbf 0 = x$, which means $x \cdot \mathbf 0 = x$, so $\mathbf 0 = 1$. The negative of $4$ must satisfy $4 \oplus y = 1$, so $y = \tfrac14$. Both agree with the facts above, because $0 \odot 4 = 4^0 = 1$ and $(-1) \odot 4 = 4^{-1} = \tfrac14$.
</Answer>
