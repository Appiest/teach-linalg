On Day 32 a matrix with enough eigenvectors factored as $A = PDP^{-1}$, and yesterday that factorization turned out to be the map written in a basis of its eigenvectors. Those eigenvectors could point in any directions at all, and some matrices do not have enough of them. Today asks which matrices always have a full set of eigenvectors at right angles. The answer is the matrices that equal their own transpose. Such a matrix $A$ stretches the plane along two perpendicular lines and the unit circle becomes an ellipse whose axes are exactly those eigenvectors. The course's last lessons depend on this, because Day 39's quadratic forms and Day 40's matrix $A^TA$ are both symmetric.

## Symmetric matrices

<Definition term="Symmetric matrix">
A matrix $A$ is **symmetric** when $A^T = A$.
</Definition>

A symmetric matrix has to be square, because $A^T$ has the shape of $A$ turned sideways. Transposing mirrors every entry across the main diagonal, so $A^T = A$ says that entry $(i, j)$ equals entry $(j, i)$. The diagonal can hold anything, and the other entries come in matching pairs:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 7 & \glow{2} \\ \glow{2} & 4 \end{bmatrix} \text{ is symmetric}, \qquad
\begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{ is not}.
$$

## Perpendicular eigenvectors

This lesson needs one tool from Day 35 early. The **dot product** of two vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ multiplies matching entries and adds them, and it can be written as a matrix product:

$$
\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \mathbf u^T\mathbf v = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \cdots + u_nv_n .
$$

Two vectors are **orthogonal**, which means perpendicular, when $\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0$. For example, $\yellow{(2, 1)} \cdot \blue{(-1, 2)} = -2 + 2 = 0$. The length of $\mathbf v$ is $\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v}$, and dividing $\mathbf v$ by its length **normalizes** it into a **unit vector** of length 1. Day 35 explains why a zero dot product means a right angle.

<Definition term="Theorem">
If $A$ is symmetric, then any two eigenvectors from different eigenspaces are orthogonal.
</Definition>

Suppose $A\mathbf v_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf v_1$ and $A\mathbf v_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf v_2$ with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$. Write the dot product as $\mathbf u^T\mathbf v$ and use $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$ from Day 6. The step where $A^T$ becomes $A$ is the only place symmetry enters:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_1\,\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2 = (A\mathbf v_1)^T\mathbf v_2 &= \mathbf v_1^T\glow{A^T}\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf v_1^T\glow{A}\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf v_1^T(\lambda_2\mathbf v_2) = \lambda_2\,\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2 \\
(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2 &= 0 .
\end{aligned}
$$

Since $\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 \ne 0$, the dot product $\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2$ must be $0$.

The matrix $B = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is not symmetric, and it has eigenvalues $5$ and $3$ with eigenvectors $(1, -1)$ and $(1, -2)$. Their dot product is $1 + 2 = 3$, so they are not perpendicular. $B$ still turns the circle into an ellipse, but the ellipse's axes point in directions that are not eigenvectors at all.
</Pair>

## Orthogonal diagonalization

A square matrix $P$ is an **orthogonal matrix** when its columns are orthonormal, which means they are unit vectors and each pair is orthogonal. Entry $(i, j)$ of $P^TP$ is the dot product of column $i$ with column $j$, so orthonormal columns give $P^TP = I$ and therefore $P^{-1} = P^T$. Undoing $P$ then costs a transpose instead of a row reduction. For a symmetric matrix, the normalized eigenvectors from different eigenvalues make exactly such a $P$.

<Definition term="The spectral theorem">
An $n \times n$ symmetric matrix $A$ has $n$ real eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity. The eigenspace for each eigenvalue has dimension equal to that multiplicity, eigenspaces for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and $A$ is **orthogonally diagonalizable**:

$$
A = PDP^T, \qquad P^TP = I, \quad D \text{ diagonal}.
$$

Conversely, every matrix of the form $PDP^T$ is symmetric, because $(PDP^T)^T = PD^TP^T = PDP^T$.
</Definition>

<Pair>
<Figure src="three-moves" alt="Four small planes in a loop. Top left: the unit circle with short yellow and blue eigenvectors at a right angle, tilted. An arrow labelled P transpose leads to the top right, where the same circle has its eigenvectors turned onto the x and y axes. An arrow labelled D leads down to an upright ellipse stretched 8 along x and 3 along y. An arrow labelled P leads left to the final tilted ellipse, the same picture A makes." />

Reading $PDP^T\mathbf x$ from right to left, $P^T$ turns the eigenvectors onto the axes, $D$ stretches along the axes, and $P$ turns them back. The circle ends up as the same ellipse that $A$ makes in one step.
</Pair>

To orthogonally diagonalize a symmetric matrix, the goal is a $P$ with orthonormal columns. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors as on Day 32, then divide each eigenvector by its length. Here is a worked example with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A - \lambda I) &= \lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 14 = (\lambda - 7)(\lambda - 2) \\
\lambda = 7:\ \yellow{\mathbf v_1} &= \yellow{(2, 1)}, \qquad \lambda = 2:\ \blue{\mathbf v_2} = \blue{(-1, 2)} \\
\mathbf u_1 &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1), \qquad \mathbf u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(-1, 2) \\
P &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A = PDP^T
\end{aligned}
$$

When an eigenvalue repeats, row reduction hands back a basis for its eigenspace that need not be orthogonal. Any basis of the eigenspace is allowed, so replace the second vector by $\mathbf z_2 = \mathbf v_2 - \frac{\mathbf v_2 \cdot \mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_1}\mathbf v_1$, which is still in the eigenspace and is orthogonal to $\mathbf v_1$. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, the eigenvalue $1$ has $\mathbf v_1 = (-1, 1, 0)$ and $\mathbf v_2 = (-1, 0, 1)$ with $\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2 = 1$, so $\mathbf z_2 = (-1, 0, 1) - \tfrac12(-1, 1, 0) = (-\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)$. The eigenvector $(1, 1, 1)$ for $\lambda = 4$ is already orthogonal to both by the theorem. Days 36 and 37 explain where the formula for $\mathbf z_2$ comes from.

## Spectral decomposition

The factorization describes $A$ as three moves, but it can also show what $A$ does along each eigenline separately. Multiplying out $PDP^T$ column by row splits $A$ into one piece per eigenvalue:

$$
A = \lambda_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T + \lambda_2\mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^T + \cdots + \lambda_n\mathbf u_n\mathbf u_n^T .
$$

This sum is the **spectral decomposition** of $A$. Each $\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^T$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, and $(\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^T)\mathbf x = (\mathbf u_j \cdot \mathbf x)\,\mathbf u_j$ is the shadow of $\mathbf x$ on the line through $\mathbf u_j$. Day 36 calls these shadows orthogonal projections and builds the same kind of matrix for any subspace. So $A\mathbf x$ is built by taking the shadow of $\mathbf x$ on each eigenline and stretching it by that eigenvalue. Note the order: $\mathbf u^T\mathbf u$ is the number $1$, while $\mathbf u\mathbf u^T$ is a matrix.

A yellow arrow 8 times the first shadow and a blue arrow 3 times the second meet tip to tail at Ax = (2, 4), drawn in teal, which lies on the ellipse." />

In the video, $\mathbf x = (0, 1)$ has shadows on both eigenlines of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Stretching them by $8$ and $3$ and adding tip to tail lands on $A\mathbf x = (2, 4)$, the second column of $A$.
</Pair>

For the worked example above,

$$
\begin{aligned}
7\,\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T &= \tfrac75\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
2\,\mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^T = \tfrac25\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \\
7\,\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T + 2\,\mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^T &= \tfrac15\begin{bmatrix} 28 + 2 & 14 - 4 \\ 14 - 4 & 7 + 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting both eigenvalues to $1$ gives $\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T + \mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^T = PP^T = I$, since the two shadows of $\mathbf x$ add back to $\mathbf x$.

Orthogonally diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Enter the eigenvalue for the eigenvector $(1, 1)$ and the eigenvalue for $(-1, 1)$. (Modeled on Lay §7.1, Exercises 13–22.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[4, -2]} labels={["λ for (1, 1)", "λ for (−1, 1)"]} />

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
A\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = 4\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
A\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = -2\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
P &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The eigenvectors have dot product $-1 + 1 = 0$, as the theorem promises, and each has length $\sqrt2$, which is why $P$ carries the factor $1/\sqrt2$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
The symmetric matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalue $6$ with eigenvector $(1, -2)$. Find the top row of the piece $6\,\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T$ in its spectral decomposition. (Modeled on Lay §7.1, Exercises 33–34.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[1.2, -2.4]} labels={["entry (1, 1)", "entry (1, 2)"]} />

<Answer>
Normalize first. The length of $(1, -2)$ is $\sqrt5$, so $\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(1, -2)$ and

$$
6\,\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T = \tfrac65\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6/5 & -12/5 \\ -12/5 & 24/5 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

The other eigenvalue is $1$ with $\mathbf u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1)$, and $1\cdot\tfrac15\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ adds to the piece above to give back $A$. Forgetting to normalize would give $6\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, which is five times too big.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
The matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 5 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalue $6$ with eigenspace basis $\mathbf v_1 = (1, -1, 0)$ and $\mathbf v_2 = (1, 0, -1)$. These two are not orthogonal. Find $\mathbf z_2$. (Modeled on Lay §7.1, Exercises 23–24.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[0.5, 0.5, -1]} />

<Answer>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf v_2 \cdot \mathbf v_1 &= 1, \qquad \mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_1 = 2 \\
\mathbf z_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac12\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

Check: $\mathbf z_2 \cdot \mathbf v_1 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + 0 = 0$, and $A\mathbf z_2 = (3, 3, -6) = 6\,\mathbf z_2$, so $\mathbf z_2$ is still an eigenvector for $6$. The third eigenvalue is $3$ with eigenvector $(1, 1, 1)$, which is orthogonal to both without any correction.
</Answer>
</Check>
