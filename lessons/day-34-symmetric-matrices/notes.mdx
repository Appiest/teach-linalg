On Day 32 a matrix with enough eigenvectors factored as $A = PDP^{-1}$, and yesterday that factorization turned out to be the map written in a basis of its eigenvectors. Those eigenvectors could point in any directions at all, and some matrices do not have enough of them. Today asks which matrices always have a full set of eigenvectors at right angles. The answer is the matrices that equal their own transpose. Such a matrix stretches the plane along two perpendicular lines, and the unit circle becomes an ellipse whose axes are exactly those eigenvectors. The course's last lessons depend on this, because Day 39's quadratic forms and Day 40's matrix $A^TA$ are both symmetric.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The box after each walkthrough names the idea you just used.

## Symmetric matrices

<Problem>
Write down the transpose of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and of $B = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Which one equals its own transpose?

<Solution>
The transpose turns each row into a column (Day 6). The first row of $A$ is $(7, 2)$, so the first column of $A^T$ is $(7, 2)$. The second row $(2, 4)$ becomes the second column. That gives $A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, which is $A$ again.

For $B$ the rows $(7, 2)$ and $(-4, 1)$ become columns, so $B^T = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -4 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The off-diagonal entries traded places, and since $2 \ne -4$, $B^T \ne B$.

Transposing mirrors every entry across the main diagonal. A matrix survives the mirror exactly when the entries on opposite sides of the diagonal already match.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="Symmetric matrix">
A matrix $A$ is **symmetric** when $A^T = A$. It has to be square, because $A^T$ has the shape of $A$ turned sideways. The diagonal entries can be anything, and every other entry $a_{ij}$ equals its mirror partner $a_{ji}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 7 & \glow{2} \\ \glow{2} & 4 \end{bmatrix} \text{ is symmetric}, \qquad
\begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \text{ is not}.
$$
</Definition>

<Choice
  question="Which matrix is symmetric?"
  options={["$\\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3 \\\\ 3 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 5 & 1 \\\\ 5 & 2 & 4 \\\\ 1 & 4 & -1 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\end{bmatrix}$"]}
  answer={1}
>
In the middle matrix, the pairs $(5, 5)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(4, 4)$ match across the diagonal. The first matrix has $-3$ opposite $3$, and the last is not square, so it cannot equal its transpose.
</Choice>

## Perpendicular eigenvectors

This section needs one tool from Day 35 early. The **dot product** of two vectors multiplies matching entries and adds them. For $\mathbf u = (2, 1)$ and $\mathbf v = (-1, 2)$ it is $2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 2 = 0$. In general,

$$
\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \mathbf u^T\mathbf v = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \cdots + u_nv_n .
$$

Two vectors are **orthogonal**, which means perpendicular, when $\mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = 0$. Day 35 explains why a zero dot product means a right angle.

<Problem>
Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Then compute the dot product of the two eigenvectors.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Find the eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial: $(6 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 \cdot 2 = \lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 18 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 14 = (\lambda - 7)(\lambda - 2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $\lambda = 7$, $A - 7I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$. Its first row says $-x_1 + 2x_2 = 0$, so $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
For $\lambda = 2$, $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Its second row says $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (-1, 2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Take the dot product: $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} \cdot \blue{\mathbf v_2} = 2 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 2 = 0$. The eigenvectors are perpendicular.
</Step>
</Steps>

Nothing in the steps asked for a right angle. It came out of the symmetry of $A$, as the theorem below explains.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Symmetry forces a right angle">
The dot product can be moved across a symmetric matrix: $(A\mathbf x) \cdot \mathbf y = \mathbf x \cdot (A\mathbf y)$. Apply that to two eigenvectors with different eigenvalues and the only way both sides can agree is a zero dot product.
</Concept>

<Definition term="Theorem">
If $A$ is symmetric, then any two eigenvectors from different eigenspaces are orthogonal.
</Definition>

Suppose $A\mathbf v_1 = \lambda_1\mathbf v_1$ and $A\mathbf v_2 = \lambda_2\mathbf v_2$ with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$. The proof computes $(A\mathbf v_1) \cdot \mathbf v_2$ in two ways.

<Steps>
<Step>
Use the eigenvalue of $\mathbf v_1$: $(A\mathbf v_1) \cdot \mathbf v_2 = (\lambda_1\mathbf v_1) \cdot \mathbf v_2 = \lambda_1\,\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Write the dot product as a matrix product and use $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$ from Day 6: $(A\mathbf v_1)^T\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf v_1^T\glow{A^T}\mathbf v_2$. Symmetry turns $A^T$ into $A$, which is the only place it enters.
</Step>
<Step>
Now use the eigenvalue of $\mathbf v_2$: $\mathbf v_1^TA\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf v_1^T(\lambda_2\mathbf v_2) = \lambda_2\,\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2$.
</Step>
<Step>
The two answers are equal, so $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2 = 0$. Since $\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 \ne 0$, the dot product $\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2$ must be $0$.
</Step>
</Steps>

## Orthogonal diagonalization

Perpendicular eigenvectors make a better $P$ than Day 32's, because such a $P$ is undone by a transpose.

<Problem>
Divide $\yellow{\mathbf v_1} = (2, 1)$ and $\blue{\mathbf v_2} = (-1, 2)$ by their lengths, where the length of $\mathbf v$ is $\|\mathbf v\| = \sqrt{\mathbf v \cdot \mathbf v}$. Put the results into the columns of $P$ and compute $P^TP$.

<Solution>
Both vectors have length $\sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt5$, so the new vectors are $\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1)$ and $\mathbf u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(-1, 2)$. Dividing by the length is called **normalizing**, and the results are **unit vectors** of length 1.

Entry $(i, j)$ of $P^TP$ is row $i$ of $P^T$ times column $j$ of $P$, which is $\mathbf u_i \cdot \mathbf u_j$:

$$
P^TP = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf u_1 \cdot \mathbf u_1 & \mathbf u_1 \cdot \mathbf u_2 \\ \mathbf u_2 \cdot \mathbf u_1 & \mathbf u_2 \cdot \mathbf u_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \tfrac{4 + 1}{5} & \tfrac{-2 + 2}{5} \\ \tfrac{-2 + 2}{5} & \tfrac{1 + 4}{5} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

So $P^TP = I$, and $P^{-1} = P^T$. Undoing $P$ costs a transpose instead of a row reduction.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Orthonormal columns invert by transposing">
A square matrix $P$ is an **orthogonal matrix** when its columns are orthonormal, which means they are unit vectors and each pair is orthogonal. Then $P^TP = I$ and $P^{-1} = P^T$. For a symmetric matrix, normalized eigenvectors from different eigenvalues make exactly such a $P$, so $A = PDP^{-1}$ becomes $A = PDP^T$.
</Concept>

<Definition term="The spectral theorem">
An $n \times n$ symmetric matrix $A$ has $n$ real eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity. The eigenspace for each eigenvalue has dimension equal to that multiplicity, eigenspaces for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and $A$ is **orthogonally diagonalizable**:

$$
A = PDP^T,
$$

where $P^TP = I$ and $D$ is diagonal.

Conversely, every matrix of the form $PDP^T$ is symmetric, because $(PDP^T)^T = PD^TP^T = PDP^T$.
</Definition>

In Day 31's language, a symmetric matrix is never defective. For the problem's matrix, $P = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ give $A = PDP^T$.

An arrow labelled D leads down to an upright ellipse stretched 8 along x and 3 along y. An arrow labelled P leads left to the final tilted ellipse, the same picture A makes." />

Reading $PDP^T\mathbf x$ from right to left, $P^T$ turns the eigenvectors onto the axes, $D$ stretches along the axes, and $P$ turns them back. The circle ends up as the same ellipse that $A$ makes in one step.
</Pair>

When an eigenvalue repeats, row reduction hands back a basis for its eigenspace, and that basis need not be orthogonal. Any basis of the eigenspace is allowed, so we can fix it.

<Problem>
The symmetric matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has the eigenvalue $1$ twice, with eigenspace basis $\mathbf v_1 = (-1, 1, 0)$ and $\mathbf v_2 = (-1, 0, 1)$. Check whether they are orthogonal. If not, replace $\mathbf v_2$ with $\mathbf z_2 = \mathbf v_2 - \dfrac{\mathbf v_2 \cdot \mathbf v_1}{\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_1}\mathbf v_1$.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
Test the pair: $\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_2 = 1 + 0 + 0 = 1$. That is not zero, so these two need fixing.
</Step>
<Step>
Compute the pieces of the formula: $\mathbf v_2 \cdot \mathbf v_1 = 1$ and $\mathbf v_1 \cdot \mathbf v_1 = 1 + 1 + 0 = 2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Subtract half of $\mathbf v_1$: $\mathbf z_2 = (-1, 0, 1) - \tfrac12(-1, 1, 0) = (-\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Check both properties. $\mathbf z_2 \cdot \mathbf v_1 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + 0 = 0$. And $\mathbf z_2$ is a combination of two eigenvectors for $1$, so it is still in that eigenspace: $A\mathbf z_2 = (-1 - \tfrac12 + 1,\ -\tfrac12 - 1 + 1,\ -\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + 2) = (-\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1)$.
</Step>
</Steps>

The eigenvector $(1, 1, 1)$ for $\lambda = 4$ is already orthogonal to both, by the theorem. Days 36 and 37 explain where the formula for $\mathbf z_2$ comes from.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Warning>
Only mix vectors that share an eigenvalue. Combining an eigenvector for $1$ with one for $4$ gives a vector that is not an eigenvector at all. Eigenvectors from different eigenspaces are already orthogonal, so they never need fixing. Also remember to normalize at the end, or $P^TP \ne I$.
</Warning>

## Spectral decomposition

The factorization describes $A$ as three moves. It can also show what $A$ does along each eigenline separately.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, with unit eigenvectors $\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1)$ for $8$ and $\mathbf u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(-1, 2)$ for $3$. For $\mathbf x = (0, 1)$, compute the shadows $(\mathbf u_1 \cdot \mathbf x)\,\mathbf u_1$ and $(\mathbf u_2 \cdot \mathbf x)\,\mathbf u_2$. Stretch them by $8$ and $3$ and add.

<Solution>
<Steps>
<Step>
First shadow: $\mathbf u_1 \cdot \mathbf x = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(0 + 1) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}$, so the shadow is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5} \cdot \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1) = \tfrac15(2, 1) = (0.4, 0.2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Second shadow: $\mathbf u_2 \cdot \mathbf x = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(0 + 2) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt5}$, so the shadow is $\tfrac25(-1, 2) = (-0.4, 0.8)$. The two shadows add back to $(0, 1) = \mathbf x$.
</Step>
<Step>
Stretch: $8(0.4, 0.2) = (3.2, 1.6)$ and $3(-0.4, 0.8) = (-1.2, 2.4)$.
</Step>
<Step>
Add: $(3.2 - 1.2,\ 1.6 + 2.4) = (2, 4)$. That is $A\mathbf x$, the second column of $A$.
</Step>
</Steps>
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="shadow-pieces" alt="The unit circle with x = (0, 1) drawn as a short purple-gray arrow. Dashed drops run from its tip to its shadows on the yellow and blue eigenlines. A yellow arrow 8 times the first shadow and a blue arrow 3 times the second meet tip to tail at Ax = (2, 4), drawn in teal, which lies on the ellipse." />

<Concept title="One piece per eigenline">
Multiplying out $PDP^T$ column by row splits $A$ into one piece per eigenvalue, the **spectral decomposition**:

$$
A = \lambda_1\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T + \cdots + \lambda_n\mathbf u_n\mathbf u_n^T .
$$

Each $\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^T$ is an $n \times n$ matrix, and $(\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^T)\mathbf x = (\mathbf u_j \cdot \mathbf x)\,\mathbf u_j$ is the shadow of $\mathbf x$ on the line through $\mathbf u_j$. Day 36 calls these shadows orthogonal projections.
</Concept>
</Pair>

Aim with the shadows. This matrix stretches the yellow line through $(1, 1)$ by $3$ and the blue line through $(-1, 1)$ by $1$, which makes it $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

<ShadowStretchSum direction={[1, 1]} lambdas={[3, 1]} target={[4, 5]} start={[1, 0]} />

For the matrix from the second section, the pieces add back up as promised:

$$
\begin{aligned}
7\,\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T &= \tfrac75\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \\
2\,\mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^T &= \tfrac25\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \\
\text{sum} &= \tfrac15\begin{bmatrix} 28 + 2 & 14 - 4 \\ 14 - 4 & 7 + 8 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<Warning>
Watch the order. $\mathbf u^T\mathbf u$ is the number $1$ for a unit vector, while $\mathbf u\mathbf u^T$ is a matrix. Setting every eigenvalue to $1$ gives $\mathbf u_1\mathbf u_1^T + \mathbf u_2\mathbf u_2^T = PP^T = I$, since the shadows of $\mathbf x$ add back to $\mathbf x$.
</Warning>

<Check>
Show that if $A$ is symmetric, then $(A\mathbf x) \cdot \mathbf y = \mathbf x \cdot (A\mathbf y)$ for all $\mathbf x, \mathbf y$ in $\mathbb R^n$. (Modeled on Lay §7.1 #27.)

<Hint>

<Answer>
$$
