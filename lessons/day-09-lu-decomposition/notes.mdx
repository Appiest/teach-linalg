Day 8 showed that every step of elimination is a matrix, so row reducing $A$ amounts to multiplying it by elementary matrices. Today's question is how to keep that work. In practice one matrix $A$ often meets many right-hand sides, and row reducing $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ from scratch repeats the same elimination every time $\mathbf b$ changes. An **LU factorization** writes down that elimination once, as a product $A = LU$, so each new $\mathbf b$ costs only two short substitutions.

Each section below opens with a problem. Try it before you open the walkthrough, even if you only get partway. The first two problems need nothing but ordinary equation solving.

## Triangular matrices

<Problem>
Solve this system for $y_1$, $y_2$ and $y_3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_1 &= -1 \\
2y_1 + y_2 &= -1 \\
-y_1 + 3y_2 + y_3 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

<VectorAnswer answer={[-1, 1, -4]} labels={["y₁", "y₂", "y₃"]} />

<Solution>
The top equation mentions only $y_1$, so it hands us $y_1 = -1$ with no work. That makes it the natural place to start.

<Steps>
<Step>
The top equation says $y_1 = -1$.
</Step>
<Step>
The second equation has one new unknown, $y_2$. Put in $y_1 = -1$: $2(-1) + y_2 = -1$, so $y_2 = -1 + 2 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step>
The third equation has one new unknown, $y_3$. Put in both known values: $-(-1) + 3(1) + y_3 = 0$, so $1 + 3 + y_3 = 0$ and $y_3 = -4$.
</Step>
</Steps>

Every equation added exactly one new unknown, so we never had to eliminate anything. In matrix form the system is $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$ with $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and every entry above its diagonal is zero.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Problem>
Now solve $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$ with $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ and the $\mathbf y = (-1, 1, -4)$ you just found.

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, -1, 2]} labels={["x₁", "x₂", "x₃"]} />

<Solution>
This time the zeros sit below the diagonal, so the bottom equation, $-2x_3 = -4$, is the one with a single unknown. We start there and work up.

<Steps>
<Step>
Bottom row: $-2x_3 = -4$, so $x_3 = 2$.
</Step>
<Step>
Middle row: $3x_2 + 2x_3 = 1$. With $x_3 = 2$ this is $3x_2 + 4 = 1$, so $3x_2 = -3$ and $x_2 = -1$.
</Step>
<Step>
Top row: $2x_1 + x_2 - x_3 = -1$. With the known values this is $2x_1 - 1 - 2 = -1$, so $2x_1 = 2$ and $x_1 = 1$.
</Step>
</Steps>

So $\mathbf x = (1, -1, 2)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Concept title="Triangular systems solve one unknown at a time">
A square matrix is **lower triangular** when every entry above its main diagonal is $0$, and **upper triangular** when every entry below it is $0$. A lower triangular matrix with 1s on its diagonal is **unit lower triangular**, like $L$ in Problem 1.

In $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$ the top row gives $y_1$ outright, and each row below adds one new unknown. Solving from the top down is **forward substitution**. In $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$ the bottom row holds the lone unknown, so you solve from the bottom up, which is **back substitution**.
</Concept>

Triangular matrices come back on Day 26, where the determinant of one is the product of its diagonal, and on Day 28, where its eigenvalues sit on that diagonal.

<Choice
  question="Which matrix is unit lower triangular?"
  options={["$\\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\\\ 0 & 1 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\\\ 5 & 1 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\\\ -3 & 1 \\end{bmatrix}$", "$\\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\\\ 1 & 0 \\end{bmatrix}$"]}
  answer={2}
>
It has zeros above the diagonal and 1s on the diagonal. The first option is upper triangular, the second has a $2$ on its diagonal, and the last has $0$s on its diagonal.
</Choice>

## Two triangular solves

The two problems above were really one problem. Multiplying out $LU$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & 5 & 0 \\ -2 & 8 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, and Problems 1 and 2 solved $LU\mathbf x = (-1, -1, 0)$ in two easy stages. The next problem shows why that works, with $2 \times 2$ matrices you can draw.

<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = LU$ with $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \pink{2} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solve $A\mathbf x = (2, 3)$ by first finding $\mathbf y$ with $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$ and then $\mathbf x$ with $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, -1]} labels={["x₁", "x₂"]} />

<Solution>
We want $A\mathbf x = L(U\mathbf x) = \mathbf b$. If we name the inside part $\mathbf y = U\mathbf x$, the equation becomes $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$, which is triangular.

Forward substitution in $L\mathbf y = (2, 3)$: the top row says $y_1 = 2$. The bottom row says $2y_1 + y_2 = 3$, so $y_2 = 3 - 4 = -1$. So $\blue{\mathbf y} = (2, -1)$.

Back substitution in $U\mathbf x = (2, -1)$: the bottom row says $x_2 = -1$. The top row says $x_1 + x_2 = 2$, so $x_1 = 3$. So $\yellow{\mathbf x} = (3, -1)$.

Check with $A$ itself: $3(1, 2) - 1(1, 3) = (3 - 1, 6 - 3) = (2, 3)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Pair>
<Figure src="shears" alt="Three small grids. On the left, a yellow arrow x = (1, 1) sits on a square grid. An arrow labelled U leads to the middle grid, sheared sideways, where x has become the blue arrow y = (2, 1). An arrow labelled L leads to the right grid, sheared vertically as well, where y has become the teal arrow b = (2, 5)." />

<Concept title="Solve Ly = b, then Ux = y">
If $A = LU$, then $A\mathbf x = L(U\mathbf x)$. Multiplying by $A$ applies $U$ first and then $L$, so $\yellow{\mathbf x} \to \blue{\mathbf y} \to \teal{\mathbf b}$. Solving runs the picture backward:

$$
L\blue{\mathbf y} = \teal{\mathbf b}, \qquad U\yellow{\mathbf x} = \blue{\mathbf y}.
$$
</Concept>
</Pair>

## Building L from elimination

So far someone handed us $L$ and $U$. The next problem finds them from $A$, and the answer is the elimination you already know from Day 3.

<Problem>
Row reduce $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ to an echelon form $U$ with one row replacement. Put the multiple you subtracted below the diagonal of $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ \ell & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and check that $LU = A$. Enter $\ell$ and the bottom-right entry of $U$.

<VectorAnswer answer={[2, 3]} labels={["ℓ", "u₂₂"]} />

<Solution>
To reach echelon form we need a $0$ below the pivot $2$. Row 2 starts with $4$, which is $2$ times the pivot, so the natural step is $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$. Row 2 becomes $(4, 5) - 2(2, 1) = (0, 3)$:

$$
U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \ell = 2.
$$

Now check. Row 1 of $LU$ is $1\cdot(2, 1) + 0\cdot(0, 3) = (2, 1)$. Row 2 is $2\cdot(2, 1) + 1\cdot(0, 3) = (4, 5)$. So $LU = A$.

Multiplying by $L$ adds $2$ times row 1 back into row 2, which undoes the elimination step. That is why the multiplier goes into $L$ with the same sign that we subtracted.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<PivotFlattener a={[[2, 1], [4, 5]]} />

For a $3 \times 3$ matrix there are three multipliers, one for each entry below the diagonal. The rule for finding them quickly is to look at each pivot column of the current matrix and divide it by its pivot.

<Problem>
Find $L$ and $U$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 & 5 & 0 \\ -2 & 8 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$. (A fresh problem modeled on Lay §2.5, Example 2.)

<Solution>
We eliminate column by column, and each step records one multiplier.

<Steps>
<Step>
The first pivot column is $(\glow{2}, \glow{4}, \glow{-2})$. Dividing by the pivot $2$ gives $(1, \pink{2}, \pink{-1})$, so the steps are $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - \pink{2}R_1$ and $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - (\pink{-1})R_1$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 4 - 4 & 5 - 2 & 0 + 2 \\ -2 + 2 & 8 + 1 & 5 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & \glow{3} & 2 \\ 0 & \glow{9} & 4 \end{bmatrix}
$$
</Step>
<Step>
The second pivot column, from the pivot down, is $(\glow{3}, \glow{9})$. Dividing by $3$ gives $(1, \pink{3})$, so the step is $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \pink{3}R_2$, and row 3 becomes $(0, 9, 4) - 3(0, 3, 2) = (0, 0, -2)$.
</Step>
<Step>
The result is echelon form, so it is $U$. The divided pivot columns are the columns of $L$:

$$
L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \pink{2} & 1 & 0 \\ \pink{-1} & \pink{3} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
</Step>
</Steps>

These are the $L$ and $U$ of Problems 1 and 2.
</Solution>
</Problem>

<Definition term="LU factorization">
Suppose the $m \times n$ matrix $A$ can be reduced to an echelon form $U$ using only row replacements that add a multiple of a row to a row below it. Then

$$
A = LU,
$$

where $L$ is an $m \times m$ unit lower triangular matrix and $U$ is that $m \times n$ echelon form. The entry of $L$ in row $i$, column $j$ is the **multiplier** $\ell_{ij}$ used in the step $R_i \leftarrow R_i - \ell_{ij} R_j$.
</Definition>

The multipliers land in $L$ because elimination is a product of elementary matrices from Day 8. In Problem 5, $E_3E_2E_1A = U$, so $A = (E_3E_2E_1)^{-1}U$, and $L = (E_3E_2E_1)^{-1}$ is the matrix that undoes each subtraction by adding the same multiple back.

<LUBuilder matrix={[[2, 1, 3], [-4, 1, -7], [6, 0, 12]]} />

<Figure src="factor" alt="The equation A = L U with 3 by 3 matrices. A is 2, 1, −1; 4, 5, 0; −2, 8, 5. L has 1s on its diagonal, zeros above, and the pink multipliers 2, −1 and 3 below. U is 2, 1, −1; 0, 3, 2; 0, 0, −2, with its pivots 2, 3 and −2 boxed in orange." wide />

<Warning>
The step subtracts $\ell$ times a row, and $L$ stores $+\ell$. A step written as $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + R_1$ subtracts $-1$ times row 1, so the entry of $L$ is $-1$. Getting this sign backwards is the most common LU mistake, and multiplying $LU$ back out catches it.
</Warning>

## Fewer pivots than rows

<Problem>
Find $L$ and $U$ for the $3 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

<Solution>
The same method applies, and only the shapes change.

The first pivot column is $(2, 4, -2)$, and dividing by $2$ gives $(1, 2, -1)$. The steps $R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 + R_1$ give rows $(2, 1)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(0, 3)$.

The second pivot column, from the pivot down, is $(1, 3)$, so the step is $R_3 - 3R_2$, which turns row 3 into $(0, 0)$.

There are no more pivots, but $L$ must be $3 \times 3$. Its third column comes from the identity:

$$
L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

Check row 3 of $LU$: $-1(2, 1) + 3(0, 1) + 1(0, 0) = (-2, 2)$.
</Solution>
</Problem>

$L$ is always square with as many rows as $A$, while $U$ has the shape of $A$. If a zero lands in a pivot position with a nonzero entry below it, a row swap is needed and plain LU does not exist. Software then uses a **permuted LU** factorization, which is the same idea with the rows reordered.

## Why this saves work

A **flop** is one addition, subtraction, multiplication or division. For a dense $n \times n$ matrix with $n$ in the dozens or more, Lay's numerical notes give these counts.

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\textsf{Task} & \textsf{Approximate flops} \\[8pt]
\textsf{Factor } A = LU \textsf{ or row reduce } [\,A \;\; \mathbf b\,] & \tfrac23 n^3 \\[6pt]
\textsf{Compute } A^{-1} & 2n^3 \\[6pt]
\textsf{Solve } L\mathbf y = \mathbf b \textsf{ and } U\mathbf x = \mathbf y & 2n^2
\end{array}
$$

<Problem>
A $1000 \times 1000$ system must be solved for $100$ different right-hand sides. Estimate the flops for row reducing each time and for factoring once and then doing two triangular solves per right side. About how many times less work is LU?

<Solution>
Here $n^3 = 10^9$ and $n^2 = 10^6$.

<Steps>
<Step>
Row reducing each time costs $\tfrac23 n^3$ per right side, so $100 \cdot \tfrac23 \cdot 10^9 \approx 6.7 \times 10^{10}$ flops.
</Step>
<Step>
LU costs one factorization plus $100$ pairs of solves: $\tfrac23 \cdot 10^9 + 100 \cdot 2 \cdot 10^6 \approx 6.7 \times 10^8 + 2 \times 10^8 = 8.7 \times 10^8$ flops.
</Step>
<Step>
The ratio is $6.7 \times 10^{10} / 8.7 \times 10^8 \approx 77$, so LU does about 77 times less work.
</Step>
</Steps>

Computing $A^{-1}$ first would cost about $2 \cdot 10^9 + 100 \cdot 2 \cdot 10^6 = 2.2 \times 10^9$ flops, and $A^{-1}\mathbf b$ is also less accurate because of roundoff.
</Solution>
</Problem>

The purple-gray line for row reducing each time climbs steadily to about 67 at k = 100. The teal line for LU once stays nearly flat along the bottom and ends at about 0.87." wide />

<Check>
Use the factorization to solve $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ for $\mathbf b = (0, -2, -2)$. (Modeled on Lay §2.5 #1.)

$$

<VectorAnswer answer={[2, -1, 1]} labels={["x₁", "x₂", "x₃"]} />

<Hint>
Solve $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$ from the top down first. Then solve $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$ from the bottom up.
</Hint>

<Answer>
Forward substitution in $L\mathbf y = \mathbf b$ gives $y_1 = 0$, then $y_2 = -2 + y_1 = -2$, then $y_3 = -2 - 3y_1 - 2y_2 = 2$.

Back substitution in $U\mathbf x = \mathbf y$ gives the rest.

$$
\begin{aligned}
2x_3 &= 2 &&\Rightarrow\ x_3 = 1 \\
x_2 - x_3 &= -2 &&\Rightarrow\ x_2 = -1 \\
x_1 + 2x_2 &= 0 &&\Rightarrow\ x_1 = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

So $\mathbf x = (2, -1, 1)$.
</Answer>
</Check>
