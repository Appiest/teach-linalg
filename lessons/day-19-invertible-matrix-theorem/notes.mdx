Day 7 found that an inverse exists exactly when a matrix does not flatten the plane. Days 13 to 18 then gave you a pile of new words for square matrices: independent columns, spanning columns, pivots, $\operatorname{Nul}A$, $\operatorname{Col}A$, rank and nullity. Today shows that for a square matrix all of those words describe one single fact, so checking any one of them tells you all the others.

## The theorem

<Definition term="The invertible matrix theorem">
Let $A$ be a **square** $n \times n$ matrix. The following statements are **equivalent**, which means that for a given $A$ they are either all true or all false.

Five statements say that nothing is crushed:

- (d) $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$ has only the solution $\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$
- (e) the columns of $A$ are linearly independent
- (f) $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ is one-to-one
- (q) $\operatorname{Nul}A = \{\mathbf 0\}$
- (r) $\dim \operatorname{Nul}A = 0$

Five say that every target is reached:

- (g) $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has a solution for every $\mathbf b$ in $\mathbb R^n$
- (h) the columns of $A$ span $\mathbb R^n$
- (i) $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ maps $\mathbb R^n$ onto $\mathbb R^n$
- (n) $\operatorname{Col}A = \mathbb R^n$
- (o) $\dim \operatorname{Col}A = n$

The rest say that an undo exists, that the pivots are full, or that the columns are a basis:

- (a) $A$ is invertible
- (j) $CA = I$ for some $n\times n$ matrix $C$
- (k) $AD = I$ for some $n\times n$ matrix $D$
- (l) $A^T$ is invertible
- (b) $A$ is row equivalent to $I_n$
- (c) $A$ has $n$ pivot positions
- (p) $\operatorname{rank}A = n$
- (m) the columns of $A$ form a basis of $\mathbb R^n$
</Definition>

The letters follow Lay §2.3 and §4.6, so you can cite a statement by its letter. The theorem says nothing about a matrix that is not square.

Each arrow in the figure is one short argument. For example, if $CA = I$ and $A\mathbf x = \mathbf 0$, then $\mathbf x = I\mathbf x = CA\mathbf x = C\mathbf 0 = \mathbf 0$, which gives (j) ⇒ (d). Following the arrows you can get from any statement to any other, and that is exactly what "equivalent" means.

## Why square matters

The two big blocks sound different. "Nothing is crushed" is about inputs, while "every target is reached" is about outputs. The rank theorem joins them: an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ columns, so

$$
\operatorname{rank}A + \dim\operatorname{Nul}A = n.
$$

If nothing is crushed, then $\dim\operatorname{Nul}A = 0$ and the rank is $n$. The column space then has dimension $n$ inside $\mathbb R^n$, so it is all of $\mathbb R^n$ and every target is reached. The step "dimension $n$ inside $\mathbb R^n$" needs the outputs to live in $\mathbb R^n$, which is why the matrix must be square. A $3\times 2$ matrix with independent columns crushes nothing, yet its column space is only a plane in $\mathbb R^3$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} &= \teal{\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} &&\operatorname{rank}A = 2 \\[4pt]
M\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} &= \glow{\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}} &&\operatorname{rank}M = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

$M$ crushes the direction $(2, -1)$, so it has nullity $1$ and rank $1$. Its outputs fill only a line, and $\mathbf b = (3, 1)$ is not on it.
</Pair>

## Deciding fast

You never need $A^{-1}$ itself to know that it exists. Pick whichever statement is cheapest to check.

- Two columns that are multiples of each other, or a zero column, make the columns dependent, so (e) fails.
- A triangular matrix with no zero on its diagonal has $n$ pivots, so (c) holds. A zero on the diagonal leaves fewer than $n$ pivots.
- Otherwise, row reduce until you can count the pivots.

<Pair>
<Figure src="decide" alt="Top: B with rows (2, 1, 0), (1, 3, 1), (0, 1, 2) is row equivalent to the echelon form with rows (2, 1, 0), (0, 5, 2), (0, 0, 8); orange boxes mark the pivots 2, 5 and 8. Bottom: C with rows (1, 2, −1), (2, 4, 0), (3, 6, 1) with its first two columns boxed in orange, beside the relation c2 = 2 c1." />

$$
\begin{aligned}
B &= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\
&\sim \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} && R_2 \leftarrow 2R_2 - R_1 \\
&\sim \begin{bmatrix} \glow{2} & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \glow{5} & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & \glow{8} \end{bmatrix} && R_3 \leftarrow 5R_3 - R_2
\end{aligned}
$$

Three pivots means (c) holds, so $B$ is invertible. The theorem now hands you the rest: $\operatorname{Nul}B = \{\mathbf 0\}$, the columns of $B$ are a basis of $\mathbb R^3$, and every $B\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has exactly one solution. The matrix $C$ needs no work at all, because $\mathbf c_2 = 2\mathbf c_1$ breaks (e).
</Pair>

The same logic runs backwards. If one statement fails, they all fail: a singular $A$ has dependent columns, a nonzero vector in $\operatorname{Nul}A$, rank below $n$, and some $\mathbf b$ for which $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has no solution.

## Invertible transformations

<Definition term="Invertible linear transformation">
A linear transformation $T:\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^n$ is **invertible** if some function $S$ satisfies $S(T(\mathbf x)) = \mathbf x$ and $T(S(\mathbf x)) = \mathbf x$ for every $\mathbf x$. If $A$ is the standard matrix of $T$, then $T$ is invertible exactly when $A$ is, and then $T^{-1}(\mathbf x) = A^{-1}\mathbf x$ (Lay §2.3, Theorem 9).
</Definition>

Because (f) and (i) are on the list, a linear map from $\mathbb R^n$ to itself that is one-to-one is automatically onto, and the other way round. For $2\times 2$ matrices you also know from Day 7 that $A$ is invertible exactly when $ad - bc \ne 0$. The determinant unit will extend "$\det A \ne 0$" to every $n\times n$ matrix and add it to the list.

<Check>
For which value of $k$ is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & k \end{bmatrix}$ singular? (Modeled on Lay §2.3, Exercises 1 to 8.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3]} labels={["k"]} />

<Answer>
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & k \end{bmatrix}
\sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & k - 4 \end{bmatrix}
\sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & k - 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

The matrix has three pivots exactly when $k - 3 \ne 0$. At $k = 3$ the last row is zero, so (c) fails and the matrix is singular. For every other $k$ it is invertible.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$ is singular. Prove it by finding a nonzero vector in its null space whose first entry is $1$. (Modeled on Lay §2.3, Exercise 22.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[1, -2, 1]} labels={["x₁", "x₂", "x₃"]} />

<Answer>
Row reduction gives $x_1 = x_3$ and $x_2 = -2x_3$, so $\mathbf x = (1, -2, 1)$ works:

$$
\begin{aligned}
1 - 4 + 3 &= 0 \\
4 - 10 + 6 &= 0 \\
7 - 16 + 9 &= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Statement (d) fails, so every statement fails. In particular the rank is $2$, the columns are dependent, and some $\mathbf b$ in $\mathbb R^3$ is not an output.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
Let $T(x_1, x_2) = (3x_1 + 5x_2,\ x_1 + 2x_2)$. Explain why $T$ is invertible, then find $T^{-1}(4, 1)$. (Modeled on Lay §2.3, Exercise 33.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, -1]} labels={["first entry", "second entry"]} />

<Answer>
The standard matrix is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ with $ad - bc = 6 - 5 = 1 \ne 0$, so $A$ is invertible and so is $T$. Then

$$
T^{-1}(4, 1) = A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -5 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

As a check, $T(3, -1) = (9 - 5,\ 3 - 2) = (4, 1)$.
</Answer>
</Check>
