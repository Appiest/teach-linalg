<Definition term="Invertible matrix">
An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is **invertible** if there is an $n \times n$ matrix $C$ with

$$
CA = I \quad\text{and}\quad AC = I.
$$

Then $C$ is the **inverse** of $A$, written $A^{-1}$. A matrix with no inverse is called **singular**, and an invertible matrix is also called **nonsingular**.
</Definition>

Both products matter. $A^{-1}A = I$ says that undoing after doing changes nothing, and $AA^{-1} = I$ says the same thing in the other order. A matrix can have only one inverse, because if $B$ were a second one, then $B = B(AC) = (BA)C = C$.

The columns of $A^{-1}$ have a direct meaning. Column 1 of $A^{-1}$ is $A^{-1}\hat\imath$, which is the point that $A$ sends onto $\hat\imath$. Column 2 is the point that $A$ sends onto $\hat\jmath$.

## The formula for a 2 × 2 inverse

<Definition term="Theorem 4">
Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$. If $ad - bc \neq 0$, then $A$ is invertible and

$$
A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}.
$$

If $ad - bc = 0$, then $A$ is not invertible. The number $ad - bc$ is the **determinant** of $A$, written $\det A$.
</Definition>

<Figure src="formula" alt="The general formula for the inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix. Below it, the formula applied to A = [2 1; 1 1]: the diagonal entries have swapped places, the off-diagonal entries have flipped sign, and the fraction 1 over 2·1 − 1·1 gives A inverse = [1 −1; −1 2] in pink." wide />

The recipe has three moves: swap $a$ and $d$, flip the signs of $b$ and $c$, then divide by $ad - bc$. It works because multiplying out gives

$$
\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & ad - bc \end{bmatrix},
$$

and dividing by $ad - bc$ turns that into $I$. For example, with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det A &= 4 \cdot 2 - 3 \cdot 2 = 2 \\
A^{-1} &= \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -3/2 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \\
AA^{-1} &= \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 3 & -6 + 6 \\ 2 - 2 & -3 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The last line is the ten-second check. Multiplying your answer by $A$ catches a swapped sign or a forgotten division right away.

## Solving with the inverse

<Definition term="Theorem 5">
If $A$ is an invertible $n \times n$ matrix, then for each $\mathbf b$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ the equation $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ has exactly one solution, $\mathbf x = A^{-1}\mathbf b$.
</Definition>

The vector $A^{-1}\mathbf b$ works because $A(A^{-1}\mathbf b) = I\mathbf b = \mathbf b$. It is the only solution, because any $\mathbf u$ with $A\mathbf u = \mathbf b$ gives $\mathbf u = A^{-1}A\mathbf u = A^{-1}\mathbf b$ after multiplying both sides by $A^{-1}$ on the left. In the video, $\teal{\mathbf b} = (4, 3)$ rides the grid back through $A^{-1}$:

$$
\yellow{\mathbf x} = \pink{A^{-1}}\teal{\mathbf b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 - 3 \\ -4 + 6 \end{bmatrix} = \yellow{\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}}
$$

Matrices never get divided. Because $A^{-1}B$ and $BA^{-1}$ are usually different, every step has to say which side the inverse multiplies on.

## The inverse of a product

If you shear the plane with $S$ and then turn it with $R$, the combined motion is $RS$. To undo it you have to undo the turn first, just as you take off your shoes before your socks.

<Definition term="Theorem 6">
If $A$ and $B$ are invertible $n \times n$ matrices, then

(a) $A^{-1}$ is invertible and $(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$,

(b) $AB$ is invertible and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$,

(c) $A^T$ is invertible and $(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$.
</Definition>

Each part is proved by checking the definition. For (b), the middle of the product collapses first:

$$
(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I,
$$

and $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = I$ collapses the same way. The guess $A^{-1}B^{-1}$ fails because $B$ and $A^{-1}$ never sit next to each other, so nothing cancels.

## Singular matrices

When $ad - bc = 0$, the two columns of $A$ lie on one line through the origin, and $A$ squashes the whole plane onto that line. Then many different points land on the same spot, so no matrix could send each one back to where it came from.

<Figure src="singular" alt="The matrix M = [1 2; 2 4] sends the three yellow points (−1, 1), (1, 0) and (3, −1), which lie on a dotted line, to the single point (1, 2). Orange dotted arrows run from each point to (1, 2), which sits on the teal line y = 2x where the whole plane lands." />

For $M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, the determinant is $1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2 = 0$. The points $(1, 0)$, $(-1, 1)$ and $(3, -1)$ all land on $(1, 2)$, because they differ by multiples of $(-2, 1)$ and $M(-2, 1) = \mathbf 0$. In general, $\mathbf x = (-b, a)$ gives $A\mathbf x = (0, ad - bc)$, so when the determinant is zero some nonzero vector lands on $\mathbf 0$, and Theorem 5's uniqueness fails.
