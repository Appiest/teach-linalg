Yesterday you multiplied a matrix by one vector at a time. Today asks what a matrix does to every vector at once. Read that way, the product is a rule: feed in any $\yellow{\mathbf x}$ and the matrix hands back $A\yellow{\mathbf x}$, and doing that to every point at once moves the whole plane. The rest of the course leans on this picture of a matrix as a motion, starting tomorrow, when Day 6 runs two motions in a row.

<Definition term="Transformation">
A **transformation** $T$ from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ is a rule that assigns to each vector $\mathbf x$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ a vector $T(\mathbf x)$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$. We write $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. The set $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the **domain**, $\mathbb{R}^m$ is the **codomain**, and $T(\mathbf x)$ is the **image** of $\mathbf x$. The set of all images is the **range** of $T$.

When $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix, $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ is a **matrix transformation**. Its domain is $\mathbb{R}^n$ because $\mathbf x$ needs one entry per column of $A$, and its codomain is $\mathbb{R}^m$ because each column has $m$ entries.
</Definition>

The range matters because it says which equations $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ have a solution. On Day 16 the range of $\mathbf x \mapsto A\mathbf x$ gets its own name, the column space of $A$.

This matrix is a **shear**. It slides each point sideways by its height, so the base line stays still and the grid leans over. The grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced, and the origin stays put.

## The standard matrix

Often you know a move in words, such as a quarter turn, and want its matrix. The goal of this section is a recipe for that matrix that needs no algebra.

Every $\mathbf x$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is $x_1\green{\mathbf e_1} + x_2\red{\mathbf e_2}$, where $\green{\mathbf e_1} = (1, 0)$ and $\red{\mathbf e_2} = (0, 1)$. So once you know where $\green{\mathbf e_1}$ and $\red{\mathbf e_2}$ land, you know where everything lands. Those two landing spots are the columns of the matrix.

<Definition term="Theorem 10">
Let $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be a linear transformation. Then there is exactly one matrix $A$ with $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ for every $\mathbf x$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, namely

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} T(\mathbf e_1) & \cdots & T(\mathbf e_n) \end{bmatrix},
$$

where $\mathbf e_j$ is column $j$ of the $n \times n$ identity matrix. This $A$ is the **standard matrix** of $T$.
</Definition>

<Pair>
<Figure src="quarter-turn" alt="A quarter turn counterclockwise. The green arrow e1 now points up to (0, 1) and the red arrow e2 points left to (−1, 0). Their coordinates form the columns of A = [[0, −1], [1, 0]]." />

A counterclockwise quarter turn sends $\green{\mathbf e_1}$ up to $(0, 1)$ and $\red{\mathbf e_2}$ left to $(-1, 0)$. Writing those as columns gives the matrix with no formula to memorize:

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} \green{0} & \red{-1} \\ \green{1} & \red{0} \end{bmatrix}
$$
</Pair>

## Linearity

The recipe above works only because matrix maps obey two rules, and naming those rules tells you which moves a matrix can make. On Day 20 the same two rules become the definition of a linear map between any vector spaces, so differentiation counts too.

<Definition term="Linear transformation">
A transformation $T$ is **linear** when, for all vectors $\mathbf u, \mathbf v$ in its domain and every scalar $c$,

(i) $T(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = T(\mathbf u) + T(\mathbf v)$, and

(ii) $T(c\,\mathbf u) = c\,T(\mathbf u)$.

Every matrix transformation is linear, because $A(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = A\mathbf u + A\mathbf v$ and $A(c\mathbf u) = cA\mathbf u$ from Day 4.
</Definition>

Two facts follow right away. Taking $c = 0$ in (ii) gives $T(\mathbf 0) = \mathbf 0$, so a linear map never moves the origin. Using (i) and then (ii) gives $T(c\,\mathbf u + d\,\mathbf v) = c\,T(\mathbf u) + d\,T(\mathbf v)$. A slide such as $T(\mathbf x) = \mathbf x + (1, 1)$ keeps grid lines straight, but it moves the origin, so it is not linear and no matrix can do it.

<Pair>
<Figure src="walk" alt="The grid moved by the matrix [[2, −1], [1, 1]]. Two green steps T(e1) = (2, 1) and one red step T(e2) = (−1, 1) are placed tip to tail, and the yellow arrow T(x) runs from the origin to their end at (3, 3)." />

The vector $\yellow{\mathbf x} = (2, 1)$ is a walk of two $\green{\mathbf e_1}$ steps and one $\red{\mathbf e_2}$ step. The map moves each step, and the walk follows:

$$
T(\yellow{\mathbf x}) = 2\,\green{T(\mathbf e_1)} + 1\,\red{T(\mathbf e_2)}
$$
</Pair>

Suppose $T$ is linear with $\green{T(\mathbf e_1)} = (1, 2)$ and $\red{T(\mathbf e_2)} = (-3, 1)$. You can find $T(4, -1)$ from those two images alone:

$$
\begin{aligned}
T\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} &= 4\,\green{T(\mathbf e_1)} - 1\,\red{T(\mathbf e_2)} \\
&= 4\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

and the standard matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} \green{1} & \red{-3} \\ \green{2} & \red{1} \end{bmatrix}$.

Going backwards asks which $\mathbf x$ has a given image $\mathbf b$, so it is Day 4's equation $A\mathbf x = \mathbf b$ seen as a picture. Because the moved grid is made of copies of $\green{T(\mathbf e_1)}$ and $\red{T(\mathbf e_2)}$, you can count how many of each step reach $\mathbf b$.

<PreimageHunt matrix={[[2, -1], [1, 1]]} target={[1, 5]} success="Found it. With $\mathbf x = (2, 3)$ you take two green steps and three red steps, and $2(2, 1) + 3(-1, 1) = (1, 5)$." />

Row reduction gives the same answer without a picture. The point $\mathbf b$ is in the range exactly when $\begin{bmatrix} A & \mathbf b \end{bmatrix}$ is consistent:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & -3 & -9 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

## Practice

<Check>
A $3 \times 5$ matrix $A$ defines $T(\mathbf x) = A\mathbf x$ as a map $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$. Find $n$ and $m$. (Modeled on Lay §1.8 #21.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[5, 3]} labels={["n", "m"]} />

<Answer>
The product $A\mathbf x$ needs one entry of $\mathbf x$ for each of the $5$ columns, so $n = 5$. Each column has $3$ entries, so $A\mathbf x$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and $m = 3$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
$T$ first performs a vertical shear that sends $\mathbf e_1$ to $\mathbf e_1 + 2\mathbf e_2$ and keeps $\mathbf e_2$ fixed, then reflects through the line $x_2 = x_1$. Find $T(1, 1)$. (Modeled on Lay §1.9 #9.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, 1]} />

<Answer>
Track each basis vector through both steps. The reflection swaps the two coordinates.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\green{\mathbf e_1} &\to (1, 2) \to (2, 1), \\
\red{\mathbf e_2} &\to (0, 1) \to (1, 0),
\end{aligned}
$$

so $A = \begin{bmatrix} \green{2} & \red{1} \\ \green{1} & \red{0} \end{bmatrix}$ and $T(1, 1) = (2, 1) + (1, 0) = (3, 1)$.
</Answer>
</Check>

<Check>
A linear map $T$ sends $\mathbf u = (1, 1)$ to $(4, 2)$ and $\mathbf v = (1, -1)$ to $(2, -4)$. Find $T(\mathbf e_1)$. (Modeled on Lay §1.8 #17.)

<VectorAnswer answer={[3, -1]} />

<Answer>
Since $\mathbf e_1 = \tfrac12(\mathbf u + \mathbf v)$, linearity gives

$$
T(\mathbf e_1) = \tfrac12\bigl(T(\mathbf u) + T(\mathbf v)\bigr) = \tfrac12\begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$

In the same way $\mathbf e_2 = \tfrac12(\mathbf u - \mathbf v)$ gives $T(\mathbf e_2) = (1, 3)$, so the standard matrix is $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.
</Answer>
</Check>
